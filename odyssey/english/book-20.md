# Odyssey Book 20

> (narrator)
1	Godlike Odysseus was lying down in the porch.
2	He spread an untanned oxhide, and above it
3	many fleeces of sheep, which the Achaeans used to sacrifice.
4	Eurynome threw a cloak on him when he had lain down.
5	There Odysseus thinking evils in his heart for the suitors
6	lay staying awake. The women from the hall
7	were going, who used to mix with the suitors before,
8	giving laughter and good cheer to one another.
9	His heart was stirred in his own chest.
10	He considered many things through the mind and through the heart,
11	whether rushing he should make death for each,
12	or still let them mix with the overbearing suitors
13	for the last and latest time, and the heart inside him was barking.
14	As a dog standing around her tender puppies
15	barks at a man she does not know and is eager to fight,
16	so his heart inside was barking, angry at the bad works.
17	Striking his chest he rebuked the heart with a word:
> Odysseus
18	Endure now, heart. You once endured another thing more doglike.
19	On the day when the Cyclops, uncheckable in force, was eating
20	my strong companions. You dared, until the device
21	led you out of the cave, thinking you would die.
> (narrator)
22	So he spoke, laying hold of the own heart in his chest.
23	The heart stayed very much in persuasion, having endured,
24	without stopping. He himself was twisting here and here.
25	As when a man at a fire of much burning
26	turns a belly here and here, full of savor and blood,
27	and he is very quickly eager for it to be roasted,
28	so he was twisting here and here, considering
29	how he would put his hands on the shameless suitors
30	being one against many. Athena came near him
31	coming down from heaven. She was like a woman in body.
32	She stood over his head and spoke a word to him:
> Athena
33	Why are you awake again, most enduring of all men?
34	This is your house, and this is your wife in the house
35	and a child, such as someone somewhere wishes a son to be.
> (narrator)
36	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
37	Yes, goddess, you have said all these things according to portion.
38	But this is what the heart in my mind is considering,
39	how I will put my hands on the shameless suitors
40	being one. They are always together inside.
41	And still I am considering this greater thing in my mind:
42	if I killed them by the will of Zeus and of you,
43	where would I escape out from under? I tell you to think of these things.
> (narrator)
44	Then the goddess, owl-eyed Athena, spoke to him:
> Athena
45	Hard man, even someone obeys a worse companion,
46	who is mortal and does not know so many plans.
47	I am a god, who guard you all the way
48	in all the labors. I will tell you openly:
49	if fifty ambushes of speaking humans
50	stood around the two of us, eager to kill us with Ares,
51	you would drive off even their cattle and strong flocks.
52	Let sleep take you. It is also a pain to guard
53	staying awake all night, and you will get out from under the evils already.
> (narrator)
54	So she spoke, and she poured sleep on his eyelids,
55	and she herself, the bright one of goddesses, reached Olympus again.
56	When sleep caught him, loosing the cares of the heart,
57	limb-loosing, the wife who knows trusted things woke.
58	She sat in the soft bed and cried.
59	When crying she had filled her own heart,
60	the bright one of women prayed first to Artemis:
> Penelope
61	Artemis, lady goddess, daughter of Zeus, I wish already
62	throwing an arrow in my chest you would take the life
63	at once now, or then a gust snatching me
64	would go carrying me along the misty ways,
65	and throw me in the outflow of backward-flowing Ocean.
66	As when gusts took up the daughters of Pandareus.
67	The gods wasted the parents, and they were left
68	orphans in the halls, and bright Aphrodite cared for them
69	with cheese and sweet honey and pleasant wine.
70	Hera gave them beyond all women
71	look and good sense, and holy Artemis gave height,
72	and Athena taught them to work famous works.
73	When bright Aphrodite went to long Olympus
74	to ask for the girls the end of a blooming marriage —
75	to Zeus who delights in the thunderbolt, for he knows well all things,
76	portion and lack of portion of mortal humans —
77	in that time the harpies snatched the girls
78	and gave them to the hated Erinyes to attend.
79	So may those who hold Olympian houses make me unseen,
80	or may well-haired Artemis hit me, so that looking on Odysseus
81	I may reach even the hated earth below,
82	and I may not gladden the thought of a worse man at all.
83	This evil is even bearable, whenever someone
84	cries by days, closely grieved in the heart,
85	and sleep holds him by nights — for it makes one forget all things,
86	good and bad, when it covers the lids around —
87	but a god has driven bad dreams on me too.
88	This night again one like him slept beside me,
89	being such as he was with the army. My heart
90	was glad, since I did not think it was a dream, but already a waking vision.
> (narrator)
91	So she spoke, and at once gold-throned Dawn came.
92	Godlike Odysseus put together the voice of her crying.
93	He then considered, and it seemed to him in his heart
94	that she already knew and was standing beside his head.
95	Gathering the cloak and the fleeces he had slept in
96	he put them down in the hall on a throne, and the oxhide
97	he set carrying it to the door, and he prayed to Zeus holding up his hands:
> Odysseus
98	Father Zeus, if you willing over the dry and the wet
99	led me to my own land, since you have done me evil too much,
100	let someone of the humans waking speak an omen-word to me
101	from inside, and from outside let another sign of Zeus appear.
> (narrator)
102	So he spoke praying. Zeus of devices heard him,
103	and at once he thundered from shining Olympus,
104	from above out of the clouds. Godlike Odysseus was glad.
105	A woman grinding sent an omen-word out of the house
106	near, where the mills sat for the shepherd of the people,
107	at which twelve women in all used to work
108	making barley-meal and wheat-meal, the marrow of men.
109	The others were sleeping, since they had ground the wheat,
110	and one had not yet stopped, and she had been made the weakest.
111	Stopping the mill she spoke a word, a sign to the lord:
> mill-woman
112	Father Zeus, who rule gods and humans,
113	you thundered greatly from the starry heaven,
114	and there is no cloud anywhere. You are showing this sign to someone.
115	Fulfill now also for miserable me the word I say.
116	May the suitors this day for the latest and last time
117	take a desirable feast in the halls of Odysseus,
118	they who have loosed my knees with heart-painful labor
119	as I make barley-meal. May they now dine for the last time.
> (narrator)
120	So she spoke, and godlike Odysseus was glad at the omen-word
121	and the thunder of Zeus. He thought he would pay the sinners.
122	The other serving-women through the beautiful house of Odysseus
123	gathering were lighting the unwearying fire on the hearth.
124	Telemachus rose from the bed, a man equal to a god,
125	putting on clothes. He set a sharp sword around his shoulder.
126	Under his shining feet he bound beautiful sandals,
127	and he took a fighting spear, pointed with sharp bronze.
128	Going he stood on the threshold and spoke to Eurycleia:
> Telemachus
129	Dear nurse, did you honor the stranger in the house
130	with bed and food, or does he lie uncared-for just so?
131	Such is my mother, even being sensible.
132	At random she honors one of speaking humans,
133	a worse one, and sending off the better she has dishonored him.
> (narrator)
134	Then careful Eurycleia spoke to him:
> Eurycleia
135	You would not now, child, blame her as without cause.
136	He drank wine sitting, as long as he himself wished,
137	and he said he was no longer hungry for bread. She asked him.
138	When he was remembering lying-down and sleep,
139	she told the serving-women to spread bedding,
140	but he, as someone wholly miserable and without portion,
141	did not wish to sleep in a bed and in blankets,
142	but on an untanned oxhide and fleeces of sheep
143	he slept in the porch. We put a cloak on him.
> (narrator)
144	So she spoke, and Telemachus went through the hall
145	holding a spear. Two swift dogs followed with him.
146	He went to go to the assembly among the well-greaved Achaeans.
147	The bright one of women again called to the serving-women,
148	Eurycleia, daughter of Ops son of Peisenor:
> Eurycleia
149	Come, some of you bustling sweep the house
150	and sprinkle, and on the well-made thrones throw
151	purple rugs. Others with sponges wipe
152	all the tables around, and clean the mixing-bowls
153	and the worked two-handled cups. Others after water
154	go to the fountain, and bring it going quickly.
155	The suitors will not be away from the hall for long,
156	but they come very early, since there is a feast for all too.
> (narrator)
157	So she spoke, and they heard her very much and obeyed.
158	Twenty went to the black-water fountain,
159	and the others were working skillfully through the house there.
160	The workers of the Achaeans came in. They then
161	split wood well and skillfully, and the women
162	came from the fountain. The swineherd came on them
163	leading down three fat hogs, which were the best among all.
164	He let them feed through the beautiful enclosures,
165	and he himself addressed Odysseus with honeyed words:
> Eumaeus
166	Stranger, do the Achaeans look at you more,
167	or do they dishonor you through the halls, as before?
> (narrator)
168	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
169	I wish, Eumaeus, the gods would pay the outrage
170	which these men having violence contrive, reckless things,
171	in another's house, and they do not have a portion of shame.
> (narrator)
172	So they spoke such things to one another,
173	and Melanthius the goatherd came near them
174	leading goats that stood out among all the herds,
175	a supper for the suitors. Two herders followed with him.
176	He tied them under the loud porch,
177	and he himself addressed Odysseus with cutting words:
> Melanthius
178	Stranger, will you still even now pain men through the house
179	begging, and will you not go out to the door?
180	I do not think the two of us will be divided at all
181	before we taste hands, since you do not according to order
182	beg. There are also other feasts of the Achaeans.
> (narrator)
183	So he spoke, and Odysseus of many devices said nothing to him,
184	but silently he moved his head, plotting evils deep.
185	A third came on them, Philoetius, leader of men,
186	leading a barren cow for the suitors and fat goats.
187	Ferrymen had brought those over, who also send
188	other humans, whoever reaches them.
189	He tied them well under the loud porch,
190	and he himself standing near asked the swineherd:
> Philoetius
191	Who is this stranger newly come, swineherd,
192	to our house? From which men does he claim to be?
193	Where is his birth and fatherland-plowland?
194	Ill-fated, he is like a king lord in body.
195	But the gods make much-wandering humans miserable,
196	whenever they spin misery even on kings.
197	He also pledged him with the right hand standing near,
198	and speaking he addressed winged words to him:
199	Be glad, father stranger. May wealth come to you even afterward.
200	But now you are held in many evils.
201	Father Zeus, no other of the gods is more destructive than you.
202	You do not pity men, when you yourself have begotten them,
203	to mix them with badness and wretched pains.
204	I sweated, as I marked him, and my eyes have tears
205	remembering Odysseus, since I think that man also
206	is wandering among humans having such rags,
207	if somewhere he still lives and sees the light of the sun.
208	If he has already died and is in the house of Hades,
209	woe then for faultless Odysseus, who set me over the cattle
210	when I was still small, in the people of the Cephallenians.
211	Now they become unspeakably many, and the birth of wide-browed cattle
212	would not otherwise grow up like grain for a man.
213	Others tell me to drive them for themselves
214	to eat. They do not care at all about the child in the halls,
215	and they do not tremble at the watch of the gods. They are already eager
216	to divide the possessions, the lord long gone.
217	This heart in my own chest
218	turns many ways. It is a very bad thing, the son being,
219	to go to the people of others going with the cattle themselves,
220	to foreign men. This is colder, staying here
221	sitting over others' cattle suffering pains.
222	I would have reached another of the over-strong kings
223	long ago, fleeing, since they are no longer bearable.
224	But I still think of that miserable man, if coming from somewhere
225	he would put a scattering of the suitor-men through the house.
> (narrator)
226	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
227	Cowherd, since you are not like a bad man or a mindless one,
228	and I myself know that good sense reaches your mind,
229	therefore I will tell you and I will swear a great oath besides.
230	Let Zeus be witness first of the gods, and the guest-table
231	and the hearth of faultless Odysseus, which I have reached,
232	that with you being here Odysseus will come home.
233	With your own eyes you will look, if you wish,
234	on the suitors being killed, who lord it here.
> (narrator)
235	Then the man, overseer of cattle, spoke to him:
> Philoetius
236	I wish the son of Cronus would finish this word, stranger.
237	You would know what my power is and how the hands follow.
> (narrator)
238	So also Eumaeus prayed to all the gods
239	that many-minded Odysseus come home to his own house.
240	So they spoke such things to one another,
241	and the suitors were fitting death and fate
242	for Telemachus. A bird came to them on the left,
243	a high-flying eagle, and it held a trembling dove.
244	Amphinomus spoke to them and said among them:
> Amphinomus
245	Friends, this plan at least will not run together for us,
246	the killing of Telemachus. Let us remember the feast.
> (narrator)
247	So Amphinomus spoke, and the word pleased them.
248	Coming into the house of godlike Odysseus
249	they put their cloaks down on the couches and thrones,
250	and they sacrificed great sheep and fat goats,
251	and they sacrificed fat hogs and a herd cow.
252	Roasting the innards they distributed them, and wine
253	they mixed in the mixing-bowls. The swineherd distributed the cups.
254	Philoetius, leader of men, distributed bread to them
255	in beautiful baskets, and Melantheus poured the wine.
256	They reached out their hands to the food lying ready.
257	Telemachus sat Odysseus, turning profits,
258	inside the well-based hall, beside the stone threshold,
259	setting an unseemly chair and a small table.
260	He set portions of the innards beside him, and he poured wine
261	in a gold cup, and he spoke a word to him:
> Telemachus
262	Sit here now among the men drinking wine.
263	I myself will hold off from you the cuttings and the hands
264	of all the suitors, since this house is not of the people
265	for you, but of Odysseus, and that man got it for me.
266	You, suitors, hold back the heart from rebuke
267	and from hands, so that no strife and quarrel rises.
> (narrator)
268	So he spoke, and they all growing with their teeth in their lips
269	wondered at Telemachus, who spoke daringly.
270	Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke among them:
> Antinous
271	Let us accept the word, Achaeans, even being hard,
272	of Telemachus. He speaks threatening us very much.
273	Zeus son of Cronus did not let us. Otherwise we would already
274	have stopped him in the halls, even being a clear speaker.
> (narrator)
275	So Antinous spoke. He did not care about the words.
276	Heralds through the town were leading a holy hecatomb of the gods.
277	The long-haired Achaeans were gathering
278	under the shadowy grove of far-shooting Apollo.
279	When they had roasted the upper meats and drawn them,
280	dividing the portions they feasted a very famous feast.
281	The men working set a portion beside Odysseus
282	equal, as they themselves were allotted. So
283	Telemachus charged, the own son of godlike Odysseus.
284	Athena would not let the proud suitors at all
285	hold back from heart-painful outrage, so that still more
286	pain would go down into the heart of Odysseus son of Laertes.
287	There was a man among the suitors who knew lawless things.
288	Ctesippus was his name, and he lived in a house in Same.
289	Trusting in unearthly possessions
290	he used to woo the wife of Odysseus long gone.
291	He then spoke among the overbearing suitors:
> Ctesippus
292	Hear me, proud suitors, so I may say something.
293	The stranger has had a portion for a long time, as is fitting,
294	an equal one. It is not beautiful or just to short
295	the strangers of Telemachus, whoever reaches this house.
296	Come, I too will give him a guest-gift, so that he himself
297	may give a prize to the bath-pourer or to some other
298	of the slaves who are through the house of godlike Odysseus.
> (narrator)
299	So speaking he threw a cow's foot with a thick hand,
300	taking it lying from the basket. Odysseus avoided it
301	gently turning his head aside, and he smiled in his heart
302	a very sardonic smile of that kind. It hit the well-built wall.
303	Telemachus rebuked Ctesippus with a word:
> Telemachus
304	Ctesippus, this was very much more profitable to your heart.
305	You did not hit the stranger. He himself avoided the missile.
306	I would have hit you in the middle with the sharp spear,
307	and instead of a marriage your father would have worked a tomb
308	here. Therefore let no one show unseemliness in my house.
309	I already think and I know each thing,
310	the good and the worse. Before I was still a child.
311	All the same we have endured these things looking on,
312	sheep being slaughtered and wine being drunk
313	and bread. It is hard for one to hold back many.
314	Come, no longer do evils to me being hostile.
315	If you already want to kill me myself with bronze,
316	I would wish even that, and it would be much more profitable
317	to die than always to look on these unseemly works,
318	strangers being driven and serving-women
319	being dragged unseemly through the beautiful house.
> (narrator)
320	So he spoke, and they all became still in silence.
321	Late then Agelaus, son of Damastor, spoke among them:
> Agelaus
322	Friends, no one would be angry laying hold with opposing words
323	after a thing said justly.
324	Do not drive the stranger or any other
325	of the slaves who are through the house of godlike Odysseus.
326	I would say a gentle word to Telemachus and the mother
327	if it pleased the heart of both of them.
328	As long as the heart in your chest hoped
329	that many-minded Odysseus would come home to his own house,
330	there was no blame to wait and to hold
331	the suitors through the house, since this was more profitable,
332	if Odysseus came home and reached the house turning back.
333	Now this is already clear, that he is no longer of homecoming.
334	Come, sitting beside your mother tell her these things,
335	to marry whoever is the best man and gives the most,
336	so that you glad may manage all the father's things,
337	eating and drinking, and she may care for another's house.
> (narrator)
338	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
339	Not by Zeus, Agelaus, and the pains of my father,
340	who somewhere far from Ithaca has either wasted or is wandering,
341	I do not put off the mother's marriage, but I tell her
342	to marry whoever she wishes, and I give countless gifts besides.
343	I am ashamed to drive her unwilling from the hall
344	with a forced word. May a god not finish this.
> (narrator)
345	So Telemachus spoke. Pallas Athena
346	raised unquenchable laughter for the suitors, and she drove their thought off course.
347	They were already laughing with jaws that were others',
348	and they were eating meat mixed with blood. Their eyes
349	were filling with tears, and the heart thought it was a lament.
350	Godlike Theoclymenus also spoke among them:
> Theoclymenus
351	Ah wretched, what is this evil you are suffering? With night
352	your heads and faces and the knees below are wrapped.
353	A wail has blazed, and the cheeks have tears,
354	and the walls and the beautiful cross-beams are sprinkled with blood.
355	The porch is full of images, and the courtyard also is full,
356	hurrying to Erebus under the gloom. The sun
357	has been lost out of heaven, and a bad mist has run over.
> (narrator)
358	So he spoke, and they all laughed sweetly at him.
359	Eurymachus, child of Polybus, began to speak among them:
> Eurymachus
360	The stranger newly come from elsewhere is out of his mind.
361	Young men, send him out of the house to the door quickly
362	to go to the assembly, since he makes these things like night.
> (narrator)
363	Then godlike Theoclymenus spoke to him:
> Theoclymenus
364	Eurymachus, I do not tell you to grant me escorts.
365	I have eyes and ears and both feet
366	and a mind made in the chest, not unseemly at all.
367	With those I will go out to the door, since I think an evil
368	is coming for you, which no one would escape out from under or avoid
369	of the suitors, who through the house of godlike Odysseus
370	having violence toward men contrive reckless things.
> (narrator)
371	So speaking he went out of the well-lived house
372	and reached Peiraeus, who received him readily.
373	All the suitors looking at one another
374	were stirring Telemachus, laughing at the strangers.
375	So one of the overbearing young men would say:
> a suitor
376	Telemachus, no one else is a worse host of strangers than you.
377	What a man this is you have, a felt-out wanderer,
378	needing bread and wine, not at all practiced
379	in works or in force, but just a burden of the plowland.
380	This other one stood up to prophesy.
381	If you would obey me in something, it would be much more profitable.
382	Throwing the strangers in a many-benched ship
383	let us send them to the Sicels, from where they would earn you something worthy.
> (narrator)
384	So the suitors spoke. He did not care about the words,
385	but silently he was looking at his father, always waiting
386	for when he would put his hands on the shameless suitors.
387	The daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope,
388	setting a very beautiful chair down opposite
389	was hearing the word of each of the men in the halls.
390	They laughing were making a supper
391	sweet and suited to the spirit, since they had sacrificed very many things.
392	No other supper could somehow be more graceless
393	than the one a goddess and a strong man were soon
394	going to set. They were the earlier ones contriving unseemly things.
