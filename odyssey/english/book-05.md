# The Odyssey: Book V

> (narrator)
1	Dawn rose from her bed beside lordly Tithonus,
2	so she might bring light to the immortals and to mortal men.
3	And the gods were sitting down to their session, and among them
4	Zeus who thunders on high, whose force is the greatest.
5	And among them Athena was telling the many griefs of Odysseus,
6	remembering him: for it was a care to her that he was in the house of the nymph:
> Athena
7	Father Zeus and you other blessed gods who are forever,
8	let no scepter-bearing king ever again be eager to be kind and gentle,
9	or know what is right in his mind,
10	but let him always be harsh and do unjust things —
11	since no one remembers godlike Odysseus
12	of the people he was lord over, and he was gentle as a father.
13	But he lies on an island suffering strong pains
14	in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who by compulsion
15	holds him. And he cannot reach his fatherland.
16	For he has no oared ships and companions
17	who might send him over the wide back of the sea.
18	Now again they are eager to kill his beloved son
19	as he comes home. He has gone after news of his father
20	to holy Pylos and to bright Lacedaemon.
> (narrator)
21	And answering her cloud-gatherer Zeus said:
> Zeus
22	My child, what kind of word has escaped the barrier of your teeth?
23	Did you not yourself plan this very purpose,
24	that Odysseus coming home would pay those men back?
25	And send Telemachus skillfully on his way — for you can —
26	so that he may come quite unharmed to his fatherland,
27	and the suitors in their ship come back the way they came.
> (narrator)
28	So he spoke, and he addressed Hermes, his dear son, face to face:
> Zeus
29	Hermes, since you are in other things too a messenger,
30	tell the fair-braided nymph the unerring will,
31	the homecoming of Odysseus of the enduring heart, that he may go
32	neither with escort of gods nor of mortal men;
33	but on a many-bound raft, suffering pains,
34	on the twentieth day he may reach fertile Scheria,
35	the land of the Phaeacians, who are near to the gods,
36	who will honor him in their hearts as a god,
37	and will send him in a ship to his dear fatherland,
38	giving him bronze and gold in plenty and clothing,
39	much, as much as Odysseus would never have taken from Troy,
40	even if he had come unharmed, having drawn his share of the plunder.
41	For so it is his portion to see his dear ones and to reach
42	his high-roofed house and his own fatherland.
> (narrator)
43	So he spoke, and the messenger, the slayer of Argus, did not disobey.
44	At once then he bound under his feet the fine sandals,
45	immortal, golden, that carried him over the wet
46	and over the boundless earth with the breaths of the wind.
47	And he took the wand, with which he charms the eyes of men
48	of those he wishes, and again wakes those who are sleeping.
49	Holding this in his hands the strong slayer of Argus flew.
50	And stepping on Pieria he dropped from the upper air into the sea;
51	then he rushed over the wave like a bird, a gull,
52	which down the dread hollows of the barren sea
53	hunting fish wets its close feathers in the brine;
54	like that Hermes rode on the many waves.
55	But when he reached the island that was far away,
56	then stepping from the violet-faced sea to the mainland
57	he went, until he came to the great cave, in which the nymph
58	of the fair braids lived. And he found her inside.
59	A great fire was burning on the hearth, and far off the scent
60	of easily-split cedar and of citron-wood was spreading over the island
61	as they burned. And she inside, singing with a beautiful voice,
62	going up and down the loom, was weaving with a golden shuttle.
63	And a wood had grown around the cave, flourishing,
64	alder and poplar and sweet-smelling cypress.
65	And there long-winged birds were making their nests,
66	owls and hawks and long-tongued sea-crows
67	of the sea, whose care is the works of the sea.
68	And there around the hollow cave had been stretched
69	a cultivated vine in its prime, and it was blooming with clusters.
70	And four springs in a row were flowing with white water,
71	close to each other, turned this way and that.
72	And around them soft meadows of violet and celery
73	were blooming. There even an immortal coming upon it
74	would gaze looking and be delighted in his mind.
75	There the messenger, the slayer of Argus, stood and gazed.
76	But when he had gazed at everything in his own heart,
77	at once he went into the wide cave. And she did not
78	fail to know him when she saw him, Calypso, shining among goddesses:
79	for the gods are not unknown to one another,
80	the immortals, not even if one lives in a house far off.
81	And he did not find great-hearted Odysseus inside,
82	but he was sitting on the shore weeping, just as before,
83	tearing his heart with tears and groans and pains.
84	He kept looking over the barren sea, shedding tears.
85	And Calypso, shining among goddesses, questioned Hermes,
86	seating him on a shining, gleaming throne:
> Calypso
87	Why, Hermes of the golden wand, have you come to me,
88	honored and dear? Before this you have not been coming often at all.
89	Speak what you have in mind. My heart bids me fulfill it,
90	if I can fulfill it, and if it is a thing that can be fulfilled.
91	But come farther in, so I may set guest-gifts beside you.
> (narrator)
92	So speaking the goddess set a table beside him
93	filled with ambrosia, and mixed red nectar.
94	And he drank and ate, the messenger, the slayer of Argus.
95	But when he had dined and filled his heart with food,
96	then he answered her with words and said:
> Hermes
97	You ask me, a god come to a goddess; and I
98	will tell you the word unerringly: for you bid me.
99	Zeus ordered me to come here, though I did not want to.
100	For who would willingly run across so much salt water,
101	measureless? And there is no city of mortals nearby, who
102	offer sacred rites to the gods and choice hecatombs.
103	But it is in no way possible for another god to go past
104	the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus, or to make it fruitless.
105	He says a man is here with you, the most wretched of all
106	of the men who fought around the city of Priam
107	for nine years, and in the tenth they sacked the city and went
108	home. But on the homecoming they offended Athena,
109	who raised against them an evil wind and long waves.
110	There all the others, the good companions, perished,
111	but wind and wave carrying him brought him here.
112	Now he has ordered you to send him away as quickly as possible:
113	for it is not his portion to die here far from his dear ones,
114	but it is still his fate to see his dear ones and to reach
115	his high-roofed house and his own fatherland.
> (narrator)
116	So he spoke, and Calypso, shining among goddesses, shuddered,
117	and speaking she addressed him with winged words:
> Calypso
118	You are hard, you gods, jealous beyond others,
119	you who resent goddesses lying beside men
120	openly, if one makes a man her dear husband.
121	So when rose-fingered Dawn took Orion,
122	you gods who live at ease resented her
123	until in Ortygia holy Artemis of the golden throne
124	going at him with her gentle arrows killed him.
125	So when fair-braided Demeter, yielding to her own heart,
126	mixed in love and in bed with Iasion
127	in a thrice-plowed fallow field: and not long was Zeus
128	without news of it, who killed him, striking with the bright thunderbolt.
129	So now again you resent it, you gods, that a mortal man is with me.
130	I myself saved him as he bestrode the keel
131	alone, after Zeus had struck his swift ship with a bright thunderbolt
132	and smashed it in the middle of the wine-faced sea.
133	There all the others, the good companions, perished,
134	but wind and wave carrying him brought him here.
135	I loved him and I reared him, and I said
136	I would make him immortal and ageless all his days.
137	But since it is in no way possible for another god to go past
138	the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus, or to make it fruitless,
139	let him go, if that one urges him and orders it,
140	over the barren sea. But I will not send him anywhere myself:
141	for I have no oared ships and companions
142	who might send him over the wide back of the sea.
143	But I will readily advise him, and I will not hide it,
144	so that he may come quite unharmed to his fatherland.
> (narrator)
145	Then the messenger, the slayer of Argus, said to her:
> Hermes
146	So now send him away, and have regard for the wrath of Zeus,
147	lest somehow afterward, angry, he be harsh with you.
> (narrator)
148	So speaking the strong slayer of Argus went away.
149	And the lady nymph went to great-hearted Odysseus,
150	when she had heard the messages of Zeus.
151	She found him sitting on the shore. And never did his eyes
152	dry of tears; the sweet life was wasting
153	as he mourned for homecoming, since the nymph no longer pleased him.
154	But at night he would sleep, even under compulsion,
155	in the hollow caves beside her, he unwilling, she willing.
156	And by day, sitting on the rocks and the beaches,
157	tearing his heart with tears and groans and pains,
158	he kept looking over the barren sea, shedding tears.
159	And standing near him she, shining among goddesses, spoke:
> Calypso
160	Hard-fated man, do not still mourn here, and do not let your life
161	waste away. For now I will send you away very readily.
162	But come, cutting long timbers, fit with bronze
163	a wide raft; and set deck-planks on it
164	high, so it may carry you over the misty-faced sea.
165	And I will put in food and water and red wine,
166	heart-satisfying, such as would keep hunger from you,
167	and I will clothe you; and I will send a wind behind you,
168	so that you may come quite unharmed to your fatherland,
169	if the gods are willing, who hold the wide sky,
170	who are stronger than I at thinking and at bringing things to pass.
> (narrator)
171	So she spoke, and much-enduring godlike Odysseus shuddered,
172	and speaking he addressed her with winged words:
> Odysseus
173	Something else, goddess, you are devising in this, and not at all an escort,
174	you who bid me cross the great gulf of the sea on a raft,
175	dread and hard. Not even well-balanced ships,
176	swift-going, cross that, glorying in the wind of Zeus.
177	I would not go aboard a raft against your will,
178	unless you, goddess, would dare to swear a great oath
179	that you will not plan some other evil harm against me myself.
> (narrator)
180	So he spoke, and Calypso, shining among goddesses, smiled,
181	and stroked him with her hand and spoke a word and called him by name:
> Calypso
182	You are a rogue, and you do not know empty things,
183	such a word as you have thought out to speak.
184	Now let this earth know, and the wide sky above,
185	and the down-flowing water of Styx, which is the greatest
186	oath and the most dread for the blessed gods,
187	that I will not plan some other evil harm against you yourself.
188	But I am thinking and will contrive the things that I would
189	devise for myself, whenever such need came upon me.
190	For I have a mind that is right, and my own
191	heart in my chest is not iron, but pitying.
> (narrator)
192	So speaking she, shining among goddesses, led the way
193	quickly. And he then walked in the tracks of the goddess.
194	They came to the hollow cave, the goddess and the man,
195	and he sat there on the throne from which
196	Hermes had risen, and the nymph set all food beside him,
197	to eat and to drink, such as mortal men eat.
198	And she herself sat opposite godlike Odysseus,
199	and the serving-women set ambrosia and nectar beside her.
200	And they put out their hands to the ready good things lying before them.
201	But when they had taken their pleasure of food and drink,
202	Calypso, shining among goddesses, began the talk among them:
> Calypso
203	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
204	so then, homeward to your dear fatherland
205	you want to go at once, right now? Be glad, even so.
206	If only you knew in your mind how many
207	griefs it is your portion to fill up, before you reach your fatherland,
208	you would stay right here with me and keep this house
209	and be immortal, much as you long to see
210	your wife, whom you always yearn for all your days.
211	I claim I am not worse than she,
212	not in body or in growth, since it is in no way even seemly
213	for mortal women to contest with immortals in body and form.
> (narrator)
214	And answering her Odysseus of many designs said:
> Odysseus
215	Lady goddess, do not be angry at me for this. I myself know
216	all of it well, that thoughtful Penelope
217	is weaker than you in form and in size to look at face to face.
218	For she is a mortal, and you are immortal and ageless.
219	But even so I want and I yearn all my days
220	to go home and to see the day of coming home.
221	And if again some god wrecks me on the wine-faced sea,
222	I will endure it, holding in my chest a heart that bears grief.
223	For already I have suffered very many things and toiled at many
224	on the waves and in war. Let this too be added to those.
> (narrator)
225	So he spoke, and the sun went down and dusk came on.
226	And the two of them, going into the inner part of the hollow cave,
227	took their pleasure in love, staying beside one another.
228	When early-born Dawn appeared, rose-fingered,
229	at once Odysseus put on a cloak and a tunic,
230	and the nymph herself put on a great silver-white robe,
231	fine and graceful, and around her waist she threw a belt,
232	beautiful, golden, and over her head a veil.
233	And then she planned an escort for great-hearted Odysseus.
234	She gave him a great axe, fitted to his palms,
235	of bronze, sharpened on both sides; and on it
236	a very beautiful olive-wood handle, well fitted.
237	Then she gave him a well-smoothed adze. And she led the way
238	to the edge of the island, where long trees had grown,
239	alder and poplar, and there was fir sky-high,
240	dry long since, thoroughly seasoned, that would float for him lightly.
241	But when she had shown him where the long trees had grown,
242	she went toward the house, Calypso, shining among goddesses,
243	and he was cutting the timbers. Quickly the work was finished for him.
244	He threw down twenty in all, and he hewed them with the bronze,
245	and he smoothed them skillfully and made them straight to the line.
246	Meanwhile Calypso, shining among goddesses, brought him augers.
247	He bored through all of them and fitted them to one another,
248	and he hammered it together with pegs and with joints.
249	As wide as a man would mark out the floor of a ship,
250	a wide freighter, one who knows well the carpenter's craft,
251	so wide a raft Odysseus made.
252	And setting up deck-planks, fitting them to close-set ribs,
253	he worked; and he finished it with long side-planks.
254	And in it he made a mast and a yard fitted to it;
255	and he also made a steering-oar, so he might keep it straight.
256	And he fenced it all through with willow wattles
257	to be a defense against the wave; and he piled on much brushwood.
258	Meanwhile Calypso, shining among goddesses, brought him cloths
259	to make sails; and he crafted those well too.
260	And he bound in it braces and ropes and sheets,
261	and with levers he hauled it down into the bright sea.
262	It was the fourth day, and everything had been finished for him.
263	And on the fifth shining Calypso sent him from the island,
264	clothing him in sweet-smelling clothes and washing him.
265	And the goddess put in a skin of dark wine for him,
266	one, and the other a great one of water, and in a bag
267	provisions. And she put in many satisfying cooked foods.
268	And she sent forth a wind, harmless and warm.
269	Glad at the wind, godlike Odysseus spread the sails.
270	And he kept it straight with the steering-oar skillfully,
271	sitting, and sleep did not fall on his eyelids
272	as he watched the Pleiades and late-setting Bootes
273	and the Bear, which they also call by the name Wagon,
274	which turns in the same place and watches Orion,
275	and she alone has no share in the baths of Ocean.
276	For Calypso, shining among goddesses, had ordered him
277	to keep her on his left hand as he sailed the sea.
278	For seventeen days he sailed, making his way on the sea,
279	and on the eighteenth the shadowy mountains appeared
280	of the land of the Phaeacians, where it was nearest to him.
281	And it looked like a shield in the misty-faced sea.
282	And the lord earth-shaker, coming up from the Ethiopians,
283	saw him from far off, from the mountains of the Solymi: for he appeared to him
284	sailing over the sea. And he was angry still more in his heart,
285	and shaking his head he spoke to his own spirit:
> Poseidon
286	Look now — the gods have surely changed their purpose otherwise
287	about Odysseus, while I was among the Ethiopians,
288	and already he is near the land of the Phaeacians, where it is his portion
289	to escape the great bound of the misery that is coming on him.
290	But I still say I will drive him to a glut of wretchedness.
> (narrator)
291	So speaking he gathered the clouds and stirred the sea,
292	taking the trident in his hands; and he roused all the storm-blasts
293	of winds of every kind, and with clouds he covered
294	earth together with the sea. And night sprang up from the sky.
295	And East Wind and South Wind fell together, and the ill-blowing West Wind,
296	and North Wind born of the clear sky, rolling a great wave.
297	And then Odysseus's knees and his own heart went loose,
298	and in distress he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:
> Odysseus
299	Wretched me — what is going to become of me at last?
300	I am afraid the goddess told everything unerringly,
301	who said that on the sea, before I reached my fatherland,
302	I would fill up pains. And now all of that is being fulfilled.
303	With such clouds Zeus is wreathing the wide sky,
304	and he has stirred the sea, and the storm-blasts are driving hard
305	of winds of every kind. Now sheer destruction is sure for me.
306	Three times blessed, and four times, the Danaans who perished then
307	in wide Troy, doing a favor for the sons of Atreus.
308	If only I too had died and met my fate
309	on that day when the most Trojans threw bronze-tipped spears
310	at me, around the dead son of Peleus.
311	Then I would have got my share of funeral rites, and the Achaeans would have carried my fame.
312	But now it was my fate to be taken by a miserable death.
> (narrator)
313	So as he was speaking a great wave drove him from above,
314	rushing on dreadfully, and it whirled the raft around.
315	And he himself fell far from the raft, and the steering-oar
316	he let go from his hands; and the mast in the middle it broke,
317	a dread squall coming of the mixing winds,
318	and far off the sail and the yard fell into the sea.
319	And it put him under water for a long time, and he could not
320	come up again quickly under the rush of the great wave.
321	For the clothes weighed him down, the ones shining Calypso had given him.
322	But late then he came up, and from his mouth he spat the brine,
323	bitter, which was gurgling in a mass from his head.
324	But even so he did not forget the raft, worn down as he was,
325	but starting after it in the waves he caught hold of it,
326	and sat in the middle, fending off the end of death.
327	And a great wave carried it down the current this way and that.
328	As when the autumn North Wind carries thistles
329	over the plain, and they cling close to one another,
330	so the winds carried it over the open sea this way and that.
331	Now the South Wind would throw it to the North Wind to carry,
332	and again the East Wind would yield it to the West Wind to chase.
333	And Cadmus's daughter saw him, Ino of the beautiful ankles,
334	Leucothea, who before was a mortal with a human voice,
335	but now in the stretches of the sea she has a share of honor from the gods.
336	She pitied Odysseus as he wandered, having pains,
337	and like a shearwater in flight she rose from the water,
338	and sat on the many-bound raft and spoke a word:
> Ino
339	Hard-fated man, why has Poseidon the earth-shaker
340	hated you so terribly, that he plants many evils for you?
341	He will not destroy you, much as he is raging.
342	But do just this — and you do not seem to me to be without sense:
343	take off these clothes and leave the raft to be carried by the winds,
344	and swimming with your hands reach after the homecoming
345	of the land of the Phaeacians, where it is your fate to escape.
346	Here, stretch this veil under your chest,
347	immortal; and you have no fear of suffering or of perishing.
348	But when you have laid hold of the mainland with your hands,
349	loose it again and throw it into the wine-faced sea
350	far from the mainland, and turn yourself away.
> (narrator)
351	So speaking the goddess gave him the veil,
352	and she herself sank back into the swelling sea
353	like a shearwater; and a dark wave covered her.
354	And much-enduring godlike Odysseus pondered,
355	and in distress he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:
> Odysseus
356	Woe is me — I am afraid some one of the immortals
357	is weaving a trick against me again, who bids me leave the raft.
358	But I will not yet obey, since far off with my eyes
359	I saw the land, where she said there would be escape for me.
360	But I will do this — and it seems to me to be best:
361	as long as the timbers hold in their joints,
362	I will stay right here and endure, suffering pains.
363	But when the wave has shaken the raft apart,
364	I will swim, since there is nothing better to look ahead to.
> (narrator)
365	While he was turning these things over in his mind and in his heart,
366	Poseidon the earth-shaker raised a great wave,
367	dread and hard, arching over, and drove it at him.
368	As when a strong-blowing wind shakes a heap
369	of dry chaff; and it scatters it this way and that —
370	so it scattered the long timbers. And Odysseus
371	bestrode one timber, riding it like a horse,
372	and he stripped off the clothes that shining Calypso had given him.
373	At once he stretched the veil under his chest,
374	and he himself fell face-down into the salt water, spreading his hands,
375	eager to swim. And the lord earth-shaker saw him,
376	and shaking his head he spoke to his own spirit:
> Poseidon
377	So now, having suffered many evils, wander over the sea,
378	until you mix with men nourished by Zeus.
379	But even so I do not expect you will make light of your wretchedness.
> (narrator)
380	So speaking he whipped the beautiful-maned horses,
381	and he came to Aegae, where his famous houses are.
382	Then Athena, daughter of Zeus, thought of something else.
383	She bound the paths of the other winds,
384	and she ordered them all to stop and to lie down;
385	and she raised a rushing North Wind, and broke the waves in front,
386	until Zeus-born Odysseus might mix with the Phaeacians who love the oar,
387	having escaped death and the dooms.
388	There two nights and two days on the solid wave
389	he was driven, and many times his heart looked death in the face.
390	But when fair-braided Dawn finished the third day,
391	then the wind stopped and a calm
392	came, windless. And he saw the land nearby,
393	looking sharply ahead, lifted under a great wave.
394	As when a welcome life appears to children
395	of a father who lies in sickness suffering strong pains,
396	wasting a long time, and a hateful divinity has assailed him,
397	and then the gods have loosed him, welcome, from his wretchedness —
398	so the land and the wood appeared welcome to Odysseus,
399	and he swam, straining with his feet to step on the mainland.
400	But when he was as far away as a man can make himself heard shouting,
401	and now he heard the boom of the sea against the reefs.
402	For a great wave was roaring against the dry land of the mainland,
403	belching dreadfully, and everything was wrapped in the foam of the sea.
404	For there were no harbors to hold ships, nor sheltering coves.
405	But there were jutting headlands, and reefs and crags.
406	And then Odysseus's knees and his dear heart went slack,
407	and in distress he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:
> Odysseus
408	Woe is me — since he has given me land unhoped-for to see,
409	Zeus, and I have cut through this gulf and crossed it,
410	no way out appears anywhere from the grey sea to land.
411	For outside there are sharp crags, and around them the wave
412	roars with a crashing surge, and a smooth rock runs up,
413	and the sea is deep close in, and there is no way with both feet
414	to stand and to escape the evil.
415	I am afraid as I come out a great wave may seize me and dash me
416	against a jagged rock; and my effort will be miserable.
417	But if I swim on still farther, if somewhere I find
418	wave-beaten beaches and harbors of the sea,
419	I am afraid a storm-blast seizing me again
420	may carry me over the fish-filled sea, groaning heavily,
421	or some great sea-monster a divinity may drive at me
422	from the sea, the kind famous Amphitrite feeds many of.
423	For I know how famous Earth-holder has been angry at me.
> (narrator)
424	While he was turning these things over in his mind and in his heart,
425	in that time a great wave was carrying him onto the rough shore.
426	There his skin would have been stripped off, and his bones crushed together,
427	if the goddess had not put it in his mind, owl-eyed Athena.
428	And rushing forward with both hands he caught hold of a rock,
429	and he held onto it groaning, until the great wave went past.
430	And that one he escaped thus, but the back-rushing wave again
431	struck him as it rushed on, and threw him far out into the open sea.
432	As when an octopus is dragged from its den
433	and many pebbles cling to its suckers,
434	so from his bold hands against the rocks
435	the skins were stripped off; and the great wave covered him.
436	There then wretched Odysseus would have perished beyond his fate,
437	if owl-eyed Athena had not given him presence of mind.
438	Coming up out of the wave that belches toward the mainland,
439	he swam sideways, looking toward land, if somewhere he might find
440	wave-beaten beaches and harbors of the sea.
441	But when at the mouth of a fair-flowing river
442	he came swimming, there the place seemed best to him,
443	smooth of rocks, and there was shelter from the wind.
444	And he recognized it flowing forth and prayed in his own heart:
> Odysseus
445	Hear me, lord, whoever you are; I come to you, much-prayed-to,
446	fleeing from the sea the rebukes of Poseidon.
447	A man is worthy of regard even to the immortal gods
448	whoever of men comes wandering, as I now
449	come to your stream and to your knees, having suffered many things.
450	But pity me, lord; I claim to be your suppliant.
> (narrator)
451	So he spoke, and the river at once stopped his current, and held back the wave,
452	and he made a calm in front of him, and saved him
453	into the river's outpourings. And he bent both his knees
