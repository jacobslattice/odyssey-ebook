# The Odyssey: Book II

> (narrator)
1	When early-born Dawn appeared, rose-fingered,
2	the dear son of Odysseus rose from his bed
3	and put on his clothes, and slung a sharp sword about his shoulder,
4	and bound fine sandals under his shining feet,
5	and went out from the chamber, like a god to look at.
6	At once he ordered the clear-voiced heralds
7	to call the long-haired Achaeans to the assembly.
8	They made the call, and the others gathered very quickly.
9	And when they were gathered and had come together,
10	he went to the assembly, and in his palm he held a bronze spear,
11	not alone: two flashing dogs went with him.
12	And Athena poured a divine grace down over him.
13	All the people gazed at him as he came forward.
14	He sat in his father's seat, and the old men made way.
15	Then the hero Aegyptius began to speak among them,
16	who was bent with age and knew countless things.
17	For his dear son too had gone with godlike Odysseus
18	to Ilium of the good horses, in the hollow ships —
19	Antiphus the spearman. The savage Cyclops killed him
20	in the hollow cave, and made him his last meal.
21	He had three other sons: one kept company with the suitors,
22	Eurynomus, and two always kept the father's work.
23	But even so he did not forget that one, mourning and grieving.
24	For him he spoke in the assembly, shedding tears, and said:
> Aegyptius
25	Hear me now, men of Ithaca, whatever I say.
26	Never has our assembly been held, nor any sitting,
27	since the day godlike Odysseus went in the hollow ships.
28	Now who has called us like this? Whom has such need come upon,
29	whether of the young men or of those who are older?
30	Has he heard some news of an army coming,
31	which he would tell us clearly, since he learned it first?
32	Or is he making known and speaking of some other public matter?
33	He seems to me a good man — blessed. May Zeus himself
34	bring to pass for him the good that he intends in his mind.
> (narrator)
35	So he spoke, and the dear son of Odysseus was glad at the spoken omen,
36	and he did not sit long, but was eager to speak.
37	He stood in the middle of the assembly. The herald put the scepter in his hand,
38	Pisenor the herald, who knew sound counsel.
39	Then first he spoke, addressing the old man:
> Telemachus
40	Old man, this man is not far off — you will soon know him yourself —
41	the one who called the people. Pain comes on me most of all.
42	I have not heard any news of an army coming
43	that I would tell you clearly, if I had learned it first,
44	nor am I making known or speaking of any other public matter,
45	but my own need: evils have fallen on my house
46	in two ways. First, I have lost my good father, who once among you
47	here was king, and was gentle as a father.
48	And now there is something far greater, which will soon smash the whole house
49	utterly, and destroy the livelihood completely.
50	Suitors have set upon my mother against her will —
51	the dear sons of the men who are the best here,
52	who shrink from going to the house of her father
53	Icarius, so that he himself might settle the bride-gifts for his daughter
54	and give her to the man he wished, one who came as pleasing to him.
55	But they, coming to our house every day,
56	slaughtering cattle and sheep and fat goats,
57	feast and drink the sparkling wine
58	recklessly. And much is wasted. For there is no longer a man
59	such as Odysseus was, to keep ruin from the house.
60	And we are not such as to defend it. And hereafter too
61	we will be wretched and untrained in fighting strength.
62	I would defend it, if only the power were with me.
63	For the deeds done are no longer bearable, and it is no longer well
64	that my house is ruined. Feel shame yourselves,
65	and have regard for the other neighboring people
66	who live around us. And fear the wrath of the gods,
67	lest they turn things around, angry at the evil deeds.
68	I beg you by Olympian Zeus and by Themis,
69	who both dissolves and seats the assemblies of men:
70	hold back, friends, and leave me alone to be worn down
71	by grim grief — unless perhaps my good father Odysseus
72	did some harm to the well-greaved Achaeans out of hostility,
73	and in paying that back you do me harm, acting as enemies,
74	urging these men on. It would be more profitable for me
75	if you yourselves ate my stored goods and my livestock.
76	If you ate them, there might someday even be requital:
77	for then we would go through the town clinging to you with words,
78	demanding our goods back, until everything was given.
79	But now you cast griefs on my heart that have no remedy.
> (narrator)
80	So he spoke in anger, and threw the scepter to the ground,
81	bursting into tears. And pity took hold of all the people.
82	Then all the others were silent, and no one dared
83	to answer Telemachus with harsh words.
84	But Antinous alone answered him and said:
> Antinous
85	Telemachus, high-talker, ungovernable in force — what kind of thing have you said,
86	shaming us? You would like to fasten blame on us.
87	The suitors of the Achaeans are not at all to blame toward you,
88	but your own dear mother, who knows wiles beyond others.
89	For it is already the third year, and soon the fourth will come,
90	since she has been afflicting the heart in the chests of the Achaeans.
91	She holds out hope to all and makes promises to each man,
92	sending messages — but her mind intends other things.
93	And she devised this other trick in her mind:
94	setting up a great loom in the halls, she wove
95	a web fine and very wide. And at once she said to us:
> Penelope
96	Young men, my suitors, since godlike Odysseus has died,
97	wait, though you are eager for my marriage, until I finish the cloth,
98	so that my spinning is not wasted and lost —
99	a burial cloth for the hero Laertes, for when
100	the ruinous fate of long-sorrowing death takes him down,
101	so that no one among the Achaean women in the district will be angry at me
102	if he who won so much should lie without a winding-sheet.
> Antinous
103	So she spoke, and our proud heart was persuaded.
104	Then by day she would weave the great web,
105	and at night she would unravel it, when she had set torches beside her.
106	So for three years she escaped notice by the trick and persuaded the Achaeans.
107	But when the fourth year came and the seasons came on,
108	then one of the women spoke, who knew it clearly,
109	and we caught her unraveling the shining web.
110	So she finished it, though she did not want to, under compulsion.
111	And the suitors answer you this way, so that you may know
112	in your own heart, and so that all the Achaeans may know:
113	send your mother away, and order her to marry
114	whichever man her father commands and who is pleasing to her.
115	But if she will still keep vexing the sons of the Achaeans for a long time,
116	thinking in her heart of those gifts Athena has given her beyond others —
117	to know beautiful work, and a good mind,
118	and wiles such as we have never yet heard of in anyone, not even of the women of old,
119	those who were before, the fair-haired Achaean women,
120	Tyro and Alcmene and Mycene of the fair crown —
121	none of these knew thoughts like Penelope's.
122	But this one thing she has not thought of in the right way.
123	For they will keep eating your livelihood and your possessions
124	as long as she holds this mind that the gods
125	now put in her chest. She is making great fame for herself,
126	but for you, a longing for much livelihood.
127	And we will not go back to our work, nor anywhere else,
128	until she marries whichever of the Achaeans she wishes.
> (narrator)
129	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
130	Antinous, it is not possible to push from the house against her will
131	the woman who bore me and raised me. My father is in another land,
132	whether he lives or has died. And it will be a hard thing for me to pay a great price
133	to Icarius, if I myself willingly send my mother away.
134	For I will suffer evils from her father, and a god will give
135	others besides, since my mother will call on the hateful Furies
136	as she leaves the house. And righteous anger from people
137	will be on me. So I will never speak this word.
138	If your own heart feels shame at this,
139	go out of my halls, and see to other feasts,
140	eating your own possessions, taking turns from house to house.
141	But if this seems better and finer to you
142	to be — that one man's livelihood be destroyed without payment —
143	then cut it down. I will call on the gods who are forever,
144	in the hope that Zeus may grant that the deeds be paid back.
145	Then you would die inside the house without payment.
> (narrator)
146	So Telemachus spoke, and wide-seeing Zeus sent two eagles for him
147	to fly from on high, from the peak of a mountain.
148	For a while they flew with the breath of the wind,
149	close to each other, stretching their wings.
150	But when they reached the middle of the many-voiced assembly,
151	then they wheeled about and shook their thick wings,
152	and looked down on the heads of all, and they looked death.
153	Tearing with their talons at cheeks and necks
154	they darted to the right, through the Ithacans' houses and city.
155	They were amazed at the birds, when they saw them with their eyes,
156	and they pondered in their hearts the things that were going to come to pass.
157	Then the old hero Halitherses spoke among them
158	as well, the son of Mastor. For he alone of his age excelled
159	at knowing birds and saying what they meant in due measure.
160	He spoke in the assembly with good intent for them, and said:
> Halitherses
161	Hear me now, men of Ithaca, whatever I say.
162	I speak this especially making it known to the suitors:
163	for a great misery is rolling toward them. Odysseus will not
164	be long away from his own people, but already, I think,
165	he is near, and he is planting death and doom for these men,
166	for all of them. And there will be evil for many others as well,
167	who live on clear-seen Ithaca. But long before that
168	let us consider how we may stop it. And let them themselves
169	stop as well. For this is at once better for them.
170	I am not prophesying as one untried, but knowing well.
171	I say that everything was fulfilled for that man
172	just as I told him, when the Argives were going up to Ilium,
173	and Odysseus of many designs went with them.
174	I said that after suffering many evils, and after losing all his companions,
175	unknown to all, in the twentieth year
176	he would come home. And now all of that is coming to pass.
> (narrator)
177	Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, spoke in answer:
> Eurymachus
178	Old man, come now, prophesy to your own children
179	when you go home, so they do not suffer some evil later.
180	In these things I am a far better prophet than you.
181	Many birds wander under the rays of the sun,
182	and not all of them are of due meaning. Odysseus
183	has died far away — as you too, with him,
184	ought to have died. You would not then be making so many god-spoken speeches,
185	nor would you be stirring up Telemachus, angry as he is, like this,
186	looking for a gift for your house, if he should give one.
187	But I will tell you, and this will be accomplished:
188	if you, who know many old things, talk a younger man around
189	with words and urge him to be harsh,
190	it will first be more painful for him himself,
191	and he will not be able to accomplish anything anyway because of these men.
192	And on you, old man, we will lay a fine, which you will resent
193	paying in your heart. And the pain will be hard for you.
194	I myself will put this before Telemachus among you all:
195	let him order his mother to go back to her father's house.
196	They will make the marriage and arrange the bride-gifts,
197	very many, as many as it is right should follow a dear daughter.
198	For I do not think the sons of the Achaeans will stop before that
199	this painful courting, since we fear no one at all —
200	not Telemachus, much-speaking though he is,
201	and we pay no heed to a god-spoken word, which you, old man,
202	tell as something unfulfilled. And you are hated still more.
203	The goods will go on being eaten badly, and there will never be
204	an equal return, as long as she puts the Achaeans off
205	from her marriage. We, waiting every day
206	keep up the quarrel because of her excellence, and we do not go after other women
207	whom it is fitting for each man to marry.
> (narrator)
208	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
209	Eurymachus, and you others, all you proud suitors,
210	I no longer beg you for these things, nor do I speak of them.
211	For the gods already know them, and all the Achaeans.
212	But come, give me a swift ship and twenty companions
213	who will make the way for me here and there.
214	For I will go to Sparta and to sandy Pylos
215	to ask after the homecoming of my father, who has been gone so long,
216	if some mortal will tell me, or I hear a voice
217	from Zeus, which most of all carries fame among people.
218	If I hear that my father is alive and coming home,
219	then, worn down as I am, I would still endure for a year.
220	But if I hear he is dead and no longer living,
221	then I will come home to my own fatherland
222	and heap a mound for him and give him funeral rites
223	in plenty, as many as are right, and I will give my mother to a man.
> (narrator)
224	So he spoke and sat down. And among them rose
225	Mentor, who was a companion of blameless Odysseus,
226	and when Odysseus went in the ships he entrusted the whole house to him,
227	to obey the old man and keep everything steadfast.
228	He spoke in the assembly with good intent for them, and said:
> Mentor
229	Hear me now, men of Ithaca, whatever I say.
230	Let no scepter-bearing king ever again be eager to be kind and gentle,
231	or know what is right in his mind,
232	but let him always be harsh and do unjust things —
233	since no one remembers godlike Odysseus
234	of the people he was lord over, and he was gentle as a father.
235	I do not at all grudge the proud suitors
236	doing violent deeds with the evil-stitching of their minds.
237	For they are staking their own heads as they eat up by force
238	the house of Odysseus, whom they say will no longer come back.
239	But now I am angry at the rest of the people, how you all
240	sit mute, and do not, going at them with words,
241	stop a few suitors, though you are many.
> (narrator)
242	Then Leiocritus, son of Euenor, spoke in answer:
> Leiocritus
243	Mentor, you ruinous man, out of your mind — what kind of thing have you said,
244	urging them to stop us? It is a hard thing
245	even for more men to fight over a feast.
246	For even if Odysseus of Ithaca himself came
247	on the proud suitors feasting in his own house
248	and set his heart on driving them out of the hall,
249	his wife would not rejoice at him, much as she wants him,
250	when he came — he would meet an unseemly fate right there,
251	if he fought with more men. You have not spoken in due measure.
252	But come, let the people scatter, each to his work,
253	and Mentor and Halitherses will speed this man on his road,
254	who have been his father's companions from the start.
255	But I think he will sit a long time in Ithaca asking after news,
256	and he will never complete this journey.
> (narrator)
257	So he spoke, and he broke up the swift assembly.
258	They scattered, each to his own house,
259	and the suitors went to the house of godlike Odysseus.
260	But Telemachus went apart to the shore of the sea,
261	and washing his hands in the gray salt water he prayed to Athena:
> Telemachus
262	Hear me, you who came yesterday as a god to our house
263	and told me to go on a ship over the misty-faced sea
264	to ask after the homecoming of my father, who has been gone so long.
265	The Achaeans are putting all this off,
266	and the suitors most of all, acting badly in their over-pride.
> (narrator)
267	So he spoke in prayer, and Athena came near him,
268	likened to Mentor in body and in voice,
269	and she spoke and addressed him with winged words:
> Athena
270	Telemachus, you will not be worthless or mindless even hereafter,
271	if in fact your father's good force has been dripped into you —
272	such as he was to finish both a deed and a word.
273	Then this journey will not be fruitless or unfinished.
274	But if you are not that man's child and Penelope's,
275	then I do not expect you will finish what you intend.
276	Few sons are like their fathers.
277	Most are worse, and few are better than their father.
278	But since you will not be worthless or mindless even hereafter,
279	and the cunning of Odysseus has not left you altogether,
280	there is hope then that you will finish these deeds.
281	So now let go the plan and the mind of the suitors,
282	the mindless ones, since they are not thoughtful and not just.
283	They do not know death and black doom at all,
284	which is already near them, for all of them to perish on one day.
285	The journey you intend will not be far from you much longer.
286	For I am such a companion to you, your father's friend,
287	and I will fit out a swift ship and go with you myself.
288	You go to the house and keep company with the suitors,
289	and get provisions ready, and fit everything in vessels —
290	wine in two-handled jars, and barley-meal, the marrow of men,
291	in tight skins. I will go through the district
292	and gather companions at once, volunteers. There are ships
293	many in sea-girt Ithaca, new and old.
294	I will look over them and pick whichever is best,
295	and we will quickly fit it out and launch it on the wide sea.
> (narrator)
296	So spoke Athena, daughter of Zeus. And not long
297	did Telemachus wait, once he had heard the voice of the god.
298	He went toward the house, his own heart heavy,
299	and found the proud suitors in the halls
300	flaying goats and singeing fat hogs in the courtyard.
301	Antinous, laughing, went straight at Telemachus,
302	and took his hand and spoke and called him by name:
> Antinous
303	Telemachus, high-talker, ungovernable in force — let no other
304	evil deed or word be a care in your chest,
305	but eat and drink with me, just as before.
306	The Achaeans will surely accomplish all these things for you —
307	the ship and chosen rowers — so you may come the sooner
308	to holy Pylos after news of your proud father.
> (narrator)
309	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
310	Antinous, it is not possible among you overbearing men
311	to feast in silence and take my ease in good cheer.
312	Is it not enough that before this you sheared away many good
313	possessions of mine, you suitors, while I was still a child?
314	Now that I am grown and hear the speech of others
315	and learn from it, and the heart inside me is growing,
316	I will try to throw evil dooms upon you —
317	either by going to Pylos, or right here in this district.
318	I will go, and the journey I speak of will not be fruitless —
319	as a passenger. For I am not in possession of a ship or of rowers.
320	That is how it seemed more profitable to you.
> (narrator)
321	So he spoke, and he pulled his hand from the hand of Antinous
322	easily. The suitors were busy with the feast through the house.
323	They mocked him and taunted him with words.
324	And one of the overproud young men would say this:
> a young man
325	Surely Telemachus is planning death for us.
326	He will bring some defenders from sandy Pylos,
327	or even from Sparta, since he is terribly eager.
328	Or he wants to go to Ephyra, that rich land,
329	to bring back heart-killing drugs from there,
330	throw them in the mixing-bowl, and kill us all.
> (narrator)
331	And another of the overproud young men would say:
> another young man
332	Who knows whether he himself, going on a hollow ship,
333	will die far from his people, wandering just as Odysseus did?
334	That way he would pile even more work on us.
335	For we would divide all the possessions, and the house
336	we would give to his mother to have, and to whoever married her.
> (narrator)
337	So they spoke. He went down into his father's high-roofed chamber,
338	the wide one, where gold and bronze lay piled,
339	and clothing in chests, and plenty of sweet-smelling oil.
340	And jars of old sweet-drinking wine
341	stood there, holding unmixed divine drink inside,
342	fitted in a row against the wall, in case Odysseus
343	should ever come home, even after suffering many pains.
344	Close-locking doors were on it, densely fitted,
345	double doors. A woman, the housekeeper, stayed there night and day,
346	and she guarded everything by the deep-knowing of her mind —
347	Eurycleia, daughter of Ops, son of Pisenor.
348	Telemachus then called her into the chamber and said:
> Telemachus
349	Nurse, come, draw me wine in two-handled jars,
350	sweet wine, the tastiest after that which you are keeping,
351	thinking of that ill-fated man, if from somewhere he should come,
352	Zeus-born Odysseus, having escaped death and dooms.
353	Fill twelve, and fit lids on all of them.
354	And pour me barley-meal in well-sewn skins.
355	Let there be twenty measures of mill-ground barley groats.
356	Keep it to yourself alone. Let it all be got ready together.
357	I will take it in the evening, whenever
358	my mother goes up to the upper room and thinks of sleep.
359	For I will go to Sparta and to sandy Pylos
360	to ask after the homecoming of my dear father, if I hear of it somewhere.
> (narrator)
361	So he spoke, and his dear nurse Eurycleia cried out,
362	and in her lament she addressed him with winged words:
> Eurycleia
363	Why, dear child, has this thought come into your mind?
364	Where do you want to go over the wide earth,
365	you, an only son, so loved? He has died far from his fatherland,
366	Zeus-born Odysseus, in a people who do not know him.
367	And they, the moment you go, will plot evils behind you,
368	how they may kill you by a trick, and they themselves will divide all this.
369	No — stay here, sitting over what is yours. There is no need
370	for you to suffer evils on the barren sea, or to wander.
> (narrator)
371	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
372	Take heart, nurse. This plan is not without a god.
373	But swear not to tell these things to my dear mother
374	until the eleventh or the twelfth day has come,
375	or until she herself misses me and hears I have set out,
376	so that she may not, weeping, spoil her beautiful skin.
> (narrator)
377	So he spoke, and the old woman swore a great oath by the gods.
378	And when she had sworn and finished the oath,
379	she at once drew wine for him in two-handled jars,
380	and poured barley-meal for him in well-sewn skins.
381	Telemachus went to the house and kept company with the suitors.
382	Then the goddess, owl-eyed Athena, thought of something else.
383	Likened to Telemachus she went through the city everywhere,
384	and standing beside each man she spoke her word,
385	and told them to gather at evening at the swift ship.
386	And she asked Noemon, the shining son of Phronius,
387	for a swift ship. He readily agreed.
388	The sun set and all the streets grew shadowy,
389	and then he hauled the swift ship to the sea, and in it
390	she set all the gear that well-benched ships carry.
391	He moored it at the outer edge of the harbor, and the good companions
392	gathered in a body. The goddess urged each one on.
393	Then the goddess, owl-eyed Athena, thought of something else.
394	She went toward the house of godlike Odysseus.
395	There she poured sweet sleep over the suitors,
396	and drove the drinkers off course, and knocked the cups from their hands.
397	They rose to go sleep through the city, and they no longer
398	sat long, since sleep was falling on their eyelids.
399	Then owl-eyed Athena spoke to Telemachus,
400	calling him out from the well-settled halls,
401	likened to Mentor in body and in voice:
> Athena
402	Telemachus, already your well-greaved companions
403	are sitting at the oars, waiting for your start.
404	Let us go, so we do not put the journey off any longer.
> (narrator)
405	So she spoke, and Pallas Athena led the way
406	quickly. He then walked in the footsteps of the god.
407	And when they had come down to the ship and the sea,
408	they found the long-haired companions on the shore.
409	Then the holy strength of Telemachus spoke among them:
> Telemachus
410	Come, friends, let us carry the provisions. Everything is already
411	together in the hall. My mother has not learned of it at all,
412	nor the other serving-women — only one has heard the word.
> (narrator)
413	So he spoke and led the way, and they went with him.
414	They carried everything and set it on the well-benched ship,
415	as the dear son of Odysseus had ordered.
416	Telemachus went on board the ship, and Athena led.
417	She sat down in the stern of the ship, and near her
418	Telemachus sat. They loosed the stern-cables,
419	and they themselves went aboard and sat down at the oarlocks.
420	Owl-eyed Athena sent them a following wind,
421	a fresh Zephyr, sounding over the wine-faced sea.
422	Telemachus urged his companions on and ordered them
423	to take hold of the gear. They heard him urging.
424	They set the fir mast inside the hollow mast-box,
425	raising it, and bound it down with forestays,
426	and hauled the white sails with well-twisted oxhide ropes.
427	The wind bellied the sail in the middle, and around the keel
428	the dark wave roared loud as the ship went.
429	She ran on the wave, making her way.
430	When they had made the gear fast on the swift black ship
431	they set up mixing-bowls crowned with wine
432	and poured libations to the deathless gods who live forever,
433	and of all of them most of all to the owl-eyed daughter of Zeus.
434	All night long she cleaved her way, and into the dawn.
