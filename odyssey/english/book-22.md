# The Odyssey: Book XXII

> (narrator)
1	Odysseus of many devices was stripped of the rags
2	and sprang onto the great threshold, holding the bow and the quiver
3	full of arrows, and he poured out the swift arrows
4	there in front of his feet, and he spoke among the suitors:
> Odysseus
5	This unhurtful contest has been finished.
6	Now again another mark, which no man has yet hit,
7	I will see if I hit it, and if Apollo gives me the boast.
> (narrator)
8	He spoke and was aiming a bitter arrow at Antinous.
9	He was going to lift a beautiful cup,
10	gold, two-handled, and he was already turning it in his hands,
11	so he might drink wine. Killing was not a care
12	in his heart. Who would think among dining men
13	that one among more, even if he were very strong,
14	would make evil death and black fate for him?
15	Odysseus taking aim hit him in the throat with the arrow,
16	and the point went through the soft neck opposite.
17	He leaned the other way, and the cup fell from his hand
18	as he was hit, and at once a thick pipe came up through the nostrils
19	of a man's blood. Quickly he pushed the table from himself
20	striking it with his foot, and he poured the food to the ground.
21	Bread and roasted meats were mixed. They made a noise,
22	the suitors through the house, when they saw the man fallen,
23	and they sprang up from the thrones stirred through the house,
24	looking around every way toward the well-built walls.
25	There was no shield anywhere and no fighting spear to take.
26	They quarreled with Odysseus in angry words:
> a suitor
27	Stranger, you shoot men badly. You will no longer
28	meet other contests. Now sheer destruction is safe for you.
29	You have now killed a man who was far the best
30	of the young men in Ithaca. Therefore vultures will eat you here.
> (narrator)
31	Each man was guessing, since they said he had not wanted
32	to kill the man. The fools did not mark
33	how the ends of destruction had been fastened on them, even all.
34	Looking from under his brows Odysseus of many devices spoke to them:
> Odysseus
35	Dogs, you no longer said I would come home turning back
36	from the people of the Trojans, because you were cutting down my house,
37	and you were lying beside the serving-women by force,
38	and while I myself was living you were wooing my wife under,
39	not fearing the gods who hold the wide heaven,
40	and not that any blame of humans would be afterward.
41	Now the ends of destruction have been fastened on you, even all.
> (narrator)
42	So he spoke, and pale fear took them all under.
43	Each looked around where he might escape sheer destruction.
44	Eurymachus alone answering spoke to him:
> Eurymachus
45	If you have come as Odysseus of Ithaca,
46	you have said these things as portioned, as many as the Achaeans were doing,
47	many reckless things in the halls, and many in the country.
48	But the one who was the cause of all already lies,
49	Antinous. He led on these works,
50	not needing the marriage so much or wanting it,
51	but thinking other things, which the son of Cronus did not finish for him,
52	so that he himself would be king through the people of well-built Ithaca,
53	and he would kill your child setting an ambush.
54	Now he has been killed in portion. Spare your
55	people. We afterward making it good through the people,
56	as much as has been drunk and eaten in the halls,
57	bringing honor besides, twenty cattle-worth each,
58	we will give back bronze and gold, until your heart
59	is warmed. Before that it is not blameworthy to be angry.
> (narrator)
60	Looking from under his brows Odysseus of many devices spoke to him:
> Odysseus
61	Eurymachus, not even if you gave back all my father's things,
62	as much as you have now and if you put other things on from somewhere,
63	not even so would I still leave off my hands from killing
64	before the suitors pay back all the overstepping.
65	Now it lies beside you opposite either to fight
66	or to flee, whoever escapes death and the fates.
67	I do not think anyone will flee sheer destruction.
> (narrator)
68	So he spoke, and their knees and own heart were loosed right there.
69	Eurymachus addressed them a second time again:
> Eurymachus
70	Friends, this man will not hold his unfastening hands,
71	but since he has taken the well-polished bow and the quiver,
72	he will shoot from the polished threshold until he kills all
73	of us. Let us remember battle.
74	Draw swords and hold the tables opposite
75	the quick-fated arrows. Let us all hold on him
76	in a heap, if we may push him from the threshold and the doors,
77	and let us go through the town, and let a shout come most quickly.
78	Then this man would soon shoot for the last time now.
> (narrator)
79	So speaking he drew a sharp sword
80	of bronze, pointed on both sides, and sprang at him
81	shouting terribly. At the same time godlike Odysseus
82	sent an arrow, and he hit the chest beside the breast,
83	and he fixed the swift missile in his liver. From his hand
84	he let the sword go to the ground, and sprawling at the table
85	he fell doubled, and he poured the food to the ground
86	and the two-handled cup. He was striking the earth with his forehead
87	paining in his heart, and with both feet
88	kicking he was shaking the throne. A mist was poured down over his eyes.
89	Amphinomus made for famous Odysseus
90	rushing opposite, and he drew a sharp sword,
91	if somehow Odysseus would give way from the doors. But Telemachus
92	reached him first, hitting him from behind with a bronze-fitted spear
93	between the shoulders, and he drove it through the chest.
94	He fell with a thud, and he drove the earth with his whole forehead.
95	Telemachus sprang away, leaving the long-shadowed spear
96	there in Amphinomus. He was afraid someone of the Achaeans
97	might drive the long-shadowed spear as he was drawing it out
98	rushing with a sword, or hitting him as he bent forward.
99	He went running, and very quickly he reached his own father,
100	and standing near he addressed winged words:
> Telemachus
101	Father, I will already bring you a shield and two spears
102	and a helmet all bronze, fitted on the temples,
103	and I myself going will put armor around me, and I will give the swineherd
104	and the cowherd others. It is better to be armed.
> (narrator)
105	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
106	Bring them running, while I have arrows to defend,
107	so they do not move me from the doors being one.
> (narrator)
108	So he spoke, and Telemachus obeyed his own father.
109	He went to go to the room where the famous gear lay.
110	From there he took out four shields, and eight spears
111	and four bronze-fitted helmets with horsehair.
112	He went carrying them, and very quickly he reached his own father.
113	He himself first put bronze around his skin.
114	So also the two slaves put on the beautiful gear,
115	and they stood around war-minded Odysseus of many-colored devices.
116	As long as he had arrows to defend himself,
117	he was always hitting one of the suitors in his own house
118	aiming. They were falling close together.
119	When the arrows left the lord shooting,
120	he leaned the bow against a post of the well-based hall
121	to stand, against the all-shining wall-faces,
122	and he himself set a four-layered shield around his shoulders,
123	and on his strong head he put a well-made helmet,
124	horse-tailed, and the crest was nodding terribly above.
125	He took two fighting spears, pointed with bronze.
126	There was a raised side-door in the well-built wall,
127	and beside the highest threshold of the well-based hall
128	there was a way to a passage, and close-fitted planks held it.
129	Odysseus told the godlike swineherd to watch it
130	standing near it. There was only one onset.
131	Agelaus spoke among them, showing a word to all:
> Agelaus
132	Friends, would someone go up through the raised side-door
133	and tell the people, and a shout come most quickly?
134	Then this man would soon shoot for the last time now.
> (narrator)
135	Then Melanthius the goatherd spoke to him:
> Melanthius
136	There is no way, Agelaus nursed by Zeus. Terribly near
137	are the beautiful door-leaves of the courtyard and the hard mouth of the passage.
138	One man would hold all back, if he were fighting-strong.
139	Come, I will bring you gear to put on
140	from the room. Inside, I think, and not anywhere else
141	Odysseus and the shining son put the gear away.
> (narrator)
142	So speaking Melanthius the goatherd went up
143	into the rooms of Odysseus up through the breaks of the hall.
144	From there he took out twelve shields, and as many spears
145	and as many bronze-fitted helmets with horsehair.
146	He went to go, and carrying them very quickly he gave them to the suitors.
147	Then the knees and own heart of Odysseus were loosed,
148	when he saw them putting the gear around them and shaking
149	long spears with their hands. The work appeared great to him.
150	At once he addressed winged words to Telemachus:
> Odysseus
151	Telemachus, surely some one of the women in the halls
152	is urging an evil war for the two of us, or Melantheus.
> (narrator)
153	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
154	Father, I myself missed this — no one else
155	is the cause — I who left the closely fitted door of the room
156	leaned open. Their watcher was better.
157	Go, godlike Eumaeus, put the door of the room to
158	and mark whether it is some one of the women who is doing these things,
159	or the son of Dolius, Melantheus, whom I think it is.
> (narrator)
160	So they spoke such things to one another,
161	and Melanthius the goatherd went to the room again
162	to bring beautiful gear. The godlike swineherd marked it,
163	and at once he addressed Odysseus being near:
> Eumaeus
164	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
165	that man again, the unseen-making man, whom we ourselves think,
166	is going to the room. Tell me without missing
167	whether I should kill him, if I become stronger,
168	or lead him here to you, so he may pay back many oversteppings,
169	as many as this man has contrived in your own house.
> (narrator)
170	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
171	I and Telemachus will hold the shining suitors
172	inside the halls, even being very eager.
173	You two, twisting his feet and the hands above
174	throw him into the room, and bind the planks behind,
175	and fastening a twisted rope from him
176	draw him up a high pillar and bring him near the roof-beams,
177	so that being alive a long time he may suffer hard pains.
> (narrator)
178	So he spoke, and they heard him very much and obeyed.
179	They went to go to the room, and they escaped his notice being inside.
180	He was searching the gear down the inner part of the room,
181	and the two stood on each side beside the posts waiting.
182	When Melanthius the goatherd was going over the threshold
183	carrying a beautiful helmet in one hand
184	and in the other an old wide shield, stained with rust,
185	of the hero Laertes, which he used to carry as a young man.
186	Then it already lay, and the stitchings of the straps were loosed.
187	The two rushing took him and dragged him inside
188	by the hair, and they threw him to the ground on the floor, paining in his heart,
189	and they bound the feet and hands together with a heart-painful bond
190	twisting them well all the way through, as
191	the son of Laertes, much-enduring godlike Odysseus, had told them.
192	Fastening a twisted rope from him
193	they drew him up a high pillar and brought him near the roof-beams.
194	Cutting him you spoke, swineherd Eumaeus:
> Eumaeus
195	Now very much, Melanthius, you will watch the night
196	lying in a soft bed, as is fitting for you.
197	Early-born gold-throned Dawn coming beside the streams of Ocean
198	will not escape you, when you drive
199	goats through the house for the suitors to work a feast.
> (narrator)
200	So he was left there, stretched in a destroying bond.
201	The two putting on gear, putting the shining door to,
202	went to war-minded Odysseus of many-colored devices.
203	There they stood breathing force, the four on the threshold,
204	and those inside the house many and good.
205	The daughter of Zeus, Athena, came near them,
206	like Mentor in body and also in voice.
207	Odysseus seeing her was glad and said a word:
> Odysseus
208	Mentor, ward off the curse, and remember a dear companion,
209	who used to do good things for you. You are of my own age.
> (narrator)
210	So he spoke, thinking it was Athena who stirs the people.
211	The suitors from the other side were shouting together in the halls.
212	Agelaus son of Damastor first rebuked her:
> Agelaus
213	Mentor, let Odysseus not talk you around with words
214	to fight the suitors and to defend him himself.
215	I think our mind will be finished this way:
216	when we kill these, the father and the son,
217	you will then be killed among them too, such things as you want
218	to do in the halls. You will pay with your own head.
219	When we have taken your violences away with bronze,
220	as many possessions as you have, those inside and those at the door,
221	we will mix with those of Odysseus. We will not let your sons
222	live in the halls, or your daughters,
223	or your trusted wife go about through the town of Ithaca.
> (narrator)
224	So he spoke, and Athena was more angry in her heart,
225	and she quarreled with Odysseus in angry words:
> Athena
226	Odysseus, the force is no longer fixed in you and there is no fighting strength
227	such as when around white-armed Helen of a good father
228	you were fighting the Trojans for nine years always without stopping,
229	and you killed many men in the terrible battle-work,
230	and by your plan the wide-streeted city of Priam was taken.
231	How now, when you reach your own house and possessions,
232	do you wail to be fighting-strong opposite the suitors?
233	Come here, dear, stand beside me and see the work,
234	so you may know what sort Mentor son of Alcimus is
235	among enemy men at paying back good deeds.
> (narrator)
236	She spoke, and she was not yet giving a victory that turns the other way at all,
237	but she was still trying the strength and the fighting strength
238	of both Odysseus and the famous son.
239	She herself sat up on the sooty roof-beam of the hall
240	springing, like a swallow to look at.
241	Agelaus son of Damastor was urging the suitors,
242	and Eurynomus and Amphimedon and Demoptolemus
243	and Peisander son of Polyctor and war-minded Polybus.
244	These were outstanding best of the suitors in excellence,
245	as many as were still living and fighting about their lives.
246	The bow and the close arrows had already broken the others.
247	Agelaus spoke among them, showing a word to all:
> Agelaus
248	Friends, this man will already hold his unfastening hands.
249	Mentor has already gone saying empty boasts,
250	and they are left alone at the first doors.
251	Therefore now do not all throw the long spears together,
252	but come, you six throw first, if somehow Zeus
253	gives Odysseus to be hit and to take fame.
254	There is no care for the others, when this man falls.
> (narrator)
255	So he spoke, and they all threw as he told them,
256	eager. Athena made all those things empty.
257	One had hit a post of the well-based hall,
258	another the closely fitted door.
259	Another's bronze-heavy ash fell in the wall.
260	When they had avoided the spears of the suitors,
261	much-enduring godlike Odysseus began the words:
> Odysseus
262	Friends, I would already say even for us
263	to throw into the crowd of the suitors, who are eager
264	to strip us on top of the earlier evils.
> (narrator)
265	So he spoke, and they all threw sharp spears
266	aiming opposite. Odysseus hit Demoptolemus,
267	Telemachus Eurades, the swineherd Elatus,
268	and the man overseer of cattle killed Peisander.
269	They then all together took the unspeakable floor with their teeth,
270	and the suitors drew back to the inner part of the hall.
271	The others rushed on and took the spears out of the dead.
272	Again the suitors threw sharp spears
273	eager. Athena made many of those things empty.
274	One had hit a post of the well-based hall,
275	another the closely fitted door.
276	Another's bronze-heavy ash fell in the wall.
277	Amphimedon hit Telemachus on the hand at the wrist
278	grazing, and the bronze damaged the surface of the skin.
279	Ctesippus with a long spear over the shield
280	scratched Eumaeus on the shoulder. It flew over and fell to the ground.
281	Those again around war-minded Odysseus of many-colored devices
282	threw sharp spears into the crowd of the suitors.
283	Then Odysseus sacker of cities hit Eurydamas,
284	Telemachus Amphimedon, the swineherd Polybus.
285	Then the man overseer of cattle
286	had hit Ctesippus in the chest, and boasting he addressed him:
> Philoetius
287	Son of Polytherses, lover of cutting words, never wholly
288	yielding to mindlessness say a great thing, but to the gods
289	turn the word over, since they are much better.
290	This is a guest-gift for you instead of the foot, which you once gave
291	to godlike Odysseus wandering through the house.
> (narrator)
292	So the overseer of the twisting cattle spoke. Odysseus
293	wounded the son of Damastor at close range with a long spear.
294	Telemachus wounded Leocritus son of Euenor
295	in the middle of the flank with a spear, and he drove the bronze through.
296	He fell forward, and he drove the earth with his whole forehead.
297	Then Athena held up the man-wasting aegis
298	from above out of the roof. Their wits were scared.
299	They were fleeing through the hall like herd cows
300	which a darting gadfly coming on has shaken
301	in the spring season, when the days become long.
302	The others like vultures, hooked-clawed, crooked-beaked,
303	coming from the mountains leap on the birds.
304	Those go cowering at the clouds in the plain,
305	and these destroy them leaping on them, and there is no fighting strength
306	and no flight. Men are glad at the hunt.
307	So they rushing at the suitors through the house
308	were hitting them turning every way. An unseemly groan was rising
309	of heads being hit, and the whole floor was raging with blood.
310	Leodes rushing at Odysseus took his knees,
311	and begging he addressed winged words to him:
> Leodes
312	I take your knees, Odysseus. Respect me and pity me.
313	I say I have not yet said or done any reckless thing
314	to any of the women in the halls. I used to
315	stop the other suitors too, whoever would do such things.
316	They were not obeying me to hold their hands from evils.
317	Therefore by their recklessness they have met an unseemly fate.
318	I a sacrifice-watcher among them having done nothing
319	will lie, so there is no thanks afterward for good-doers.
> (narrator)
320	Looking from under his brows Odysseus of many devices spoke to him:
> Odysseus
321	If you claim to be a sacrifice-watcher among them,
322	you were often going to pray in the halls
323	that the end of a sweet homecoming be far from me,
324	and that my own wife follow you and that you bear children.
325	Therefore you would not escape a hard-to-tell death.
> (narrator)
326	So speaking he took a sword in a thick hand
327	lying, which Agelaus had sent forward to the ground
328	being killed. With that he drove him through the middle of the neck.
329	While he was still speaking his head was mixed in the dust.
330	The singer, son of Terpius, was still avoiding black fate,
331	Phemius, who used to sing among the suitors by force.
332	He stood holding the clear lyre in his hands
333	near beside the raised side-door. He was considering two ways in his mind,
334	whether slipping out of the hall he should sit at the altar of great Zeus
335	of the enclosure, built, where
336	Laertes and Odysseus used to burn many thigh-bones of cattle,
337	or rushing he should beg Odysseus at the knees.
338	As he was thinking it seemed more profitable
339	to take hold of the knees of Odysseus son of Laertes.
340	He put the hollow lyre down to the ground
341	between the mixing-bowl and the silver-nailed throne,
342	and he himself rushing at Odysseus took his knees,
343	and begging he addressed winged words to him:
> Phemius
344	I take your knees, Odysseus. Respect me and pity me.
345	There will be a pain for you yourself afterward, if you
346	kill a singer, who sings for gods and humans.
347	I am self-taught, and a god has grown in my mind
348	paths of song of all kinds. I am fit to sing beside you
349	as beside a god. Therefore do not be eager to cut my throat.
350	Telemachus also would say these things, your own son,
351	that I was not at all willing going to your house and not wanting it
352	coming among the suitors to sing at the feasts,
353	but many more and stronger led me by force.
> (narrator)
354	So he spoke, and the holy force of Telemachus heard him,
355	and at once he addressed his own father being near:
> Telemachus
356	Hold, and do not wound this man without cause with bronze.
357	We will also save the herald Medon, who always
358	used to care for me in our house when I was a child,
359	if Philoetius has not already killed him, or the swineherd,
360	or he has met you stirred through the house.
> (narrator)
361	So he spoke, and Medon heard him, knowing thoughtful things.
362	He was lying crouched under a throne, and around him
363	he had put on a newly-skinned hide of an ox, avoiding black fate.
364	At once he rose from the throne, and quickly he stripped off the oxhide
365	and then rushing at Telemachus took his knees,
366	and begging he addressed winged words to him:
> Medon
367	Dear, I am this man here. Hold, and tell your father
368	not to damage me being stronger with sharp bronze,
369	angry at the suitor-men, who were cutting
370	the possessions in the halls, and they as fools did not honor you at all.
> (narrator)
371	Smiling on him Odysseus of many devices spoke:
> Odysseus
372	Take heart, since this man has already drawn you and saved you,
373	so you may know in your heart, and also say to another,
374	that good-doing is much better than bad-working.
375	Go out of the halls and sit at the door
376	from the killing into the courtyard, you and the much-voiced singer,
377	until I work through the house whatever I must.
> (narrator)
378	So he spoke, and the two went out of the hall going,
379	and they sat at the altar of great Zeus,
380	looking around every way, always waiting for killing.
381	Odysseus looked through his own house, if any of the men
382	still living might be hiding, avoiding black fate.
383	He saw them very all fallen in blood and dust
384	many, as when fish which fishermen
385	have drawn out of the grey sea onto a hollow beach
386	with a many-eyed net. They all
387	wanting the waves of the sea are poured on the sands.
388	Shining Sun has taken the spirit out of them.
389	So then the suitors were poured on one another.
390	Then Odysseus of many devices spoke to Telemachus:
> Odysseus
391	Telemachus, come, call the nurse Eurycleia for me,
392	so I may say a word that is according to my heart.
> (narrator)
393	So he spoke, and Telemachus obeyed his own father.
394	Moving the door he spoke to the nurse Eurycleia:
> Telemachus
395	Come here, rise, old woman born long ago, who are the watcher
396	of the serving-women through our halls.
397	Come. My father is calling you, so he may say something.
> (narrator)
398	So he spoke, and the word was wingless for her.
399	She opened the doors of the well-lived halls
400	and went to go. Telemachus was leading in front.
401	She then found Odysseus among the killed dead,
402	stained with blood and gore like a lion
403	who comes having eaten an ox of the field.
404	All his chest and both cheeks
405	are bloody, and he is terrible to look at in the face.
406	So Odysseus was stained, the feet and the hands above.
407	When she saw the dead and the unspeakable blood,
408	she started to raise the cry, since she saw a great work.
409	Odysseus held her back and checked her even though she was eager,
410	and speaking he addressed winged words to her:
> Odysseus
411	Be glad in your heart, old woman, and hold, and do not raise the cry.
412	It is not holy to boast over killed men.
413	The fate of the gods and hard works broke these.
414	They used to honor no one of humans on the earth,
415	not a bad one and not a good one, whoever reached them.
416	Therefore by their recklessness they have met an unseemly fate.
417	Come, tell me the women in the halls,
418	those who dishonor me and those who are without fault.
> (narrator)
419	Then the own nurse Eurycleia spoke to him:
> Eurycleia
420	I will tell you the truth, child.
421	There are fifty women in the halls for you,
422	serving-women, whom we have taught to work works,
423	to card wool and to bear slavery.
424	Of those twelve in all have gone onto shamelessness,
425	honoring neither me nor Penelope herself.
426	Telemachus was newly growing, and the mother
427	would not let him give orders over the serving-women.
428	Come, I going up to the shining upper rooms
429	will tell your wife, on whom some god has raised sleep.
> (narrator)
430	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
431	Do not wake this woman yet. Tell the women here
432	to come, those who were contriving unseemly things before.
> (narrator)
433	So he spoke, and the old woman went through the hall
434	to tell the women and urge them to come.
435	He calling Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd
436	to himself addressed winged words:
> Odysseus
437	Begin now to carry the dead, and tell the women.
438	Then the very beautiful thrones and the tables
439	clean with water and many-holed sponges.
440	When you have set the whole house in order,
441	leading the serving-women out of the well-based hall,
442	between the round-house and the faultless enclosure of the courtyard,
443	strike them with long-edged swords, until from all of them
444	you take the lives out and they forget Aphrodite,
445	which they used to have under the suitors, mixing in secret.
> (narrator)
446	So he spoke, and the women came all together,
447	lamenting terribly, pouring a blooming tear.
448	First they were carrying the dead who had died,
449	and they set them down under the porch of the well-fenced courtyard,
450	propping them on one another. Odysseus was giving signs
451	himself urging them on. They were carrying them out even by force.
452	Then they were cleaning the very beautiful thrones and the tables
453	with water and many-holed sponges.
454	Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd
455	were scraping the floor of the closely made house with shovels.
456	The serving-women were carrying it and setting it at the door.
457	When they had set the whole hall in order,
458	leading the serving-women out of the well-based hall,
459	between the round-house and the faultless enclosure of the courtyard,
460	they penned them in a narrow place, from which there was no way to escape.
461	Thoughtful Telemachus began to speak among them:
> Telemachus
462	I would not take the life from these with a clean death,
463	who have poured insults on my head
464	and on our mother, and they used to sleep beside the suitors.
> (narrator)
465	So he spoke, and fastening a cable of a dark-prowed ship
466	from a great pillar he threw it around the round-house,
467	stretching it high, so that no one would reach the floor with her feet.
468	As when long-winged thrushes or doves
469	strike into a snare that has been set in a bush,
470	going to a roost, and a hated lying-down has received them,
471	so they had their heads in a row, and around all
472	the throats there were nooses, so they would die most pitiably.
473	They gasped with their feet a little while, not very long at all.
474	They led Melanthius out through the doorway and the courtyard.
475	They cut off his nose and ears with pitiless bronze
476	and they drew out the genitals, for the dogs to divide raw,
477	and they cut off the hands and feet with an angry heart.
478	Then washing their hands and feet
479	they went to Odysseus into the house, and the work was finished.
480	He spoke to his own nurse Eurycleia:
> Odysseus
481	Bring sulphur, old woman, a cure of evils, and bring me fire,
482	so I may sulphur the hall. Tell Penelope
483	to come here with serving-women.
484	Urge all the serving-women to come through the house.
> (narrator)
485	Then the own nurse Eurycleia spoke to him:
> Eurycleia
486	Yes, my child, you have said these things according to portion.
487	Come, I will bring you a cloak and a tunic, clothes,
488	and do not stand so in the halls packed with rags
489	on the wide shoulders. It would be blameworthy.
> (narrator)
490	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
491	Let fire first now come for me in the halls.
> (narrator)
492	So he spoke, and the own nurse Eurycleia did not disobey.
493	She brought fire and sulphur. Odysseus
494	sulphured well the hall and the house and the courtyard.
495	The old woman went away through the beautiful house of Odysseus
496	to tell the women and urge them to come.
497	They were going from the hall holding a torch in their hands.
498	They poured around Odysseus and greeted him,
499	and they kissed him loving him, the head and the shoulders
500	and taking the hands. A sweet desire was taking him
501	of crying and groaning, and he knew all of them in his mind.
