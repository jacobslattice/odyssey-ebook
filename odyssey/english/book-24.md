# The Odyssey: Book XXIV

> (narrator)
1	Hermes Cyllenian was calling out the souls
2	of the suitor men. He had a wand in his hands,
3	beautiful, gold, with which he charms the eyes of men
4	whom he wishes, and again he wakes those who are sleeping.
5	With this he led them, having stirred them, and they followed twittering.
6	As when bats in the recess of a sacred cave
7	fly twittering, when one has fallen
8	from the cluster off the rock, and they hold onto one another:
9	so they went together twittering. And leading them
10	kind Hermes went along the moldy ways.
11	They went past the streams of Ocean and the White Rock,
12	and past the gates of the Sun and the people of dreams
13	they went. Soon they reached the asphodel meadow,
14	where souls live, images of those who have labored.
15	They found the soul of Achilles son of Peleus
16	and of Patroclus and of blameless Antilochus
17	and of Ajax, who was best in form and body
18	of the other Danaans after the blameless son of Peleus.
19	So they were gathering around him. And near
20	came the soul of Agamemnon son of Atreus
21	grieving. And around him others had gathered, as many as with him
22	died in Aegisthus's house and followed their fate.
23	The soul of the son of Peleus spoke to him first:
> Achilles
24	Son of Atreus, we said that to Zeus who delights in the thunderbolt
25	you were dear, among hero men, all the days,
26	because you were lord over many and strong men
27	in the people of the Trojans, where we Achaeans suffered pains.
28	But even to you too, early, ruinous fate was going to stand beside you,
29	which no one escapes, whoever is born.
30	You should have, having enjoyed the honor you were lord of,
31	followed death and fate in the people of the Trojans.
32	Then the All-Achaeans would have made you a tomb,
33	and for your child too they would have won great fame afterward.
34	But now it was fated for you to be taken by the most pitiable death.
> (narrator)
35	Then the soul of the son of Atreus spoke to him:
> Agamemnon
36	Blessed son of Peleus, Achilles like the gods,
37	you who died at Troy far from Argos. And around you others
38	were being killed, the best sons of Trojans and Achaeans,
39	fighting around you. And you in the swirl of dust
40	lay great, greatly, having forgotten horsemanship.
41	And we were fighting the whole day. We would not at all
42	have stopped the war, if Zeus had not stopped it with a storm.
43	But when we had carried you to the ships out of the war,
44	we laid you on a bed, having cleaned the beautiful skin
45	with warm water and oil. And around you many
46	hot tears the Danaans poured, and they cut their hair.
47	And your mother came from the salt with the deathless sea-women,
48	hearing the news. A cry rose over the sea,
49	unearthly, and trembling took all the Achaeans under.
50	And they would have sprung up and gone to the hollow ships,
51	if a man had not held them back, one who knew old things and many things,
52	Nestor, whose counsel even before appeared the best.
53	He with good sense spoke in assembly among them and said:
54	Hold, Argives, do not flee, young men of the Achaeans.
55	This is the mother from the salt with the deathless sea-women
56	coming, meeting her son who has died.
57	So he spoke, and the great-hearted Achaeans held back from flight.
58	And around you the daughters of the old man of the sea stood
59	lamenting pitiably, and they put immortal clothes around you.
60	And the nine Muses, all of them, answering with a beautiful voice
61	were singing the dirge. Then you would not have seen anyone without tears
62	of the Argives. For such a thing the clear Muse stirred up.
63	Seventeen days and nights alike
64	we wept for you, deathless gods and mortal humans.
65	On the eighteenth we gave you to the fire, and around you many
66	very fat sheep we killed and crumpled-horn cattle.
67	You were burning in clothing of the gods and much oil
68	and sweet honey. And many hero Achaeans
69	in armor moved around the pyre as you burned,
70	both on foot and horsemen. A great din rose.
71	But when the flame of Hephaestus had finished you,
72	at dawn we gathered your white bones, Achilles,
73	in unmixed wine and oil. And your mother gave
74	a gold amphora. She said it was a gift of Dionysus,
75	the work of far-famed Hephaestus.
76	In it your white bones lie, shining Achilles,
77	mixed with those of dead Patroclus son of Menoetius,
78	and apart those of Antilochus, whom you honored above all
79	the other companions, after Patroclus had died.
80	Around them then a great and blameless tomb
81	we heaped, the holy army of Argive spearmen,
82	on a jutting headland, on the wide Hellespont,
83	so that it would be seen from far off from the sea by men
84	who are now born and who will be afterward.
85	And your mother, having asked the gods, set very beautiful prizes
86	in the middle of the contest for the best of the Achaeans.
87	You have already met the burial of many men,
88	heroes, whenever a king has died
89	and the young men gird themselves and prepare prizes.
90	But those you would have wondered at most in your heart, seeing them,
91	such very beautiful prizes the goddess set down for you,
92	silver-footed Thetis. For you were very dear to the gods.
93	So you, even having died, have not lost your name, but always
94	over all humans there will be noble fame for you, Achilles.
95	But for me what is this pleasure, since I wound up the war?
96	For in the homecoming Zeus planned a ruinous destruction for me
97	under the hands of Aegisthus and my accursed wife.
> (narrator)
98	So they were saying such things to each other,
99	and near them came the guide, slayer of Argus,
100	leading down the souls of the suitors overcome by Odysseus.
101	The two of them, astonished, went straight, when they saw them.
102	And the soul of Agamemnon son of Atreus recognized
103	the dear son of Melaneus, far-famed Amphimedon.
104	For he was a guest-friend to him, living in a house in Ithaca.
105	The soul of the son of Atreus spoke to him first:
> Agamemnon
106	Amphimedon, what did you suffer that you went down into the dark earth,
107	all chosen and of the same age? Not otherwise
108	picking would one pick the best men through the city.
109	Did Poseidon overcome you in ships,
110	having raised hard winds and long waves?
111	Or did hostile men harm you on the dry land
112	as you were cutting off cattle and beautiful flocks of sheep,
113	or fighting around a city and women?
114	Tell me asking. I claim to be your guest-friend.
115	Do you not remember when I came down there to your house,
116	urging Odysseus with godlike Menelaus
117	to follow together to Ilium on well-benched ships?
118	And we crossed the whole wide sea in a whole month,
119	having barely persuaded Odysseus the city-sacker.
> (narrator)
120	Then the soul of Amphimedon spoke to him:
> Amphimedon
121	Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon lord of men,
122	I remember all these things, Zeus-nurtured, as you say.
123	And I will tell you all very well and exactly,
124	the bad end of our death, how it happened.
125	We were courting the wife of Odysseus, gone a long time.
126	She neither refused the hateful marriage nor finished it,
127	planning death and black fate for us,
128	but she devised this other trick in her mind:
129	setting up a great loom in the halls she wove
130	a web fine and very wide. And at once she said to us:
131	Young men, my suitors, since shining Odysseus has died,
132	wait, though you are eager for my marriage, until I finish the cloth,
133	so that my spinning is not wasted and lost,
134	a burial cloth for the hero Laertes, for when
135	the ruinous fate of long-sorrowing death takes him down,
136	so that no one among the Achaean women in the district will be angry at me
137	if he who won so much should lie without a winding-sheet.
138	So she spoke, and our proud heart was persuaded.
139	Then by day she would weave the great web,
140	and at night she would unravel it, when she had set torches beside her.
141	So for three years she escaped notice by the trick and persuaded the Achaeans.
142	But when the fourth year came and the seasons came on,
143	as the months were waning, and many days were finished around it,
144	then one of the women spoke, who knew it clearly,
145	and we caught her unraveling the shining web.
146	So she finished it, though she did not want to, under compulsion.
147	When she showed the cloth, having woven the great web,
148	having washed it, like the sun or the moon,
149	then a bad god from somewhere brought Odysseus
150	to the edge of the country, where the swineherd lived in a house.
151	There the dear son of godlike Odysseus came,
152	coming from sandy Pylos with a black ship.
153	The two of them, preparing a bad death for the suitors,
154	reached the far-famed town. Odysseus
155	was later, and Telemachus was leading in front.
156	And the swineherd was leading him, having bad clothes on his skin,
157	like a wretched beggar and an old man
158	leaning on a stick. And he had worn miserable clothes on his skin.
159	And not one of us was able to know him being that man
160	suddenly appearing, not even those who were older,
161	but we were assailing him with bad words and with throws.
162	But he for a while endured in his own halls
163	being thrown at and assailed, with an enduring heart.
164	But when the mind of Zeus who holds the aegis woke him,
165	with Telemachus he lifted the very beautiful armor
166	and put it down in the room and locked the bars,
167	and by many-profit devices he bid his own wife
168	set the bow for the suitors and the grey iron,
169	for us of terrible fate, prizes and the beginning of slaughter.
170	And not one of us was able of the strong bow
171	to string the string, and we were far lacking.
172	But when the great bow of Odysseus reached his hands,
173	then we all were shouting with words
174	not to give the bow, not even if he said very many things.
175	But Telemachus alone urging him ordered it.
176	But he took it in his hand, much-enduring shining Odysseus,
177	and easily he strung the bow, and he sent it through the iron,
178	and he stood going onto the threshold, and he poured out the swift arrows
179	looking around terribly, and he hit Antinous the king.
180	Then he was sending groaning missiles at the others,
181	aiming opposite. And they were falling close together.
182	And it was known that some one of the gods was a helper to him.
183	For at once through the house, following with their own fury,
184	they were killing turning this way and that, and an unseemly groan rose
185	of heads being struck, and the whole floor was seething with blood.
186	So we, Agamemnon, perished, whose even now
187	bodies lie uncared-for in the halls of Odysseus.
188	For the dear ones do not yet know, through the house of each,
189	who would wash the black gore from the wounds
190	and laying them down would lament. For that is the gift of honor of the dead.
> (narrator)
191	Then the soul of the son of Atreus spoke to him:
> Agamemnon
192	Blessed son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
193	truly with great excellence you got a wife.
194	How good the wits were in blameless Penelope,
195	daughter of Icarius. How well she remembered Odysseus,
196	her wedded husband. Therefore her fame will never perish
197	of her excellence, and the deathless will make a graceful song
198	for the earth-dwellers, for careful Penelope,
199	not as the daughter of Tyndareus planned bad works,
200	killing her wedded husband, and a hateful song
201	will be upon humans, and she will give a hard report
202	to female women, even one who is a good-worker.
> (narrator)
203	So they were saying such things to each other,
204	standing in the house of Hades, under the hiding-places of the earth.
205	And they, when they had gone down from the city, soon reached the field,
206	the beautiful well-worked field of Laertes, which he himself once
207	Laertes won, after he labored very many things.
208	There his house was, and around it a shed ran all the way,
209	in which they used to eat and sit and sleep,
210	slaves under compulsion, who worked dear things for him.
211	And in it there was an old Sicilian woman, who used to tend the old man
212	kindly in the country, apart from the city.
213	There Odysseus spoke a word to the slaves and to his son:
> Odysseus
214	You now go into the well-built house,
215	and at once sacrifice for dinner whichever of the pigs is best.
216	But I will test our father,
217	whether he will recognize me and mark me with his eyes,
218	or fail to know me, being apart a long time.
> (narrator)
219	Speaking so he gave the slaves the warlike armor.
220	They then went quickly toward the house, and Odysseus
221	went nearer the fruit-rich orchard, testing.
222	And he did not find Dolius, going down into the great orchard,
223	nor any of the slaves nor of the sons. But they
224	had gone to gather stones to be a fence of the orchard,
225	and the old man was leading the way for them.
226	And he found his father alone in the well-built orchard,
227	hoeing a plant. And he was wearing a dirty tunic,
228	stitched, unseemly, and around his shins oxhide
229	stitched greaves he had bound, avoiding scratches,
230	and gloves on his hands because of the brambles. And above
231	he had a goatskin cap on his head, nursing his grief.
232	When much-enduring shining Odysseus marked him
233	worn by old age, and having great grief in his mind,
234	standing under a tall pear he was shedding a tear down.
235	Then he considered in his mind and in his heart
236	whether to kiss and wrap himself around his own father, and tell each thing,
237	how he had come and reached the fatherland earth,
238	or first question him and test each thing.
239	And as he was thinking this seemed more profitable to him,
240	first to test him with cutting words.
241	Thinking these things shining Odysseus went straight to him.
242	He was holding his head down, digging around a plant.
243	And standing beside him the shining son spoke to him:
> Odysseus
244	Old man, it is not lack of skill that holds you in tending
245	the orchard, but the care is good for you, and not at all
246	not a plant, not a fig, not a vine, not an olive,
247	not a pear, not a garden-bed of yours is without care through the garden.
248	But I will tell you something else, and do not put anger in your heart:
249	you yourself do not have good care, but together with ruinous old age
250	you have it, and you are badly dirty and you wear unseemly things.
251	It is not at least for idleness that your lord does not tend you,
252	and nothing slavish is fitting to look at in you,
253	form and size. For you are like a king man.
254	And you are like such a one, when he has washed and eaten,
255	to sleep softly. For that is the way of old men.
256	But come, tell me this and recount it exactly:
257	Whose slave of men are you? Whose orchard are you tending?
258	And tell me this genuine, so I may know well,
259	if truly we have reached this Ithaca, as a man told me,
260	this man just now meeting me as I was coming here,
261	not very right-minded, since he did not dare each thing
262	to say and to hear my word, as I was asking
263	about my guest-friend, whether he lives somewhere and is,
264	or has already died and is in the house of Hades.
265	For I will tell you out, and you put it together and hear me.
266	I once hosted a man in my own dear fatherland earth
267	coming to our house, and not yet has any other mortal
268	of far-away guest-friends come more welcome to my house.
269	He claimed his race was from Ithaca, and he said
270	Laertes son of Arceisius was his father.
271	Him I leading to the house hosted well,
272	loving him kindly, though many were in the house,
273	and I gave him guest-gifts, such as were fitting.
274	Of well-worked gold I gave him seven talents,
275	and I gave him an all-silver mixing-bowl, flowered,
276	and twelve single cloaks, and as many rugs,
277	and as many beautiful cloths, and as many tunics on top of those,
278	and apart again women, knowing blameless works,
279	four good-looking ones, whom he himself wanted to choose.
> (narrator)
280	Then the father answered him, shedding a tear down:
> Laertes
281	Stranger, you have reached the land you are asking about,
282	but violent and reckless men hold it.
283	And these gifts you were granting empty, giving countless things.
284	For if you had found him living in the people of Ithaca,
285	then he would have sent you well, paying you back with gifts
286	and with good guest-friendship. For that is the ordinance, whoever starts it.
287	But come, tell me this and recount it exactly:
288	what number year is it, when you hosted that man,
289	your miserable guest-friend, my child, if ever he was,
290	ill-fated? Whom somewhere far from dear ones and the fatherland earth
291	either somewhere in the sea fish ate, or on the dry land
292	he became spoil for beasts and birds. And his mother did not
293	weep wrapping him, nor his father, we who bore him.
294	Nor his wife of many gifts, careful Penelope,
295	wailed over her own husband on a bed, as was fitting,
296	having closed his eyes. For that is the gift of honor of the dead.
297	And tell me this genuine, so I may know well:
298	Who are you and from where of men? Where is your city and your parents?
299	And where does the swift ship stand, which brought you here
300	and your godlike companions? Or have you come as a trader
301	on another's ship, and they, having gone out, went on?
> (narrator)
302	Odysseus of many devices spoke answering him:
> Odysseus
303	Then I will tell you all very exactly.
304	I am from Alybas, where I live in a famous house,
305	son of Apheidas son of Polypemon the lord.
306	And my name is Eperitus. But a god
307	drove me wandering from Sicania to come here, not wishing it.
308	And this ship of mine stands in the country apart from the city.
309	And for Odysseus this is the fifth year,
310	since he went from there and has left my fatherland,
311	ill-fated. Truly there were good birds for him going,
312	on the right, rejoicing at which I was sending that man,
313	and he was glad going. And our heart still hoped
314	to meet in guest-friendship and to give shining gifts.
> (narrator)
315	So he spoke, and a black cloud of grief covered him.
316	And with both hands taking ashy dust
317	he poured it down over his grey head, groaning thickly.
318	And his heart was stirred, and already up through his nostrils
319	a sharp fury rushed, looking at his own father.
320	And leaping on him he kissed him wrapping around him, and spoke to him:
> Odysseus
321	That man is this I myself, father, whom you are asking after.
322	I have come in the twentieth year to the fatherland earth.
323	But hold from weeping and tearful lament.
324	For I will tell you out. And we must hurry all the same.
325	I have killed the suitors in our house,
326	paying the heart-painful outrage and the bad works.
> (narrator)
327	Then Laertes answered him and spoke:
> Laertes
328	If truly you are Odysseus my child reaching here,
329	tell me now some very clear sign, so I may trust.
> (narrator)
330	Odysseus of many devices spoke answering him:
> Odysseus
331	First mark this scar with your eyes,
332	which a boar drove into me on Parnassus with a white tooth
333	when I went. And you were sending me, and my lady mother,
334	to the father Autolycus, dear to my mother, so I might take
335	gifts, which coming here he promised me and nodded yes.
336	But come, I will also tell you the trees through the well-built orchard
337	which you once gave me, and I asked you for each
338	being a child, following through the garden. And through them
339	we were going, and you named them and said each.
340	You gave me thirteen pears and ten apple-trees,
341	and forty fig-trees. And rows of vines you named for me thus
342	you would give fifty, and each was bearing through the harvest;
343	there clusters of all kinds are on them,
344	whenever the seasons of Zeus would weigh down from above.
> (narrator)
345	So he spoke, and his knees and his own heart were loosened right there,
346	recognizing the sure signs that Odysseus had shown him.
347	And he threw his arms around his own dear child. And to himself
348	much-enduring shining Odysseus drew him, fainting.
349	But when he had breathed again and the heart was gathered into the mind,
350	again answering with words he spoke to him:
> Laertes
351	Father Zeus, truly you gods still are on long Olympus,
352	if truly the suitors have paid their reckless violence.
353	But now I am terribly afraid in my mind that soon all
354	the Ithacans will come on us here, and messages
355	they will send everywhere to the cities of the Cephallenians.
> (narrator)
356	Odysseus of many devices spoke answering him:
> Odysseus
357	Take heart, let these things not be a care in your mind.
358	But let us go toward the house, which lies near the orchard.
359	And there Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd
360	I sent ahead, so they might make dinner ready as quickly as possible.
> (narrator)
361	Speaking so the two of them went toward the beautiful house.
362	And when they reached the well-lived house,
363	they found Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd
364	cutting much meat and mixing flashing wine.
365	Meanwhile great-hearted Laertes in his own house
366	the Sicilian serving woman washed and anointed with oil,
367	and she threw a beautiful cloak around him. And Athena
368	standing near made the limbs thrive for the shepherd of the people,
369	and she made him greater than before and thicker to look at.
370	He stepped from the bathtub. And his own son wondered at him,
371	when he saw him like the deathless gods face to face.
372	And speaking he addressed winged words to him:
> Odysseus
373	Father, truly some one of the gods who are always
374	has made you better in form and size to look at.
> (narrator)
375	Then thoughtful Laertes spoke opposite:
> Laertes
376	If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
377	such as I was when I took Nericus, the well-built city,
378	a headland of the mainland, being lord over the Cephallenians,
379	being such yesterday in our house,
380	having armor on my shoulders, to stand and defend
381	against the suitor men. Then I would have loosened their knees
382	of many in the halls, and you would have been glad in your mind inside.
> (narrator)
383	So they were saying such things to each other.
384	And when they had stopped the labor and made the feast,
385	they sat in order on chairs and thrones.
386	Then they were putting their hands to dinner, and near
387	came the old man Dolius, and with him the sons of that old man,
388	laboring from the work, since going forward the mother had called them,
389	the old Sicilian woman, who reared them and used to tend the old man
390	kindly, since old age had caught him.
391	And when they saw Odysseus and marked him in their heart,
392	they stood in the halls stunned. And Odysseus
393	addressing them with gentle words spoke:
> Odysseus
394	Old man, sit to dinner, and forget your astonishment.
395	For a long time eager to put our hands to food
396	we have been waiting in the halls, always expecting you.
> (narrator)
397	So he spoke, and Dolius went straight, spreading both hands,
398	and taking Odysseus he kissed his hand at the wrist,
399	and speaking he addressed winged words to him:
> Dolius
400	Dear one, since you have come home to us who wanted it very much
401	and were no longer expecting it, and the gods themselves have brought you up,
402	be well and be very glad, and may the gods give you blessed things.
403	And tell me this genuine, so I may know well,
404	whether careful Penelope already knows clearly
405	that you have come home here, or should we send a messenger.
> (narrator)
406	Odysseus of many devices spoke answering him:
> Odysseus
407	Old man, she already knows. Why must you labor at these things?
> (narrator)
408	So he spoke, and he sat down again on the well-polished stool.
409	And in the same way the sons of Dolius around famous Odysseus
410	were greeting him with words and growing onto his hands,
411	and they sat in order beside Dolius, their own father.
412	So they were laboring around dinner in the halls.
413	And Rumor as a messenger went quickly through the city everywhere,
414	telling the hateful death and fate of the suitors.
415	And they hearing alike were going, one from one place and one from another,
416	with moaning and groaning, in front of the house of Odysseus.
417	And they were carrying the dead out of the house and each was burying them,
418	and those from other cities, each one home
419	they were sending to take, putting them on swift ships for fishermen.
420	And they themselves went to the assembly in a crowd, grieving at heart.
421	But when they had been gathered and become assembled,
422	Eupeithes stood up among them and spoke.
423	For an unforgettable grief for his child lay in his mind,
424	Antinous, whom shining Odysseus killed first.
425	For him he, pouring a tear, spoke in assembly and said:
> Eupeithes
426	Friends, truly this man has planned a great work against the Achaeans.
427	Those many and good men, leading them with ships,
428	he lost the hollow ships, and he lost the people from them.
429	And these, coming, he killed, by far the best of the Cephallenians.
430	But come, before this man either reaches Pylos quickly
431	or even shining Elis, where the Epeians have power,
432	let us go. Or even afterward we will be downcast always.
433	For this is an outrage even for those who will be to hear,
434	if we do not pay the killers of our children and brothers.
435	It would not be sweet for me in my mind
436	to live, but dying very quickly I would be among the perished.
437	But let us go, lest those men get across first.
> (narrator)
438	So he spoke pouring a tear, and pity took all the Achaeans.
439	And near them came Medon and the divine singer
440	from the halls of Odysseus, since sleep had released them.
441	They stood in the middle. And astonishment took each man.
442	And Medon, knowing thoughtful things, spoke among them:
> Medon
443	Hear me now, Ithacans. For Odysseus
444	did not plan these works against the will of the deathless gods.
445	I myself saw an immortal god, who beside Odysseus
446	was standing near and was like Mentor in all things.
447	And the deathless god at one time in front of Odysseus
448	was appearing, encouraging him, and at another time stirring the suitors
449	he was rushing through the hall. And they were falling close together.
> (narrator)
450	So he spoke, and pale fear was taking them all under.
451	And among them the old hero Halitherses spoke,
452	son of Mastor. For he alone was looking forward and behind.
453	He with good sense spoke in assembly among them and said:
> Halitherses
454	Hear me now, Ithacans, whatever I say.
455	By your own badness, friends, these works have happened.
456	For you did not obey me, nor Mentor shepherd of the people,
457	to stop your children from mindlessness,
458	who did a great work by bad reckless transgressions,
459	cutting down the possessions and dishonoring the wife
460	of a man who was best. And they no longer thought he would come home.
461	And now let it be this way. Obey me as I speak.
