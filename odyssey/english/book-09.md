# The Odyssey: Book IX

> (narrator)
1	And answering him Odysseus of many devices said:
> Odysseus
2	Lord Alcinous, most distinguished of all the people,
3	truly this is a fine thing, to listen to a singer
4	such as this man is, like the gods in his voice.
5	For I say there is no fulfillment more delightful
6	than when good cheer holds through a whole people,
7	and the banqueters throughout the house listen to a singer
8	sitting in order, and beside them the tables are full
9	of bread and meat, and drawing wine from the mixing-bowl
10	the wine-pourer carries it and pours it into the cups.
11	This seems to my mind the finest thing there is.
12	But your heart has turned to ask about my groan-filled cares,
13	so that I may still more mourn and groan.
14	What then shall I tell you first, and what last?
15	since the gods of the sky have given me many cares.
16	Now I will tell my name first, so that you too
17	may know it, and I, having escaped the pitiless day,
18	may be your guest-friend even while I live in a house far away.
19	I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, who by all my tricks
20	am a care to men, and my fame reaches the sky.
21	I live in clear-seen Ithaca. On it there is a mountain,
22	Neriton, leaf-shaking, standing out. And around it islands
23	many lie inhabited, very close to one another,
24	Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus.
25	But Ithaca itself lies low, farthest out in the sea
26	toward the gloom, and the others lie off toward the dawn and the sun,
27	rugged, but a good nurse of young men. I for my part
28	can see nothing sweeter than one's own land.
29	Calypso, shining among goddesses, held me there
30	in her hollow caves, longing for me to be her husband.
31	And in the same way Circe held me back in her halls,
32	the wily Aeaean, longing for me to be her husband.
33	But they never persuaded the heart in my chest.
34	So nothing is sweeter than one's own fatherland and parents,
35	even if someone far away has a rich house
36	and lives in a foreign land, away from his parents.
37	But come, let me tell you also my homecoming of many cares,
38	which Zeus laid on me as I went from Troy.
39	From Ilium a wind carrying me brought me to the Cicones,
40	at Ismarus. There I sacked the city and killed them.
41	Taking from the city their wives and much property
42	we divided it, so that no one would go deprived of his equal share through me.
43	Then I ordered that we flee with a quick foot,
44	but they, greatly child-minded, would not obey.
45	There much wine was drunk, and many sheep
46	they slaughtered by the shore, and shambling crooked-horn cattle.
47	Meanwhile Cicones who had gone called out to other Cicones,
48	who were their neighbors, more numerous and stronger at once,
49	living on the mainland, knowing how from horses
50	to fight with men, and where one must fight on foot.
51	Then they came, as many as leaves and flowers grow in season,
52	early. Then an evil portion of Zeus stood by
53	us of terrible fate, so that we would suffer many pains.
54	They took their stand and fought a battle beside the swift ships,
55	and they threw bronze-tipped spears at one another.
56	As long as it was morning and the holy day was growing,
57	we held them off and stood our ground, though they were more.
58	But when the sun was shifting toward the unyoking of oxen,
59	then the Cicones turned the Achaeans and overpowered them.
60	And six well-greaved companions from each ship
61	died. The rest of us escaped death and doom.
62	From there we sailed on, grieving in our hearts,
63	glad to be out of death, having lost our dear companions.
64	Nor did my curved ships go farther
65	before I had called three times to each of those poor companions
66	who died on the plain, cut down by the Cicones.
67	Then cloud-gatherer Zeus roused the North Wind against the ships
68	in a god-spoken storm, and with clouds he covered
69	earth and sea together. And night rose from the sky.
70	Then the ships were driven sideways, and the sails
71	the force of the wind tore into three and four pieces.
72	We lowered them into the ships, fearing destruction,
73	and we rowed the ships themselves hard toward land.
74	There two nights and two days without a break
75	we lay, eating our hearts with exhaustion and pains together.
76	But when fair-haired Dawn finished the third day,
77	we set up the masts and hoisted the white sails
78	and sat, and the wind and the steersmen kept them straight.
79	And I would have reached my fatherland unharmed.
80	But the wave and the current, as I was rounding Malea,
81	and the North Wind drove me off, and pushed me past Cythera.
82	From there I was carried for nine days by destructive winds
83	over the fish-full sea. And on the tenth we set foot
84	on the land of the Lotus-eaters, who eat a flowery food.
85	There we went onto the mainland and drew water,
86	and at once the companions took their meal beside the swift ships.
87	But when we had tasted of food and of drink,
88	then I sent companions to go and find out
89	what men they were who ate bread on the earth,
90	choosing two men, and sending a third with them as herald.
91	They went at once and mixed with the Lotus-eater men.
92	The Lotus-eaters did not plan destruction for our companions,
93	but they gave them lotus to taste.
94	And whoever of them ate the honey-sweet fruit of the lotus
95	no longer wanted to bring word back or to return,
96	but they wanted to stay there among the Lotus-eater men
97	browsing on lotus and to forget their homecoming.
98	I led them back to the ships, weeping, by force,
99	and dragging them under the benches I bound them in the hollow ships.
100	Then I ordered the rest of my trusty companions
101	to go quickly and board the fast ships,
102	so that no one, eating lotus, would forget his homecoming.
103	They boarded at once and sat down at the oarlocks,
104	and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with the oars.
105	From there we sailed on, grieving in our hearts.
106	And to the land of the Cyclopes, overbearing, without law,
107	we came, who, trusting in the immortal gods,
108	neither plant a crop with their hands nor plow,
109	but all these things grow unsown and unplowed,
110	wheat and barley and vines, which bear
111	wine from rich clusters, and the rain of Zeus makes them grow.
112	They have neither counsel-bearing assemblies nor laws,
113	but they live on the peaks of high mountains
114	in hollow caves, and each one makes law
115	for his children and wives, and they pay no heed to one another.
116	Then a low island is stretched out beside the harbor,
117	neither near the land of the Cyclopes nor far off,
118	wooded. On it countless goats have been born,
119	wild ones. For no path of men drives them off,
120	nor do hunters go onto it, who through the woods
121	suffer pains, ranging the peaks of the mountains.
122	It is held neither by flocks nor by plowlands,
123	but it stays unsown and unplowed all its days,
124	empty of men, and it feeds bleating goats.
125	For the Cyclopes have no red-cheeked ships,
126	nor are there shipwrights among them who would build
127	well-benched ships, which would accomplish all things,
128	coming to the cities of men, as so often
129	men cross the sea to one another in ships.
130	Such men would have made even this island well-settled for them.
131	For it is not a bad island at all, and it would bear all things in season.
132	There are meadows by the banks of the gray sea,
133	watery and soft. The vines would be quite unfailing.
134	And there is smooth plowland. They would always cut a deep crop
135	when the season came, since there is rich fat under the ground.
136	And there is a harbor good for mooring, where there is no need of a cable,
137	nor to throw out anchors nor to fasten stern-cables,
138	but only to beach and wait the time until the sailors'
139	heart urges them and the winds blow.
140	And at the head of the harbor a bright water flows,
141	a spring under a cave, and poplars have grown around it.
142	There we sailed in, and some god was guiding us
143	through the dark night, and it did not show forth to be seen.
144	For a deep mist was around the ships, and the moon
145	did not show forth from the sky, but was held by clouds.
146	Then no one looked on the island with his eyes,
147	nor did we see the long waves rolling toward the dry land
148	before the well-benched ships ran ashore.
149	When the ships had beached we lowered all the sails,
150	and we ourselves also went out onto the breaking edge of the sea.
151	There we fell asleep and waited for bright Dawn.
152	When early-born rose-fingered Dawn appeared,
153	we went about over the island, wondering at it.
154	And the nymphs, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, started up
155	mountain-dwelling goats, so the companions might dine.
156	At once curved bows and long-socketed hunting-spears
157	we took from the ships, and dividing ourselves in three
158	we shot. At once the god gave a hunt to satisfy the heart.
159	Twelve ships followed me, and to each
160	nine goats fell by lot. And they took out ten for me alone.
161	So then all day long until the sun went down
162	we sat feasting on meat beyond measure and sweet wine.
163	For the red wine had not yet been used up from the ships,
164	but it was still in them. For much in the two-handled jars each of us
165	had drawn, when we took the holy city of the Cicones.
166	And we looked toward the land of the Cyclopes, who were near,
167	their smoke and the voices of them and of sheep and goats.
168	When the sun went down and dusk came on,
169	then we lay down to sleep on the breaking edge of the sea.
170	When early-born rose-fingered Dawn appeared,
171	then I called an assembly and spoke among them all:
172	The rest of you stay here now, my trusty companions.
173	But I with my ship and my companions
174	will go and make trial of these men, who they are,
175	whether they are violent and wild and not just,
176	or friendly to strangers, and their mind is god-fearing.
177	So saying I went up onto the ship, and I ordered the companions
178	to go aboard themselves and to loose the stern-cables.
179	They boarded at once and sat down at the oarlocks,
180	and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with the oars.
181	But when we reached the place that was near,
182	there at the edge of the land we saw a cave close to the sea,
183	high, roofed over with laurels. And there many
184	flocks, both sheep and goats, used to sleep. Around it a yard
185	had been built high, with stones set deep in the ground
186	and with tall pines and high-foliaged oaks.
187	And there a huge man used to sleep, who the flocks
188	used to shepherd alone, far off. He did not go among others,
189	but lived apart, and he knew lawless things.
190	For he had been made a huge wonder, and he was not like
191	a bread-eating man, but like a wooded peak
192	of high mountains, that shows alone apart from the others.
193	Then I ordered the others, the trusty companions,
194	to stay there by the ship and to guard the ship,
195	but I picked twelve of the best of the companions
196	and went. I had a goatskin bag of dark wine,
197	sweet, which Maron gave me, the son of Euanthes,
198	priest of Apollo, who watched over Ismarus,
199	because we protected him with his child and his wife,
200	in reverence. For he lived in a wooded grove
201	of Phoebus Apollo. And he gave me shining gifts.
202	He gave me seven talents of well-worked gold,
203	and he gave me a mixing-bowl all of silver, and then
204	wine in two-handled jars, having drawn it into all twelve,
205	sweet, unmixed, a divine drink. No one
206	of the slaves or of the serving-women in the house knew it,
207	but only he himself and his dear wife and one housekeeper.
208	And when they would drink that honey-sweet red wine,
209	he would fill one cup and pour twenty measures of water,
210	and a sweet smell would rise from the mixing-bowl,
211	a wondrous one. Then it would not be dear to you to hold back from it.
212	Of this I carried a great skin filled, and provisions too
213	in a bag. For at once my proud heart suspected
214	that a man would come upon us, clothed in great strength,
215	a wild one, who knew well neither judgments nor laws.
216	Quickly we came to the cave, and we did not find him inside,
217	but he was herding fat flocks through the pasture.
218	Coming into the cave we gazed at each thing.
219	The racks were heavy with cheeses, and the pens were crowded
220	with lambs and kids. Separated, each kind
221	were shut in, apart the first-born, and apart the later-born,
222	and apart again the newborns. All the vessels were swimming with whey,
223	both pails and bowls, well-made, that he milked into.
224	Then first of all the companions begged me with words
225	to take the cheeses and go back, and then
226	quickly drive the kids and the lambs to the fast ship
227	out of the pens, and sail over the salt water.
228	But I would not obey, and it would have been far more profitable,
229	so that I might see him, and whether he would give me guest-gifts.
230	But he was not going to be a delight to the companions when he appeared.
231	Then we kindled a fire and made offerings, and we ourselves also
232	took cheeses and ate, and we waited for him inside,
233	sitting, until he came herding. He carried a huge load
234	of dry wood, so it would serve for his evening meal,
235	and throwing it inside the cave he made a crash.
236	And we in fear rushed back to the inmost part of the cave.
237	But he drove into the wide cave the fat flocks,
238	all that he milked, and he left the males outside the door,
239	rams and he-goats, outside the deep yard.
240	Then he set a great door-stone in place, lifting it high,
241	a massive one. Not even twenty-two wagons,
242	good four-wheeled ones, could have levered it from the ground —
243	such a towering rock he set against the doors.
244	Sitting down he milked the ewes and the bleating goats,
245	all in due order, and put a young one under each.
246	At once, curdling half of the white milk,
247	he gathered it and set it down in wicker baskets,
248	and the other half he stood in vessels, so it would be there
249	for him to take and drink, and so he would have it for supper.
250	But when he had hurried through and finished his work,
251	then he lit a fire and saw us, and asked us:
> Polyphemus
252	Strangers, who are you? From where do you sail the wet paths?
253	Is it on some business, or do you wander at random
254	as pirates do, over the sea, who roam
255	staking their lives, bringing evil to men of other lands?
> (narrator)
256	So he spoke, and our own heart was broken in us,
257	fearing the heavy voice and the monster himself.
258	But even so I answered him with words and said:
> Odysseus
259	We are Achaeans, driven off course from Troy
260	by winds of every kind over the great gulf of the sea,
261	longing for home. Another road, other paths
262	we came by. So I suppose Zeus wished to plan it.
263	We claim to be the people of Atreus's son Agamemnon,
264	whose glory now is the greatest under heaven.
265	For he sacked so great a city and destroyed many
266	people. And we, in turn, coming to your knees
267	have come, if you would give some guest-gift, or otherwise
268	grant a present, which is the right of strangers.
269	But have regard for the gods, best of men. We are your suppliants.
270	Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and of strangers,
271	Zeus of strangers, who walks with respected strangers.
> (narrator)
272	So I spoke, and he answered me at once with a pitiless heart:
> Polyphemus
273	You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away,
274	you who tell me to fear the gods or to avoid them.
275	For the Cyclopes do not care about aegis-bearing Zeus
276	or the blessed gods, since we are far better.
277	I would not spare you or your companions to avoid
278	the hatred of Zeus, unless my own heart told me to.
279	But tell me where you put your well-made ship when you came,
280	whether at the edge of the land, or near, so I may know.
> (narrator)
281	So he spoke, testing me, but he did not fool me, who knew many things.
282	I spoke back to him with crafty words:
> Odysseus
283	Poseidon the earth-shaker smashed my ship
284	throwing it on the rocks at the edge of your land,
285	driving it on a headland. A wind brought it from the open sea.
286	I, with these men, escaped sheer destruction.
> (narrator)
287	So I spoke, and he answered me nothing with his pitiless heart,
288	but springing up he reached his hands at my companions,
289	and seizing two together, like puppies, he dashed them
290	to the ground. The brain flowed on the floor and soaked the earth.
291	Cutting them up limb from limb he made his supper ready.
292	He ate like a mountain-bred lion, and he left nothing,
293	the innards and the flesh and the marrow-filled bones.
294	We, weeping, held up our hands to Zeus,
295	watching the cruel work, and helplessness held our hearts.
296	But when the Cyclops had filled his great belly
297	eating the man's flesh and drinking the unmixed milk on it,
298	he lay down inside the cave, stretched out among the sheep.
299	I planned in my great-hearted spirit
300	to go closer, drawing the sharp sword from beside my thigh,
301	and stab him in the chest, where the midriff holds the liver,
302	feeling for the place with my hand. But another thought held me back.
303	For there we too would have died sheer destruction.
304	We would not have been able, from the high doors,
305	to push away with our hands the massive stone he had set there.
306	So then, groaning, we waited for bright Dawn.
307	When early-born Dawn appeared, rose-fingered,
308	then he lit a fire and milked his famous flocks,
309	all in due order, and put a young one under each.
310	But when he had hurried through and finished his work,
311	he seized two together again and made his meal ready.
312	When he had eaten he drove the fat flocks out of the cave,
313	easily taking away the great door-stone. And then
314	he set it back, as a man would set a lid on a quiver.
315	With a great whistling he turned the fat flocks toward the mountain,
316	the Cyclops. I was left behind, plotting evils deep down,
317	if somehow I might pay him back, and Athena give me the boast.
318	And this plan seemed best to me in my heart.
319	For a great club of the Cyclops lay beside the pen,
320	green olive-wood. He had cut it so he might carry it
321	when it was dry. We looking at it thought it
322	as big as the mast of a black twenty-oared ship,
323	a wide freighter, that crosses the great gulf.
324	So great it was in length, so great in thickness to look at.
325	I stood by and cut off about a fathom of it
326	and set it down for my companions, and told them to shave it smooth.
327	They made it even. I stood by and sharpened
328	the tip, and at once taking it I charred it in the blazing fire.
329	Then I hid it well, burying it under the dung
330	that was piled in the cave in great heaps.
331	I told the others to cast lots,
332	who would dare, lifting the stake with me,
333	to grind it in his eye, when sweet sleep came on him.
334	The lot fell on those I myself would have chosen,
335	four men, and I was numbered fifth among them.
336	In the evening he came, herding his beautiful-haired flocks.
337	At once he drove the fat flocks into the wide cave,
338	all of them. He left nothing outside the deep yard,
339	whether he suspected something, or a god told him so.
340	Then he set the great door-stone in place, lifting it high,
341	and sitting down he milked the ewes and the bleating goats,
342	all in due order, and put a young one under each.
343	But when he had hurried through and finished his work,
344	he seized two together again and made his supper ready.
345	And then I spoke to the Cyclops, standing close by,
346	holding in my hands a bowl of dark wine:
> Odysseus
347	Cyclops, here, drink wine, since you have eaten man's flesh,
348	so you may know what kind of drink this was that our ship
349	was hiding. I was bringing it as a drink-offering for you, if you would pity me
350	and send me home. You are raging past what anyone can bear.
351	Cruel man, how would anyone else of the many men
352	come to you later, since you have not acted in the right way?
> (narrator)
353	So I spoke, and he took it and drank it down. He was terribly pleased
354	drinking the sweet drink, and he asked me for it a second time:
> Polyphemus
355	Give me more, readily, and tell me your name
356	right now, so I may give you a guest-gift that you will enjoy.
357	For the grain-giving earth bears for the Cyclopes
358	wine from good grapes, and the rain of Zeus makes it grow.
359	But this is a drip of ambrosia and nectar.
> (narrator)
360	So he spoke, and I gave him the flashing wine again.
361	Three times I brought it and gave it. Three times he drank it in his mindlessness.
362	But when the wine had gone around the Cyclops's wits,
363	then I spoke to him with honeyed words:
> Odysseus
364	Cyclops, you ask me my famous name. I will
365	tell you. Give me the guest-gift, as you promised.
366	Nobody is my name. They call me Nobody,
367	my mother and my father and all my other companions.
> (narrator)
368	So I spoke, and he answered me at once with a pitiless heart:
> Polyphemus
369	Nobody I will eat last among his companions,
370	and the others first. That will be your guest-gift.
> (narrator)
371	He spoke, and leaning back he fell sprawling, and then
372	he lay there, turning his thick neck to one side, and sleep
373	that tames all took him. Wine rushed from his throat
374	and bits of man. He belched, heavy with wine.
375	Then I drove the stake under the deep ash
376	until it grew hot, and with words I cheered all my companions
377	so that no one would hang back from me in fear.
378	But when the olive stake in the fire was just about
379	to catch, green as it was, and it glowed terribly,
380	then I brought it closer from the fire, and my companions
381	stood around. A god breathed great courage into us.
382	They took the olive stake, sharp at the tip,
383	and rammed it in the eye. I from above, leaning on it,
384	spun it, as when a man bores a ship-timber
385	with a drill, and others below keep it spinning with a strap
386	they have taken hold of on either side, and it runs without stopping.
387	So we took the fire-pointed stake in his eye
388	and spun it, and the blood flowed around it, hot as it was.
389	The breath of the burning pupil singed all his eyelids
390	and his brows, and the roots crackled in the fire.
391	As when a smith dips a great axe or an adze
392	in cold water, and it hisses loud
393	as he tempers it — for that is the strength of iron —
394	so his eye sizzled around the olive stake.
395	He gave a great terrible cry, and the rock rang around,
396	and we, afraid, rushed back. He pulled the stake
397	out of his eye, fouled with much blood.
398	Then he threw it from him with his hands, raving,
399	and he shouted loud to the Cyclopes, who lived around him
400	in caves along the windy ridges.
401	They, hearing the cry, came from every side,
402	and standing around the cave they asked what was hurting him:
> Cyclopes
403	Why so much, Polyphemus, have you cried out in pain like this
404	through the immortal night, and kept us sleepless?
405	Is someone of mortal men driving your flocks against your will?
406	Is someone killing you yourself by craft or by force?
> (narrator)
407	Strong Polyphemus spoke to them from the cave:
> Polyphemus
408	Friends, Nobody is killing me by craft, not by force.
> (narrator)
409	They answered him and spoke winged words:
> Cyclopes
410	If nobody is doing you violence, and you are alone,
411	there is no way to avoid a sickness of great Zeus.
412	But you pray to your father, lord Poseidon.
> (narrator)
413	So they spoke and went away, and my own heart laughed
414	at how my name had deceived them, and my faultless cunning.
415	The Cyclops, groaning and writhing in pains,
416	groping with his hands, took the stone from the doors,
417	and sat in the doorway himself, spreading out his hands,
418	to catch anyone walking out the door among the sheep.
419	He hoped in his mind I would be such a fool.
420	I was planning how it would turn out best,
421	if I could find some release from death for my companions and myself.
422	I was weaving all kinds of crafts and cunning
423	as if for our lives. For a great evil was close.
424	And this plan seemed best to me in my heart.
425	There were well-fed rams, thick-fleeced,
426	handsome and large, with wool dark as violets.
427	I bound them quietly with well-twisted withies
428	on which the Cyclops, the monster who knew lawless things, used to sleep,
429	taking them in threes. The one in the middle carried a man,
430	and the other two went on either side, saving my companions.
431	Three sheep carried each man. I myself —
432	for there was a ram, by far the best of all the flocks —
433	taking hold of his back, curled under his shaggy belly
434	and lay there. With my hands in the godlike wool
435	I held on without letting go, twisted, with an enduring heart.
436	So then, groaning, we waited for bright Dawn.
437	When early-born Dawn appeared, rose-fingered,
438	then the male flocks rushed out to the pasture,
439	and the females bleated, unmilked, around the pens.
440	For their udders were bursting. The master, in his bad pains,
441	worn down, felt along the backs of all the sheep
442	as they stood upright. The fool did not notice
443	that they were bound under the breasts of the woolly sheep.
444	Last of all the ram of the flock walked out the door,
445	weighted with his wool and with me, thinking closely.
446	Feeling him, strong Polyphemus spoke:
> Polyphemus
447	Dear ram, why do you rush out of the cave like this
448	last of the flock? Before this you were never left behind
449	when the sheep went. You graze first the tender flowers of the grass,
450	striding far, and you reach the streams of the rivers first,
451	and you are first to long to go back to the fold
452	in the evening. Now you are last of all. Do you miss
453	your master's eye, which a bad man blinded
454	with his wretched companions, after he tamed my wits with wine,
455	Nobody, who I say has not yet escaped destruction.
456	If only you would think with me and become able to speak
457	and say where that man is skulking from my force —
458	then his brain would be dashed through the cave this way and that
459	as I struck him on the ground, and my heart
460	would be lightened of the evils that worthless Nobody gave me.
> (narrator)
461	So speaking he sent the ram from him out the door.
462	When we had gone a little way from the cave and the yard
463	I first loosed myself from under the ram, and I loosed my companions.
464	Quickly we drove the long-legged sheep, fat with tallow,
465	turning them about often, until we reached the ship.
466	We were a welcome sight to our dear companions,
467	we who had escaped death. They groaned, mourning the others.
468	But I would not let them. I nodded to each man with my brows
469	not to weep, and I told them to throw the beautiful-haired sheep
470	quickly, many of them, into the ship, and sail the salt water.
471	They went aboard at once and sat down at the benches,
472	and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with the oars.
