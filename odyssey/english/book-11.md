# Odyssey Book 11

> Odysseus
1	But when we had gone down to the ship and the sea,
2	we first hauled the ship into the bright salt water,
3	and we set the mast and the sails in the black ship,
4	and taking the sheep we put them aboard, and we ourselves
5	went aboard, grieving, shedding thick tears.
6	For us, behind the dark-prowed ship,
7	she sent a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion,
8	fair-haired Circe, a dread goddess with a human voice.
9	We made each piece of tackle ready through the ship
10	and sat. The wind and the steersman kept her straight.
11	All day long the sail was stretched as she crossed the sea.
12	The sun went down and all the ways grew dark.
13	She reached the limits of deep-flowing Ocean.
14	There is the people and the city of Cimmerian men,
15	hidden in mist and cloud. Never does
16	the shining sun look down on them with his rays,
17	neither when he climbs toward the starry sky
18	nor when he turns back from the sky toward the earth,
19	but deadly night is stretched over poor mortals.
20	Coming there we beached the ship, and we took out
21	the sheep. We ourselves went along the stream of Ocean
22	until we reached the place Circe had marked.
23	There Perimedes and Eurylochus held the victims.
24	I, drawing the sharp sword from beside my thigh,
25	dug a pit, about a cubit this way and that,
26	and around it I poured a drink-offering to all the dead,
27	first with honey-milk, then with sweet wine,
28	and third with water. I sprinkled white barley on it.
29	I prayed much to the strengthless heads of the dead,
30	that when I came to Ithaca I would sacrifice a barren cow, the best,
31	in the halls and fill a pyre with good things,
32	and for Teiresias apart I would sacrifice a ram,
33	all-black, that stands out among our flocks.
34	When I had begged the tribes of the dead with vows and prayers,
35	taking the sheep I cut their throats
36	into the pit, and the dark-clouded blood ran.
37	The souls of the dead who have died gathered from under Erebus.
38	Brides and young men and much-enduring old men
39	and tender girls with a heart still new to grief,
40	and many wounded with bronze-tipped spears,
41	men killed in war, wearing bloodied armor.
42	These many came around the pit from this side and that
43	with an unearthly cry. Pale fear took me.
44	Then I urged my companions and told them
45	to flay the sheep that lay there, slaughtered with the pitiless bronze,
46	and to burn them, and to pray to the gods,
47	to strong Hades and dread Persephone.
48	I myself, drawing the sharp sword from beside my thigh,
49	sat, and I did not let the strengthless heads of the dead
50	come near the blood before I learned from Teiresias.
51	The first soul to come was my companion Elpenor.
52	He had not yet been buried under the wide-wayed earth.
53	We had left his body in Circe's hall
54	unwept and unburied, since another work pressed us.
55	I wept when I saw him, and I pitied him in my heart,
56	and speaking I said winged words to him:
57	Elpenor, how did you come under the misty dark?
58	You got here on foot faster than I did with the black ship.
> (narrator)
59	So I spoke, and he groaned and answered me with a word:
> Elpenor
60	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
61	a bad lot from a god and wine beyond measure ruined me.
62	Lying down in Circe's hall I did not think
63	to go back down the long ladder,
64	but I fell straight down from the roof. My neck
65	broke from the vertebrae, and my soul went down to Hades.
66	Now I beg you by those left behind, who are not here,
67	by your wife and by your father, who raised you when you were small,
68	and by Telemachus, whom you left alone in the halls.
69	I know that going from here, out of the house of Hades,
70	you will beach your well-made ship on the island of Aeaea.
71	There then, lord, I bid you remember me.
72	Do not go on and leave me behind unwept, unburied,
73	turning away, or I may become some wrath of the gods for you.
74	But burn me with the armor I have,
75	and heap a mound for me on the gray shore of the sea,
76	of a poor man, for those who will come to hear of it.
77	Do these things for me, and plant on the tomb the oar
78	I rowed with when I was alive, among my companions.
> (narrator)
79	So he spoke, and I answered him:
> Odysseus
80	These things, poor man, I will finish and I will do.
> (narrator)
81	So the two of us sat answering each other with hateful words,
82	I holding my sword over the blood apart,
83	and the image of my companion spoke much from the other side.
84	Then the soul of my dead mother came,
85	Anticleia, daughter of great-hearted Autolycus,
86	whom I had left alive when I went to holy Ilium.
87	I wept when I saw her, and I pitied her in my heart.
88	Even so I would not let her, though I was in thick grief,
89	come near the blood before I learned from Teiresias.
90	Then the soul of Theban Teiresias came,
91	holding a gold staff. He knew me and spoke:
> Teiresias
92	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
93	why now, poor man, leaving the light of the sun,
94	have you come, to see the dead and this joyless place?
95	But stand back from the pit, hold off the sharp sword,
96	so I may drink the blood and tell you things that do not miss.
> (narrator)
97	So he spoke, and I drew back and sheathed the silver-studded sword
98	in its scabbard. When he had drunk the dark blood,
99	then the faultless seer spoke to me with words:
> Teiresias
100	You are seeking a honey-sweet homecoming, shining Odysseus.
101	A god will make it hard for you. I do not think
102	you will escape the earth-shaker, who has put anger in his heart
103	in his rage that you blinded his dear son.
104	Even so you may still reach home, though you suffer evils,
105	if you are willing to hold back your own heart and your companions',
106	when you first bring the well-made ship near
107	the island of Thrinacia, escaping the violet sea,
108	and you find the cattle and the fat sheep grazing
109	of Helios, who looks on all things and hears all things.
110	If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming,
111	you may still reach Ithaca, though you suffer evils.
112	If you harm them, then I mark destruction for you,
113	for the ship and the companions. If you yourself should escape,
114	you will come late and badly, having lost all your companions,
115	on another man's ship. You will find pains in the house,
116	overbearing men, who eat up your livelihood
117	courting your godlike wife and giving bride-gifts.
118	But you will pay back their violence when you come.
119	Then when you have killed the suitors in your halls,
120	whether by a trick or openly with the sharp bronze,
121	go then, taking a well-fitted oar,
122	until you reach men who do not know the sea
123	and do not eat food mixed with salt.
124	They do not know ships with crimson cheeks
125	or well-fitted oars, which are wings for ships.
126	I will tell you a sign, very clear, and it will not escape you.
127	When another traveler, meeting you,
128	says you have a winnowing-fan on your shining shoulder,
129	then plant the well-fitted oar in the earth
130	and make fine offerings to lord Poseidon,
131	a ram and a bull and a breeding boar,
132	and go home and offer holy hecatombs
133	to the deathless gods who hold the wide sky,
134	to all of them in order. Death will come to you yourself from the sea,
135	a very gentle one, the kind that will kill you
136	worn out in sleek old age. Around you the people
137	will be blessed. I tell you things that do not miss.
> (narrator)
138	So he spoke, and I answered him:
> Odysseus
139	Teiresias, these things the gods themselves have spun, I suppose.
140	But come, tell me this and say it exactly.
141	I see here the soul of my dead mother.
142	She sits silent near the blood, and she has not
143	dared to look her own son in the face or speak to him.
144	Tell me, lord, how she might know I am the one I am.
> (narrator)
145	So I spoke, and he answered me at once:
> Teiresias
146	I will tell you an easy word and put it in your mind.
147	Whomever of the dead who have died you let
148	come near the blood, he will tell you what does not miss.
149	Whomever you grudge, he will go back again.
> (narrator)
150	So speaking, the soul of lord Teiresias went into the house of Hades
151	when he had said his oracles.
152	I stayed right there, until my mother
153	came and drank the dark-clouded blood. At once she knew me,
154	and lamenting she said winged words to me:
> Anticleia
155	My child, how did you come under the misty dark
156	while you are alive? These things are hard for the living to see.
157	For in the middle are great rivers and terrible streams,
158	Ocean first, which there is no way to cross
159	on foot, unless someone has a well-made ship.
160	Have you now come here wandering from Troy
161	with your ship and companions for a long time? Have you not yet
162	reached Ithaca, and have you not seen your wife in the halls?
> (narrator)
163	So she spoke, and I answered her:
> Odysseus
164	Mother of mine, need brought me down to Hades
165	to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias.
166	I have not yet come near Achaea, and I have not yet
167	set foot on our land, but I have always wandered holding misery,
168	from the first moment I followed godlike Agamemnon
169	to Ilium of the good horses, to fight the Trojans.
170	But come, tell me this and say it exactly.
171	What death overcame you, of long-lamented death?
172	Was it a long disease, or did Artemis who showers arrows
173	come at you with her gentle shafts and kill you?
174	Tell me of my father and of my son, whom I left.
175	Is my honor still with them, or does some other
176	man already have it, and they say I will no longer come home?
177	Tell me the will and the mind of my wedded wife,
178	whether she stays by the child and keeps everything firm,
179	or whether already some best of the Achaeans has married her.
> (narrator)
180	So I spoke, and the lady mother answered at once:
> Anticleia
181	She stays very much, with an enduring heart,
182	in your halls. The miserable nights and days
183	always waste her as she sheds tears.
184	No one yet has your fine honor, but at his ease
185	Telemachus holds the estates and shares equal feasts,
186	such as a justice-giving man should have care of.
187	All call him. Your father stays there
188	in the country and does not come down to the city. He has no
189	bed, no bedding and cloaks and shining coverlets,
190	but in winter he sleeps where the slaves in the house sleep,
191	in the dust near the fire, and he wears bad clothes on his skin.
192	When summer comes and the flourishing harvest,
193	everywhere on the slope of his vineyard plot
194	low beds of fallen leaves are thrown down.
195	There he lies grieving, and a great grief grows in his mind,
196	longing for your homecoming, and a hard old age comes on him.
197	So I too died and met my fate.
198	The sharp-eyed showerer of arrows did not
199	come at me in the halls with her gentle shafts and kill me,
200	nor did any disease come on me, the kind that most
201	takes the heart out of the limbs with a hateful wasting.
202	It was longing for you, and your counsels, shining Odysseus,
203	and your gentleness, that took away my honey-sweet heart.
> (narrator)
204	So she spoke, and I wanted, turning it in my mind,
205	to take hold of the soul of my dead mother.
206	Three times I started toward her, and my heart told me to take hold.
207	Three times she flew from my hands like a shadow or a dream.
208	A sharp grief grew in my heart still more,
209	and speaking I said winged words to her:
> Odysseus
210	Mother of mine, why do you not stay for me, when I want to take hold,
211	so that even in Hades, throwing our arms around each other,
212	we two might have our fill of cold lament?
213	Or is this some image that proud Persephone
214	sent me, so I would groan and lament still more?
> (narrator)
215	So I spoke, and the lady mother answered at once:
> Anticleia
216	Oh my child, most luckless of all men,
217	Persephone, daughter of Zeus, is not deceiving you.
218	This is the way of mortals, when someone dies.
219	The sinews no longer hold the flesh and the bones,
220	but the strong force of burning fire
221	overcomes them, once the heart has left the white bones,
222	and the soul, like a dream, flies off and flutters.
223	But strain toward the light as quickly as you can. Know all these things,
224	so that later you may tell them to your wife as well.
> (narrator)
225	So we two answered each other with words, and the women
226	came, for proud Persephone sent them,
227	all who were wives and daughters of the best men.
228	They gathered in a crowd around the dark blood.
229	I thought how I might question each one.
230	This plan seemed best to my heart:
231	drawing the long-edged sword from beside my thick thigh
232	I would not let them all drink the dark blood at once.
233	They came up one after another, and each
234	told her birth. I questioned them all.
235	There first I saw Tyro of the noble father.
236	She said she was the child of faultless Salmoneus,
237	and she said she was the wife of Cretheus, son of Aeolus.
238	She had loved the river, divine Enipeus,
239	who is by far the most beautiful of rivers that run on the earth,
240	and she used to go along the beautiful streams of Enipeus.
241	The earth-holder, the earth-shaker, likening himself to him,
242	lay with her at the mouth of the swirling river.
243	A dark wave stood around, equal to a mountain,
244	arched over, and it hid the god and the mortal woman.
245	He loosed her maiden belt and poured sleep over her.
246	When the god had finished the work of love,
247	he took her hand and spoke a word and called her by name:
> Poseidon
248	Be glad, woman, in this love. When the year has turned
249	you will bear shining children, for the beds of
250	the deathless are not barren. You must tend them and raise them.
251	Now go to the house, and hold back, and do not name me.
252	I am Poseidon the earth-shaker.
> (narrator)
253	So speaking he went down under the swelling sea.
254	She conceived and bore Pelias and Neleus,
255	and both became strong servants of great Zeus,
256	both of them. Pelias lived in wide Iolcus
257	rich in sheep, and the other in sandy Pylos.
258	The queen of women bore the others to Cretheus,
259	Aeson and Pheres and Amythaon who fights from a chariot.
260	After her I saw Antiope, daughter of Asopus,
261	who claimed she had slept in the arms of Zeus,
262	and she bore two sons, Amphion and Zethus,
263	who first founded the seat of seven-gated Thebes
264	and walled it, since they could not live in
265	wide Thebes unwalled, strong as they were.
266	After her I saw Alcmene, wife of Amphitryon,
267	who bore Heracles, bold in endurance, lion-hearted,
268	when she had mixed in the arms of great Zeus.
269	And Megara, daughter of high-hearted Creon,
270	whom the son of Amphitryon had, his force never worn down.
271	I saw the mother of Oedipus, beautiful Epicaste,
272	who did a great deed in the ignorance of her mind,
273	marrying her own son. He, having killed his father,
274	married her. The gods soon made it known to people.
275	He in much-loved Thebes, suffering pains,
276	ruled the Cadmeans through the deadly plans of the gods.
277	She went to the house of Hades, strong keeper of the gate,
278	fastening a steep noose from the high roof-beam,
279	held by her grief. She left behind for him
280	very many pains, as many as a mother's Furies bring to pass.
281	And I saw Chloris, very beautiful, whom Neleus once
282	married for her beauty, when he had given countless bride-gifts,
283	youngest daughter of Amphion, son of Iasus,
284	who once ruled with force in Minyan Orchomenus.
285	She was queen of Pylos, and she bore him shining children,
286	Nestor and Chromius and lordly Periclymenus.
287	Besides these she bore strong Pero, a wonder to mortals,
288	whom all the neighbors courted. Neleus
289	would not give her to anyone who did not drive the spiral-horned, wide-browed cattle
290	from Phylace, the cattle of the force of Iphicles,
291	hard ones. The faultless seer alone promised
292	to drive them. A hard lot from a god bound him,
293	painful bonds and country cowherds.
294	But when the months and the days were completed
295	as the year turned back and the seasons came,
296	then the force of Iphicles released him
297	when he had said all the oracles. The will of Zeus was fulfilled.
298	And I saw Leda, the wife of Tyndareus,
299	who bore under Tyndareus two strong-minded sons,
300	Castor the horse-tamer and Polydeuces good with his fists.
301	The life-giving earth holds both of them alive.
302	Even under the earth they have honor from Zeus:
303	on alternate days they live, and on the other days
304	they are dead. They have drawn honor equal to the gods.
305	After her I saw Iphimedeia, wife of Aloeus,
306	who said she had mixed with Poseidon,
307	and she bore two sons, but they were short-lived,
308	godlike Otus and far-famed Ephialtes,
309	whom the grain-giving plowland raised as the tallest
310	and by far the most beautiful after famous Orion.
311	At nine years they were nine cubits
312	in breadth, and they were nine fathoms in height.
313	They threatened the deathless on Olympus
314	that they would raise the din of furious war.
315	They were eager to put Ossa on Olympus, and on Ossa
316	Pelion of the shaking leaves, so the sky would be climbable.
317	They would have finished it, if they had reached the measure of youth.
318	But the son of Zeus, whom fair-haired Leto bore, destroyed
319	both of them, before the down under their temples
320	had bloomed and covered their chins with a fine-flowering beard.
321	I saw Phaedra and Procris and beautiful Ariadne,
322	daughter of ruin-minded Minos, whom Theseus once
323	led from Crete toward the slope of holy Athens,
324	but he had no joy of her. Before that Artemis killed her
325	on sea-girt Dia, on the witness of Dionysus.
326	I saw Maera and Clymene and hateful Eriphyle,
327	who took precious gold for her own husband's life.
328	I could not tell or name them all,
329	all the wives and daughters of heroes I saw.
330	The deathless night would wear out first. It is also the time
331	to sleep, either going to the fast ship to the companions
332	or here. The escort will be a care to the gods and to you.
> (narrator)
333	So he spoke, and they all became hushed in silence,
334	held by the spell through the shadowy halls.
335	White-armed Arete began the words among them:
> Arete
336	Phaeacians, how does this man seem to you to be
337	in form and size and in the balanced mind inside?
338	He is my guest besides, and each of you has his share of honor.
339	So do not send him off in a hurry, and do not
340	cut short the gifts he needs like this. Many
341	possessions lie in your halls by the will of the gods.
> (narrator)
342	The old hero Echeneus also spoke among them,
343	who was older than the Phaeacian men:
> Echeneus
344	Friends, the thoughtful queen has not spoken
345	beside the mark or beside our thought. Obey her.
346	On Alcinous from this point hang the work and the word.
> (narrator)
347	Then Alcinous answered him and spoke:
> Alcinous
348	This word will be so, as long as I
349	am alive and rule the oar-loving Phaeacians.
350	Let the stranger endure, though he very much wants his homecoming,
351	to wait until tomorrow, until I have finished
352	the whole gift. The escort will be a care to the men,
353	to all of them, and most to me. The power in the people is mine.
> (narrator)
354	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
355	Lord Alcinous, distinguished among all the people,
356	if you should bid me stay right here even for a year,
357	and urge on the escort and give shining gifts,
358	I would want that, and it would be much more profitable
359	to reach my own dear fatherland with a fuller hand.
360	I would be more respected and more loved by the men,
361	all who saw me come home to Ithaca.
> (narrator)
362	Then Alcinous answered him and spoke:
> Alcinous
363	Odysseus, looking at you we do not at all liken you
364	to be a deceiver and a thief, the kind the black earth
365	feeds many of, men scattered far,
366	fitting together lies from which no one could see.
367	On you there is a form of words, and there are good wits in you.
368	You have told the story skillfully, as when a singer tells,
369	the painful cares of all the Argives and of yourself.
370	But come, tell me this and say it exactly:
371	did you see any of the godlike companions who went with you
372	to Ilium and met their fate there?
373	This night is very long, beyond measure. It is not yet time
374	to sleep in the hall. Tell me the wondrous deeds.
375	I would hold out until bright dawn, if you
376	would endure to tell your cares in the hall.
> (narrator)
377	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
378	Lord Alcinous, distinguished among all the people,
379	there is a time for many stories, and a time for sleep.
380	If you still long to listen, I would not
381	grudge you these, and I would tell still more pitiable things,
382	the cares of my companions, who died afterward,
383	who escaped the groaning war-cry of the Trojans
384	and died on the way home by the will of a bad woman.
385	When holy Persephone had scattered the souls
386	of the female women this way and that,
387	the soul of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, came,
388	grieving. Around him others gathered, all who
389	died with him in the house of Aegisthus and met their fate.
390	He knew me at once when he had drunk the dark blood.
391	He cried out shrilly, shedding a thick tear,
392	stretching his hands toward me, wanting to reach me.
393	But he no longer had firm force or any vigor,
394	such as was in his bent limbs before.
395	I wept when I saw him, and I pitied him in my heart,
396	and speaking I said winged words to him:
397	Son of Atreus, most honored, lord of men Agamemnon,
398	what death overcame you, of long-lamented death?
399	Did Poseidon overcome you among the ships
400	raising a miserable blast of hard winds?
401	Or did hostile men harm you on the dry land
402	as you were cutting off cattle and fine flocks of sheep,
403	or fighting over a city and women?
> (narrator)
404	So I spoke, and he answered me at once:
> Agamemnon
405	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
406	Poseidon did not overcome me among the ships
407	raising a miserable blast of hard winds,
408	nor did hostile men harm me on the dry land.
409	Aegisthus made death and doom for me
410	and killed me with my cursed wife, calling me home,
411	feasting me, as a man kills an ox at the manger.
412	So I died a most pitiable death. Around me the other companions
413	were killed without pause, like white-tusked pigs
414	in the house of a rich man of great power
415	at a wedding or a shared feast or a flourishing banquet.
416	You have already met the slaughter of many men,
417	killed one by one and in the strong fight.
418	But you would have pitied it most in your heart if you had seen
419	how around the mixing-bowl and the tables full of food
420	we lay in the hall, and the whole floor ran with blood.
421	The most pitiable voice I heard was Priam's daughter,
422	Cassandra, whom crafty-minded Clytemnestra killed
423	over me. I, raising my hands toward the earth,
424	threw them as I died around the sword. The dog-faced woman
425	turned away, and she did not dare, even as I went to Hades,
426	to close my eyes with her hands and shut my mouth.
427	So there is nothing more terrible and more doglike than a woman
428	who puts such deeds in her mind.
429	That is the unseemly work she planned,
430	making death for her wedded husband. I had thought
431	I would come home welcome to my children and my slaves.
432	She, knowing outstandingly grim things,
433	poured shame on herself and on the female women
434	who will come after, even on one who is a good worker.
> (narrator)
435	So he spoke, and I answered him:
> Odysseus
436	Oh, far-seeing Zeus has terribly
437	hated the race of Atreus through the counsels of women
438	from the beginning. Many of us died because of Helen,
439	and Clytemnestra fitted a trick for you while you were far away.
> (narrator)
440	So I spoke, and he answered me at once:
> Agamemnon
441	So now you too must never be gentle even to your wife.
442	Do not tell her the whole story that you know well,
443	but say one part, and let one part be hidden.
444	But for you, Odysseus, there will be no death from a woman.
445	She is too thoughtful, and she knows good counsels in her mind,
446	the daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope.
447	We left her a young bride when we
448	went to the war. A child was at her breast,
449	a little one, who by now sits among men in the count,
450	blessed. His own father will see him when he comes,
451	and that one will embrace his father, as is right.
452	My wife did not even let me have my fill of my son
453	with my eyes. She killed me first, myself as well.
454	I will tell you something else, and put it in your mind.
455	In secret, not openly, bring your ship
456	to your own dear fatherland. Women are no longer to be trusted.
457	But come, tell me this and say it exactly,
458	if you hear anywhere that my son is still alive,
459	whether in Orchomenus or in sandy Pylos
460	or with Menelaus in wide Sparta.
461	Godlike Orestes has not yet died on the earth.
> (narrator)
462	So he spoke, and I answered him:
> Odysseus
463	Son of Atreus, why do you ask me this? I do not know at all
464	whether he is alive or dead. It is bad to speak empty words.
> (narrator)
465	So the two of us stood answering each other with hateful words,
466	grieving, shedding thick tears.
467	Then the soul of Achilles, son of Peleus, came,
468	and of Patroclus and faultless Antilochus
469	and of Ajax, who was best in form and body
470	of the other Danaans after the faultless son of Peleus.
471	The soul of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus knew me
472	and lamenting said winged words to me:
> Achilles
473	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
474	hard man, what still greater work will you plot in your mind?
475	How did you dare to come down to Hades, where the dead
476	live without wits, images of worn-out mortals?
> (narrator)
477	So he spoke, and I answered him:
> Odysseus
478	Achilles, son of Peleus, far the best of the Achaeans,
479	I came on an errand for Teiresias, if he would say
480	some counsel, how I might reach rugged Ithaca.
481	I have not yet come near Achaea, and I have not yet
482	set foot on our land, but I always have evils. Of you, Achilles,
483	no man was more blessed before or afterward.
484	While you were alive we Argives honored you equal to the gods,
485	and now you have great power among the dead
486	here. So do not grieve that you are dead, Achilles.
> (narrator)
487	So I spoke, and he answered me at once:
> Achilles
488	Do not speak lightly to me of death, shining Odysseus.
489	I would rather be on the soil as a hired man for another,
490	for a man without a lot, who does not have much livelihood,
491	than rule over all the worn-out dead.
492	But come, tell me the story of my proud son,
493	whether he went to the war to be a front fighter, or not.
494	And tell me of faultless Peleus, if you have heard anything,
495	whether he still has honor among the many Myrmidons,
496	or they dishonor him through Hellas and Phthia,
497	because old age holds his hands and his feet.
498	I am not there as a helper under the rays of the sun,
499	being such as I once was in wide Troy
500	when I killed the best of the people, defending the Argives.
501	If I could come like that even for a little to my father's house,
502	I would make someone hate my force and my unapproachable hands,
503	whoever does him violence and keeps him from his honor.
> (narrator)
504	So he spoke, and I answered him:
> Odysseus
505	I have heard nothing of faultless Peleus,
506	but of your son, your own dear Neoptolemus,
507	I will tell the whole truth, as you bid me.
508	I myself brought him in a hollow even ship
509	from Scyros among the well-greaved Achaeans.
510	Whenever we were forming plans around the city of Troy,
511	he always spoke first and he did not miss the mark in his words.
512	Godlike Nestor and I alone used to beat him.
513	When we were fighting with bronze on the plain of the Trojans,
514	he never stayed in the mass of men or in the crowd,
515	but he ran far out in front, yielding his force to no one,
516	and he killed many men in the terrible slaughter.
517	I could not tell or name them all,
518	how many of the people he killed defending the Argives.
519	But what a man he cut down with the bronze, the son of Telephus,
520	the hero Eurypylus, and many companions around him,
521	Ceteians, were killed because of a woman's gifts.
522	I saw him as the most beautiful after godlike Memnon.
523	When we went down into the horse that Epeius made,
524	the best of the Argives, and everything was laid on me,
525	both to open the close ambush and to shut it,
526	there the other leaders and counsellors of the Danaans
527	wiped tears and each man's limbs trembled under him.
528	I never saw that one with my eyes at all
529	either pale in his handsome skin or wiping
530	a tear from his cheeks. He begged me very much
531	to let him out of the horse, and he kept feeling for the hilt of his sword
532	and his bronze-heavy spear, and he was planning evils for the Trojans.
533	When we had sacked the steep city of Priam
534	he went aboard the ship with his share and a fine prize,
535	unhurt, neither hit by the sharp bronze
536	nor wounded in close fight, as happens many times
537	in war. Ares rages mixed.
> (narrator)
538	So I spoke, and the soul of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus
539	went with long strides down the asphodel meadow,
540	glad that I had said his son was distinguished.
541	The other souls of the dead who have died
542	stood grieving, and each asked about her cares.
543	The soul of Ajax, son of Telamon, alone
544	stood apart, angry because of the victory
545	that I won over him when we contended by the ships
546	for the arms of Achilles. His lady mother had set them out.
547	The children of the Trojans judged, and Pallas Athena.
548	I wish I had not won in such a contest.
549	Such a head the earth covered because of them,
550	Ajax, who was beyond the others in form and in deeds
551	of the other Danaans after the faultless son of Peleus.
552	I spoke to him with honeyed words:
553	Ajax, son of faultless Telamon, were you never
554	even in death going to forget your anger at me over the
555	cursed arms? The gods set this pain on the Argives.
556	Such a tower you were lost to them. For you the Achaeans
557	grieved as much as for the head of Achilles, son of Peleus,
558	when you died, without a break. No one else
559	is to blame, but Zeus terribly hated the army
560	of the Danaan spearmen, and he put this fate on you.
561	But come here, lord, so you may hear our word
562	and our story. Master your force and your proud heart.
> (narrator)
563	So I spoke, and he answered me nothing. He went after the other
564	souls into Erebus of the dead who have died.
565	There he would have spoken anyway, angry as he was, or I would have spoken to him.
566	But the heart in my own chest wanted
567	to see the souls of the other dead.
568	There I saw Minos, the shining son of Zeus,
569	holding a gold staff, giving judgments to the dead,
570	sitting, and they asked the lord for judgments around him,
571	sitting and standing, through the wide-gated house of Hades.
572	After him I marked huge Orion
573	herding beasts together down the asphodel meadow,
574	the ones he himself had killed on the lonely mountains,
575	holding in his hands a club all of bronze, always unbreakable.
576	And I saw Tityos, son of famous Earth,
577	lying on the ground. He lay over nine measures,
578	and two vultures sitting on either side tore his liver,
579	diving inside the caul, and he did not keep them off with his hands.
580	He had dragged Leto, the honored wife of Zeus,
581	as she went to Pytho through Panopeus of the beautiful dancing-floors.
582	And I saw Tantalus having hard pains,
583	standing in a pool. The water came up to his chin.
584	He stood thirsty, and he could not take it to drink.
585	As often as the old man bent, wanting to drink,
586	so often the water would be swallowed away, and around his feet
587	the black earth would show, and a god would dry it.
588	Trees with high leaves poured fruit down from above,
589	pears and pomegranates and apple trees with shining fruit
590	and sweet figs and flourishing olives.
591	Whenever the old man reached out to take them in his hands,
592	the wind would toss them toward the shadowy clouds.
593	And I saw Sisyphus having hard pains,
594	heaving a huge stone with both hands.
595	Bracing himself with his hands and his feet
596	he would push the stone up toward the ridge. When it was about
597	to go over the top, then a mighty force would turn it back.
598	The shameless stone would roll down to the plain again.
599	He would push it back again, straining, and the sweat
600	ran down from his limbs, and dust rose from his head.
601	After him I marked the force of Heracles,
602	an image. He himself among the deathless gods
603	delights in the feasts and has Hebe of the beautiful ankles,
604	child of great Zeus and of Hera of the gold sandals.
605	Around him there was a cry of the dead, like birds,
606	scattering every way. He, like dark night,
607	held a bare bow and an arrow on the string,
608	glaring terribly, always like one about to shoot.
609	A terrible baldric was around his chest,
610	a gold strap, where wondrous works were made,
611	bears and wild pigs and bright-eyed lions
612	and fights and battles and killings and the slaughter of men.
613	May the man who put that strap in his craft
614	not have made it, and may he not make anything else.
615	He knew me when he saw me with his eyes,
616	and lamenting he said winged words to me:
> Heracles
617	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
618	Poor man, do you too drag some bad fate,
619	such as I used to bear under the rays of the sun?

621	endless misery. I was made subject to a much worse man,
622	and he laid hard contests on me.
623	He once sent me here to fetch the dog. He did not think
624	there was any other contest stronger than this one for me.

626	Hermes sent me, and owl-eyed Athena.
