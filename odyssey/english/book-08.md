# Odyssey Book 8

> (narrator)
1	When early-born Dawn appeared, the rose-fingered one,
2	the holy strength of Alcinous rose from his bed,
3	and Zeus-born Odysseus rose too, the sacker of cities.
4	The holy strength of Alcinous led them
5	to the Phaeacians' assembly, which was built for them beside the ships.
6	Coming there they sat down on the polished stones
7	close together. And Pallas Athena went through the city
8	likened to the herald of wise Alcinous,
9	planning a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus,
10	and standing beside each man she spoke a word:
> Athena
11	Come now, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians,
12	go to the assembly, so you may learn of the stranger
13	who has just come to the house of wise Alcinous
14	driven over the sea, in form like the immortals.
> (narrator)
15	So speaking she stirred the force and heart of each.
16	Quickly the assembly-places and seats of mortals were filled
17	as they gathered; and many wondered when they saw
18	the wise son of Laertes; and Athena
19	poured a divine grace on his head and shoulders
20	and made him taller and thicker to look at,
21	so that he would become dear to all the Phaeacians
22	and terrible and worthy of regard, and would complete the contests
23	many of them, those the Phaeacians put Odysseus to the test in.
24	But when they were gathered and had come together,
25	Alcinous spoke in the assembly among them and said:
> Alcinous
26	Hear me, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians,
27	so I may say what the heart in my chest bids me.
28	This stranger — I do not know who he is — has come wandering to my house,
29	whether from people of the dawn or of the evening;
30	he urges an escort, and he begs that it be sure.
31	And we, as we have before, let us urge on the escort.
32	For no other man either, whoever comes to my house,
33	stays here long lamenting for the sake of an escort.
34	But come, let us drag a black ship down to the bright sea,
35	one that has not yet sailed, and let two and fifty young men
36	be chosen through the people, whoever are the best.
37	And when you have all well bound the oars to the thole-pins
38	go ashore; and then make ready a swift feast
39	coming to our house; and I will provide well for all.
40	These things I charge the young men; but the others,
41	the scepter-bearing kings, to my fine house
42	come, so we may show the stranger guest-friendship in the halls,
43	and let no one refuse. And call the godlike singer
44	Demodocus; for to him the god has given song beyond others
45	to delight, in whatever way his heart stirs him to sing.
> (narrator)
46	So speaking he led the way, and they followed with him,
47	the scepter-bearers; and the herald went after the godlike singer.
48	And the young men, two and fifty chosen,
49	went, as he ordered, to the shore of the barren sea.
50	But when they came down to the ship and the sea,
51	they dragged the black ship into the depths of the sea,
52	and they set the mast and the sails in the black ship,
53	and they fitted the oars in the leather straps,
54	all in due measure, and they spread the white sails.
55	And they moored her high in the water; and then
56	they went to go to the great house of wise Alcinous.
57	And the porches and the courtyards and the rooms were filled with men
58	gathering; and there were many, young and old.
59	And for them Alcinous sacrificed twelve sheep,
60	and eight white-tusked pigs, and two shambling oxen;
61	they flayed them and dressed them, and made a lovely feast.
62	And the herald came near leading the trusted singer,
63	whom the Muse loved beyond others, and she gave him both good and bad;
64	she took away his eyes, and she gave him sweet song.
65	And Pontonous set for him a silver-studded throne
66	in the middle of the feasters, leaning it against a tall pillar;
67	and from a peg he hung the clear lyre
68	above his head and showed him to take it in his hands,
69	the herald; and he set beside him a basket and a fine table,
70	and beside him a cup of wine, to drink when his heart should bid him.
71	And they reached out their hands to the food lying ready.
72	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
73	the Muse stirred the singer to sing the famous deeds of men,
74	from the song whose fame then reached the wide sky,
75	the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles son of Peleus,
76	how once they contended at a flourishing feast of the gods
77	with terrible words, and the lord of men Agamemnon
78	rejoiced in his mind that the best of the Achaeans were contending.
79	For so Phoebus Apollo had told him, prophesying,
80	in holy Pytho, when he crossed the stone threshold
81	to inquire of the oracle; for then the beginning of pain was rolling
82	toward Trojans and Danaans through the plans of great Zeus.
83	These things the far-famed singer sang; but Odysseus
84	taking a great purple cloak in his strong hands
85	drew it down over his head, and covered his handsome face;
86	for he was ashamed before the Phaeacians, shedding tears under his brows.
87	Yes, whenever the godlike singer stopped singing,
88	he would wipe away the tear and take the cloak from his head
89	and taking a two-handled cup he would pour a libation to the gods;
90	but whenever he began again and they urged him to sing,
91	the best of the Phaeacians, since they delighted in the words,
92	Odysseus would cover his head again and groan.
93	There he escaped the notice of all the others as he shed tears,
94	but Alcinous alone marked him and understood,
95	sitting near him, and he heard him groaning heavily.
96	And at once he spoke among the Phaeacians who love the oar:
> Alcinous
97	Hear me, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians.
98	Already we have satisfied our hearts with the equal feast
99	and with the lyre, which is the companion of the flourishing feast;
100	now let us go out and try the contests
101	of all kinds, so that the stranger may tell his friends
102	when he has come home, how we surpass others
103	in boxing and wrestling and in jumps and with our feet.
> (narrator)
104	So speaking he led the way, and they followed with him.
105	And from a peg he hung the clear lyre,
106	and he took Demodocus by the hand and led him out of the hall,
107	the herald; and he led him on the same road that the others
108	the best of the Phaeacians went, to watch the games.
109	They went to go to the assembly-place, and a great crowd followed with them,
110	thousands; and many young men stood up, good ones too.
111	Acroneus rose, and Ocyalus and Elatreus,
112	and Nauteus and Prymneus and Anchialus and Eretmeus,
113	and Ponteus and Proreus, Thoon and Anabesineus
114	and Amphialus, son of Polyneus son of Tecton;
115	and Euryalus rose too, equal to Ares the bane of men,
116	son of Naubolus, who was best in looks and in body
117	of all the Phaeacians after blameless Laodamas.
118	And three sons of blameless Alcinous stood up,
119	Laodamas and Halius and godlike Clytoneus.
120	These first tried themselves with their feet.
121	The course was stretched from the turning-post; and they all together
122	flew quickly, kicking up the dust of the plain;
123	and of them by far the best at running was blameless Clytoneus;
124	as far as the range of a pair of mules in fallow land,
125	so far running out ahead he reached the people, and the others were left behind.
126	And they tried the painful wrestling;
127	and in this Euryalus surpassed all the best men.
128	And in the jump Amphialus was far the foremost of all;
129	and with the discus Elatreus was much the strongest of all,
130	and in boxing Laodamas, the good son of Alcinous.
131	But when they had all delighted their hearts in the contests,
132	then Laodamas, the son of Alcinous, spoke among them:
> Laodamas
133	Come, friends, let us ask the stranger if some contest
134	he knows and has learned. In build at least he is not a poor man,
135	in thighs and shins and both arms above
136	and his strong neck and great strength; and he does not
137	lack youth, but he has been broken by many hard things;
138	for I say there is nothing else worse than the sea
139	to confound a man, even if he should be very strong.
> (narrator)
140	Then Euryalus answered him and said:
> Euryalus
141	Laodamas, you have spoken this word very much in due measure.
142	Go yourself now and challenge him and make the word clear.
> (narrator)
143	But when the good son of Alcinous heard this,
144	he stood going into the middle and spoke to Odysseus:
> Laodamas
145	Come here, you too, stranger-father, and try the contests,
146	if you have learned any somewhere; it is likely you know contests;
147	for there is no greater fame for a man, as long as he lives,
148	than what he does with his feet and with his own hands.
149	But come, try it, and scatter the cares from your heart.
150	And your road will no longer be long away, but already
151	the ship has been dragged down and the companions are ready.
> (narrator)
152	Then Odysseus of many devices answered him and said:
> Odysseus
153	Laodamas, why do you bid me this, taunting me?
154	Cares are more in my mind than contests,
155	I who before suffered very many things and toiled many things,
156	and now I sit in your assembly, wanting a homecoming,
157	begging the king and the whole people.
> (narrator)
158	Then Euryalus answered him and quarreled with him to his face:
> Euryalus
159	No, stranger, I do not liken you to a man skilled
160	in contests, such as are many among people,
161	but to one who, going often with a many-benched ship,
162	is a captain of sailors who are traders,
163	a man mindful of his cargo and a watcher of the freight
164	and of grasping profits; you do not look like an athlete.
> (narrator)
165	Then looking from under his brows Odysseus of many devices said:
> Odysseus
166	Stranger, you have not spoken well; you seem like a man of reckless transgression.
167	So the gods do not give gracious gifts to all
168	men, not build, nor mind, nor speaking in assembly.
169	For one man is weaker in looks,
170	but a god crowns his form with words, and people
171	look at him with delight; and he speaks unfailingly
172	with gentle respect, and he stands out among those gathered,
173	and they look on him as on a god when he walks through the city.
174	Another again is like the immortals in looks,
175	but no grace is crowned around his words,
176	as with you the looks are outstanding, and not otherwise
177	would a god even make them, but you are a vain man in mind.
178	You have stirred the heart in my own chest
179	speaking not in good order. I am not unlearned in contests,
180	as you say, but I think I was among the first
181	as long as I trusted in my youth and in my hands.
182	Now I am held by hardship and pains; for I have endured many things,
183	the wars of men and cutting through painful waves.
184	But even so, having suffered many hard things, I will try the contests;
185	for your word bites the heart, and you have spurred me by speaking.
> (narrator)
186	He spoke, and with his cloak still on he sprang up and took a discus
187	bigger and thicker, heavier by no small amount
188	than the one the Phaeacians had been throwing among themselves.
189	This he whirled and sent from his strong hand,
190	and the stone hummed; and they crouched down toward the ground,
191	the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships,
192	under the rush of the stone; and it flew beyond all the marks
193	running lightly from his hand. And Athena set the turning-points
194	likened in body to a man, and she spoke a word and called him by name:
> Athena
195	Even a blind man, stranger, could pick out this mark
196	feeling it, since it is not at all mixed in the crowd,
197	but far the first. And take heart in this contest;
198	no one of the Phaeacians will reach this, or throw beyond it.
> (narrator)
199	So she spoke, and much-enduring godlike Odysseus was glad,
200	rejoicing that he saw a kindly companion in the gathering.
201	And then he spoke more lightly among the Phaeacians:
> Odysseus
202	Reach this now, young men. Soon later another
203	I think I will send as far as this, or even farther.
204	And of the others, whoever his heart and spirit bid,
205	come here and let him try, since you have angered me too much,
206	in boxing or in wrestling or even with the feet, I do not grudge it,
207	of all the Phaeacians, except Laodamas himself.
208	For this man is my guest-friend; who would fight with one who shows him guest-love?
209	A fool indeed is that man, and a worthless one,
210	whoever offers a quarrel of contests to his host
211	in a people of another land; he cuts short all his own things.
212	Of the others I refuse no one and I do not slight him,
213	but I want to know and to try them face to face.
214	For I am not poor in all things, among men, as many contests as there are;
215	I know well how to handle a well-polished bow;
216	I would be first to strike a man, shooting in a crowd
217	of enemy men, even if very many companions
218	stood close by and were shooting at the men.
219	Philoctetes alone used to surpass me with the bow
220	in the land of the Trojans, when we Achaeans were shooting.
221	And of the others I say I am far the foremost,
222	as many mortals as now are on the earth eating bread.
223	But I will not wish to contend with men of former time,
224	neither with Heracles nor with Eurytus of Oechalia,
225	who used to contend even with the immortals about bows.
226	Therefore great Eurytus died quickly too, and he did not reach
227	old age in his halls; for Apollo in his anger
228	killed him, because he challenged him to shoot.
229	And I throw the spear as far as no other man does an arrow.
230	Only with my feet I fear that someone may pass me
231	of the Phaeacians; for I was broken too unseemly
232	in many waves, since there was no care aboard the ship
233	lasting; therefore my dear limbs are loosened.
> (narrator)
234	So he spoke, and they all grew still in silence.
235	But Alcinous alone answered him and said:
> Alcinous
236	Stranger, since these things you say among us are not without grace,
237	but you want to show your excellence, which attends you,
238	angry that this man, standing beside you in the gathering,
239	quarreled with you, as no mortal would make light of your excellence
240	who knew in his mind how to speak things that fit;
241	but come now, understand my word, so that to another
242	of the heroes you may tell it, when in your own halls
243	you feast beside your wife and your children,
244	remembering our excellence, what things also to us
245	Zeus has set as works all the way through, from our fathers still.
246	For we are not blameless boxers or wrestlers,
247	but we run swiftly with our feet and we are best with ships,
248	and always the feast is dear to us, and the lyre and dances
249	and changes of clothes and warm baths and beds.
250	But come, as many of the Phaeacians as are the best dancers,
251	play, so that the stranger may tell his friends
252	when he has come home, how we surpass others
253	in seafaring and with our feet and in dance and in song.
254	And let someone go quickly for Demodocus and the clear lyre
255	bring it, which lies somewhere in our house.
> (narrator)
256	So spoke Alcinous like the gods, and the herald rose
257	to bring the hollow lyre from the house of the king.
258	And the chosen stewards, nine in all, stood up,
259	public men, who used to manage each thing well at the games,
260	and they smoothed the dancing-floor, and they widened the gathering-place finely.
261	And the herald came near carrying the clear lyre
262	for Demodocus; and he then went into the middle; and around him young men
263	in first youth stood, skilled in dancing,
264	and they struck the holy dance with their feet. But Odysseus
265	watched the flashing of their feet, and he wondered in his heart.
266	But the other, playing the lyre, struck up to sing a beautiful song
> Demodocus
267	about the love of Ares and fair-crowned Aphrodite,
268	how first they lay together in the house of Hephaestus
269	in secret, and he gave many things, and he shamed the bed and the sleeping-place
270	of lord Hephaestus. And at once a messenger came to him,
271	Helios, who had noticed them lying together in love.
272	And Hephaestus, when he heard the heart-paining word,
273	went to go to his forge, brooding bad things in his mind,
274	and he set a great anvil on the anvil-block, and he hammered bonds
275	unbreakable, not to be loosened, so they would stay fast right there.
276	But when he had made the trap, angry at Ares,
277	he went to go to the chamber where his own dear bed lay,
278	and around the bedposts he poured the bindings in a circle everywhere;
279	and many too were poured down from above from the roof-beam,
280	like fine spiderwebs, which no one could even see,
281	not even of the blessed gods; for they were made very crafty.
282	But when he had poured the whole trap around the bed,
283	he made as if to go to Lemnos, the well-built city,
284	which of all lands is by far the dearest to him.
285	And Ares of the golden reins did not keep a blind watch,
286	when he saw Hephaestus of the famous craft going apart;
287	he went to go toward the house of far-famed Hephaestus
288	hungry for the love of fair-crowned Cythereia.
289	And she, just come from her father, the mighty son of Cronus,
290	was sitting down; and he went inside the house,
291	and he took her hand, and he spoke a word and called her by name:
> Ares
292	Come here, love, let us go to bed and lie down;
293	for Hephaestus is no longer in the country, but already somewhere
294	he has gone to Lemnos among the Sintians of the wild speech.
> Demodocus
295	So he spoke, and it seemed welcome to her to go to bed.
296	The two of them went to the bed and lay down; and around them the bonds
297	skillfully made were poured, of Hephaestus of many thoughts,
298	and they could not move their limbs at all, nor lift them.
299	And then they knew that there was no longer any escape.
300	And near them came the far-famed one, lame in both legs,
301	turning back again before he reached the land of Lemnos;
302	for Helios had kept watch for him and told him the word.
303	He went to go toward his house, grieving in his own heart;
304	and he stood in the doorway, and a savage anger seized him;
305	and he cried out terribly, and he called so all the gods could hear:
> Hephaestus
306	Father Zeus and you other blessed gods who are forever,
307	come here, so you may see laughable deeds, and not to be endured,
308	how me, being lame, the daughter of Zeus, Aphrodite,
309	always dishonors, and she loves ruinous Ares,
310	because he is handsome and sound of foot, but I
311	was born feeble. And no one else is to blame for me,
312	but my two parents; I wish they had not begotten me.
313	But you will see where the two of them are sleeping in love
314	having gone into my bed, and I looking on am in pain.
315	I do not think they will still want to lie a little while like this,
316	even though they love each other very much; soon they will not both want
317	to sleep; but the trap and the bond will hold them,
318	until her father pays me back all the bride-gifts,
319	as many as I put in his hands for the sake of the dog-faced girl,
320	because his daughter is beautiful, but she has no self-control.
> Demodocus
321	So he spoke, and the gods gathered toward the bronze-floored house;
322	Poseidon the earth-holder came, the runner came,
323	Hermes, and the lord Apollo who works from afar came.
324	But the female gods stayed at home, each in shame.
325	The gods stood in the doorway, givers of good things;
326	and unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods
327	as they looked on the crafts of Hephaestus of many thoughts.
328	And one would say, looking at another near him:
> (a god)
329	Bad deeds do not prosper; the slow catches the swift,
330	as now Hephaestus, being slow, has caught Ares,
331	though he is the swiftest of the gods who hold Olympus,
332	being lame, by his crafts; therefore Ares owes the adulterer's fine.
> Demodocus
333	So they spoke such things to one another;
334	and the lord Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to Hermes:
> Apollo
335	Hermes, son of Zeus, guide, giver of good things,
336	would you be willing, pressed in strong bonds,
337	to sleep in bed beside golden Aphrodite?
> Demodocus
338	Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, answered him:
> Hermes
339	If only this would happen, lord Apollo who shoots from afar;
340	let bonds three times as many, numberless, hold me around,
341	and you gods look on, and all the goddesses,
342	and still I would sleep beside golden Aphrodite.
> Demodocus
343	So he spoke, and laughter rose among the immortal gods.
344	But laughter did not hold Poseidon, and he kept begging
345	Hephaestus of the famous work that he release Ares.
346	And speaking to him he addressed him with winged words:
> Poseidon
347	Release him; and I myself promise you, as you bid,
348	that he will pay all that is due among the immortal gods.
> Demodocus
349	Then the far-famed one, lame in both legs, said to him:
> Hephaestus
350	Do not bid me this, Poseidon earth-holder;
351	wretched are the pledges pledged for wretched men.
352	How could I bind you among the immortal gods,
353	if Ares should go, slipping the debt and the bond?
> Demodocus
354	Then Poseidon the earth-shaker said to him:
> Poseidon
355	Hephaestus, even if Ares, slipping the debt,
356	should go fleeing, I myself will pay you this.
> Demodocus
357	Then the far-famed one, lame in both legs, answered him:
> Hephaestus
358	It is not possible, nor is it fitting, to refuse your word.
> Demodocus
359	So speaking the force of Hephaestus undid the bond.
360	And when the two were released from the bond, strong though it was,
361	at once they sprang up, and he went toward Thrace,
362	and she reached Cyprus, laughter-loving Aphrodite,
363	to Paphos; and there she has a precinct and an altar of incense.
364	There the Graces washed her and anointed her with oil
365	ambrosial, such as is shed on the gods who are forever,
366	and they put lovely clothes around her, a wonder to look at.
> (narrator)
367	These things the far-famed singer sang; but Odysseus
368	delighted in his own mind as he listened, and the others too,
369	the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships.
370	And Alcinous bid Halius and Laodamas
371	to dance alone, since no one contended with them.
372	And when they had taken a beautiful ball in their hands,
373	a purple one, which wise Polybus had made for them,
374	the one would throw it toward the shadowy clouds
375	bending back, and the other, lifting high from the ground,
376	would catch it easily, before he reached the earth with his feet.
377	But when they had tried the ball straight up,
378	then they danced on the much-nourishing earth
379	changing places thick and fast; and the other young men beat time
380	standing in the gathering-place, and a great din rose up.
381	Then godlike Odysseus spoke to Alcinous:
> Odysseus
382	Lord Alcinous, most distinguished of all the people,
383	you promised you were the best dancers,
384	and it has been made good; awe holds me as I look.
> (narrator)
385	So he spoke, and the holy strength of Alcinous was glad,
386	and at once he spoke among the Phaeacians who love the oar:
> Alcinous
387	Hear me, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians.
388	The stranger seems to me to be a man of very good sense.
389	But come, let us give him a guest-gift, as is fitting.
390	For twelve distinguished kings throughout the people
391	hold rule as leaders, and I myself am the thirteenth;
392	of these let each bring a well-washed cloak and a tunic
393	and a talent of precious gold.
394	And quickly let us bring it all together, so that in his hands
395	the stranger may have it and go to supper, glad in his heart.
396	And let Euryalus win him over himself with words
397	and with a gift, since he did not speak a word in due measure.
> (narrator)
398	So he spoke, and they all approved and so ordered,
399	and each sent a herald out to bring the gifts.
400	Then Euryalus answered him and said:
> Euryalus
401	Lord Alcinous, most distinguished of all the people,
402	then I will win over the stranger, as you bid.
403	I will give him this sword all of bronze, on which the hilt
404	is silver, and a scabbard of newly sawn ivory
405	is curled around it; it will be worth much to him.
> (narrator)
406	So speaking he put in his hands the silver-studded sword
407	and speaking to him he addressed him with winged words:
> Euryalus
408	Be glad, father, stranger; and if any word has been spoken
409	terrible, at once let the storm-winds snatch it up and carry it away.
410	And may the gods grant you to see your wife and to reach your fatherland,
411	since now for a long time you suffer pains far from your friends.
> (narrator)
412	Then Odysseus of many devices answered him and said:
> Odysseus
413	And you, friend, be very glad, and may the gods give you blessings.
414	And may there be no longing afterward for this sword
415	which you have given me, winning me over with words.
> (narrator)
416	He spoke, and he put the silver-studded sword around his shoulders.
417	And the sun set, and the famous gifts were there for him.
418	And the proud heralds carried them to the house of Alcinous;
419	and the sons of blameless Alcinous received them
420	and set the very beautiful gifts beside their honored mother.
421	The holy strength of Alcinous led them,
422	and coming there they sat down on the high thrones.
423	Then the strength of Alcinous spoke to Arete:
> Alcinous
424	Come here, wife, bring a chest of distinction, whichever is the best;
425	and in it set yourself a well-washed cloak and a tunic.
426	And heat bronze for him over the fire, and warm water,
427	so that when he has bathed and seen well laid out all
428	the gifts that the blameless Phaeacians have brought here,
429	he may delight in the feast and hear the hymn of song.
430	And I will give him this very beautiful cup of mine,
431	of gold, so that remembering me all his days
432	he may pour in his hall to Zeus and the other gods.
> (narrator)
433	So he spoke, and Arete spoke among the serving-women
434	to set a great tripod over the fire as quickly as possible.
435	They set a bath-pouring tripod on the blazing fire,
436	and they poured water in, and taking wood they kindled it underneath.
437	The fire went around the belly of the tripod, and the water grew warm;
438	meanwhile Arete brought out for the stranger a very beautiful chest
439	from the chamber, and she set in it the beautiful gifts,
440	clothing and gold, which the Phaeacians had given him;
441	and in it she herself set a fine cloak and a tunic,
442	and speaking to him she addressed him with winged words:
> Arete
443	See to the lid yourself now, and quickly throw a fastening on it,
444	so that no one may harm you on the road, whenever again
445	you sleep a sweet sleep going in the black ship.
> (narrator)
