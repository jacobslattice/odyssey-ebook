# Odyssey Book 4

> (narrator)
1	And they came to hollow Lacedaemon, the land of ravines,
2	and they drove toward the house of glorious Menelaus.
3	They found him giving a marriage-feast for many kinsmen
4	of his son and his blameless daughter, in his own house.
5	He was sending her to the son of Achilles, breaker of men —
6	for in Troy first he had promised and nodded
7	that he would give her, and the gods were fulfilling the marriage for them.
8	So he was sending her there with horses and chariots to go
9	to the far-famed city of the Myrmidons, whose king that son was.
10	And for his son he was bringing a girl from Sparta, Alector's daughter —
11	the son born to him late-born, strong Megapenthes,
12	from a slave woman; and the gods no longer showed Helen a child
13	after she first bore her lovely daughter,
14	Hermione, who had the beauty of golden Aphrodite.
15	So they were feasting through the high-roofed great house,
16	neighbors and kinsmen of glorious Menelaus,
17	taking their pleasure; and among them a divine singer was making music
18	playing the lyre, and two tumblers among them,
19	as he led the song, were whirling through the middle.
20	But those two, in the forecourt of the house, themselves and their horses,
21	the hero Telemachus and Nestor's shining son,
22	halted; and coming forward lord Eteoneus saw them,
23	the ready attendant of glorious Menelaus,
24	and he went to tell it through the house to the shepherd of the people,
25	and standing near he addressed him with winged words:
> Eteoneus
26	There are two strangers here, Menelaus, Zeus-nourished,
27	two men, and they are like the stock of great Zeus.
28	But say: shall we unyoke their swift horses for them,
29	or send them on to someone else to reach, who will befriend them?
> (narrator)
30	Then fair-haired Menelaus, greatly troubled, spoke to him:
> Menelaus
31	You were not a fool before, Eteoneus, son of Boethous —
32	not before; but now you talk child-minded things like a child.
33	Surely we two, after eating many guest-gifts
34	of other people, came here, in the hope that Zeus
35	hereafter might stop our misery. But unyoke the horses
36	of the strangers, and bring the men themselves farther in to feast.
> (narrator)
37	So he spoke, and the man darted through the hall and called the other
38	ready attendants to come with him.
39	They unyoked the horses from under the yoke, sweating,
40	and they tied them at the mangers for horses,
41	and threw spelt beside them, and mixed in white barley,
42	and they leaned the chariots against the shining front-walls,
43	and they led the men themselves into the divine house. And they, looking,
44	wondered through the house of the Zeus-nourished king;
45	for a gleam as of the sun or of the moon
46	was through the high-roofed house of glorious Menelaus.
47	But when they had taken their fill of looking with their eyes,
48	they went into well-polished bathtubs and bathed.
49	And when the serving-women had washed them and anointed them with oil,
50	they threw thick cloaks about them, and tunics,
51	and they sat on chairs beside Menelaus, son of Atreus.
52	A serving-woman brought water for the hands and poured it from a pitcher,
53	a beautiful golden one, over a silver basin,
54	to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside them.
55	The respected housekeeper brought and set bread,
56	putting many dishes on it, giving freely of what was there.
57	The carver lifted and set plates of meats
58	of all kinds, and he set golden cups beside them.
59	And greeting the two, fair-haired Menelaus spoke:
> Menelaus
60	Take hold of the food, and be glad. And afterward,
61	when you have tasted the meal, we will ask who you are
62	among men; for the stock of your parents has not perished in you,
63	but you are the stock of men who are Zeus-nourished kings
64	bearing the scepter, since no low men would have begotten such as you.
> (narrator)
65	So he spoke, and he set beside them the fat back of an ox,
66	roast, taking it in his hands, which they had set before him as his own portion of honor.
67	They put out their hands to the ready food lying before them.
68	But when they had put from them the desire of drink and food,
69	then Telemachus spoke to the son of Nestor,
70	holding his head close, so the others would not hear:
> Telemachus
71	Look, son of Nestor, you who are dear to my heart —
72	the flash of bronze through the echoing halls,
73	and of gold and amber and silver and ivory.
74	The court of Olympian Zeus must be like this inside,
75	so unspeakably many are these things. Awe holds me as I look.
> (narrator)
76	Fair-haired Menelaus heard him as he spoke,
77	and raising his voice he addressed them with winged words:
> Menelaus
78	Dear children, no one among mortals would vie with Zeus;
79	for his houses and his possessions are deathless.
80	But of men, someone may rival me, or he may not,
81	in possessions. For after suffering much and wandering much
82	I brought them in my ships, and I came in the eighth year,
83	having wandered to Cyprus and Phoenicia and the Egyptians,
84	and I reached the Ethiopians and the Sidonians and the Erembi
85	and Libya, where the lambs grow horns at once.
86	For the sheep bear three times in a full year.
87	There neither the lord nor any shepherd is lacking
88	in cheese and meats or sweet milk,
89	but they always give milk in plenty to suck.
90	While I was wandering about those places gathering much livelihood,
91	in that time another man killed my brother
92	in secret, unforeseen, by the trick of his ruinous wife.
93	So it is not with joy that I am lord of these possessions.
94	And you are likely to have heard this from your fathers, whoever they
95	are to you, since I suffered very many things, and I lost a house
96	well lived-in, that held many good things.
97	I wish I had a third share in my halls
98	and lived, and that those men were safe who then perished
99	in wide Troy, far from horse-pasturing Argos.
100	But even so, though I mourn and grieve for them all,
101	often sitting in our halls
102	I take pleasure in my mind with lament, and then again
103	I stop; for there is a quick satiety of icy lament.
104	Of all of them I do not mourn so much, though I am grieved,
105	as for one man, who makes sleep and food hateful to me
106	when I remember him, since no one of the Achaeans toiled so much
107	as Odysseus toiled and endured. And for him it was going
108	to be cares of his own, and for me a grief never to be forgotten
109	for that man, how long he has been gone, and we do not know at all
110	whether he is alive or has died. They must be mourning him,
111	old Laertes and sound-minded Penelope
112	and Telemachus, whom he left a newborn in the house.
> (narrator)
113	So he spoke, and he stirred in the other a longing to lament for his father.
114	He let a tear fall from his eyelids to the ground when he heard of his father,
115	holding a purple cloak up before his eyes
116	with both hands. And Menelaus noticed him,
117	and then he pondered in his mind and in his heart
118	whether he should let him mention his father himself
119	or first question him and test each thing.
120	While he was turning these things in his mind and in his heart,
121	Helen came out of her fragrant high-roofed chamber,
122	like Artemis of the golden distaff.
123	Adreste set a well-made chair for her,
124	and Alcippe brought a rug of soft wool,
125	and Phylo brought a silver basket, which Alcandre had given her,
126	the wife of Polybus, who lived in Thebes
127	in Egypt, where the most possessions lie in the houses;
128	he gave Menelaus two silver bathtubs,
129	and two tripods, and ten talents of gold.
130	And separately his wife gave Helen beautiful gifts:
131	she granted a golden distaff and a wheeled basket,
132	silver, and the rims were finished with gold.
133	This the serving-woman Phylo brought and set beside her,
134	packed full of worked thread. And on it
135	the distaff was laid out, holding dark-violet wool.
136	She sat in the chair, and a footstool was under her feet.
137	At once she questioned her husband with words about each thing:
> Helen
138	Do we know, then, Zeus-nourished Menelaus, who these men
139	among men claim to be, coming to our house?
140	Shall I speak false, or shall I tell the truth? My heart bids me.
141	For I say I have never yet seen anyone so like
142	either man or woman. Awe holds me as I look,
143	as this one is like the son of great-hearted Odysseus,
144	Telemachus, whom that man left a newborn in the house
145	when for the sake of me, shameless-faced as I am, you Achaeans
146	came under Troy, driving on a bold war.
> (narrator)
147	Then fair-haired Menelaus spoke in answer to her:
> Menelaus
148	Just so I too think now, wife, as you liken him.
149	For such are that man's feet, and such his hands,
150	and the glances of his eyes, and his head and the hair above it.
151	And just now I was speaking, remembering Odysseus,
152	how many things that man suffered in misery and toiled
153	for my sake, and he was shedding a bitter tear under his brows,
154	holding a purple cloak up before his eyes.
> (narrator)
155	Then Nestor's son Pisistratus spoke facing him:
> Pisistratus
156	Son of Atreus, Zeus-nourished Menelaus, leader of the people,
157	this is truly that man's son, as you say.
158	But he is sound-minded, and he feels righteous anger in his heart
159	at first coming here like this and showing a rush of words
160	before you, whose voice we two take pleasure in as in a god's.
161	But Gerenian horseman Nestor sent me out
162	to go with him as escort. For he longed to see you,
163	so that you might put some word or some deed in his mind.
164	For a child has many pains when his father is gone
165	in the halls, if he has no other helpers,
166	as now for Telemachus: that man is gone, and there are no others
167	who might keep ruin off him through the district.
> (narrator)
168	Then fair-haired Menelaus spoke in answer to him:
> Menelaus
169	Oh, then truly the son of a man dear to me has come to my house,
170	who for my sake toiled through many contests.
171	And I said that if he came I would befriend him beyond the other
172	Argives, if wide-seeing Zeus the Olympian had granted us two
173	a homecoming over the sea in the swift ships.
174	And I would have settled a city for him in Argos and built him a house,
175	bringing him from Ithaca with his possessions and his child
176	and all his people, after sacking one city
177	of those that live around and are ruled by me myself.
178	And being here we would often have kept company. And nothing else
179	would have parted us, loving and taking our pleasure,
180	until the black cloud of death covered us over.
181	But these things, I suppose, the god himself was going to begrudge,
182	who made that wretched man alone without a homecoming.
> (narrator)
183	So he spoke, and in all of them he stirred a longing for lament.
184	Argive Helen wept, born of Zeus,
185	and Telemachus wept, and Menelaus son of Atreus,
186	nor did Nestor's son keep his eyes without tears.
187	For he remembered in his heart blameless Antilochus,
188	whom the shining son of bright Dawn killed.
189	Remembering him he spoke winged words:
> Pisistratus
190	Son of Atreus, Nestor the old man used to say that you were
191	clear-headed beyond mortals, whenever we remembered you
192	in his halls, and asked one another.
193	And now, if there is any way, be persuaded by me. For I myself
194	take no pleasure in mourning after the meal, and besides, Dawn
195	early-born will come. I feel no righteous anger at all
196	at weeping for whoever of mortals has died and met his portion.
197	This then is the only honor-gift for wretched mortals:
198	to cut the hair and let a tear fall from the cheeks.
199	For my own brother has died, not at all the worst
200	of the Argives. You are likely to know. For I myself
201	did not meet him or see him. But they say he was beyond others,
202	Antilochus, outstanding as a swift runner and a fighter.
> (narrator)
203	Then fair-haired Menelaus spoke in answer to him:
> Menelaus
204	Dear friend, since you have said as much as a clear-headed man
205	would say and do, even one who was older.
206	For you are of such a father, you who speak clear-headed things.
207	A man's offspring is easily known, a man to whom the son of Cronus
208	spins prosperity both at his marrying and at his birth,
209	as now he has given to Nestor all his days through:
210	that he himself grow old in richness in his halls,
211	and that his sons be both sensible and best with the spear.
212	But we will let go the weeping that happened just now,
213	and turn our minds back to the meal, and let them pour water over our hands.
214	There will be stories even from dawn on
215	for Telemachus and me to tell out to one another.
> (narrator)
216	So he spoke, and Asphalion poured water over their hands,
217	the ready attendant of glorious Menelaus.
218	They put out their hands to the ready food lying before them.
219	Then Helen, born of Zeus, thought of something else.
220	At once she threw a drug into the wine they were drinking from,
221	pain-killing and anger-freeing, making one forget all evils.
222	Whoever swallowed it, once it was mixed in the bowl,
223	would not that day let a tear fall down his cheeks,
224	not even if his mother and his father died,
225	not even if right in front of him they cut down his brother or his dear son
226	with bronze, and he saw it with his own eyes.
227	Such were the cunning drugs Zeus's daughter had,
228	good ones, which Polydamna gave her, the wife of Thon,
229	an Egyptian woman; for her the grain-giving earth bears the most
230	drugs, many good ones mixed, and many baneful.
231	And each is a healer, knowing beyond all
232	people. For they are of the stock of Paieon.
233	But when she had thrown it in and told them to pour the wine,
234	she spoke again in answer with stories:
> Helen
235	Son of Atreus, Zeus-nourished Menelaus, and you here too,
236	sons of good men. A god gives now to one, now to another,
237	Zeus, both good and bad. For he can do all things.
238	Now feast, sitting in the halls,
239	and take pleasure in stories. For I will tell things that fit.
240	I could not tell or name them all,
241	as many as are the contests of Odysseus of the enduring heart.
242	But such a thing as this the strong man did and endured
243	in the land of the Trojans, where you Achaeans suffered pains.
244	Beating himself with unseemly blows,
245	throwing a poor wrap around his shoulders, like a house-slave,
246	he went down into the wide-wayed city of men who hated him.
247	And hiding himself he made himself like another man,
248	a beggar, who was nothing like that on the Achaeans' ships.
249	Like that he went down into the Trojans' city, and they did not know him.
250	I alone recognized him as the man he was,
251	and I questioned him. But he evaded me with cunning.
252	But when I was washing him and rubbing him with oil,
253	and I put clothes around him and swore a strong oath
254	not to make Odysseus known among the Trojans
255	before he reached the swift ships and the huts,
256	then he told me the whole mind of the Achaeans.
257	And killing many of the Trojans with the long-edged bronze
258	he came back to the Argives, and he brought back much knowledge.
259	Then the other Trojan women wailed sharp; but my heart
260	was glad, since already my heart had turned to go
261	back home, and I was groaning after the ruinous blindness that Aphrodite
262	gave me, when she led me there from my own fatherland,
263	leaving behind my child and my marriage-chamber and my husband,
264	who was lacking in nothing, neither in mind nor in looks.
> (narrator)
265	Then fair-haired Menelaus spoke in answer to her:
> Menelaus
266	Yes, wife, you have said all this in due measure.
267	Already I have come to know the counsel and the mind of many
268	heroic men, and I have gone over much land.
269	But I have never yet seen with my eyes such a thing
270	as the dear heart of Odysseus of the enduring heart was.
271	Such a thing as this too the strong man did and endured
272	in the polished horse, where we all, the best
273	of the Argives, sat, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans.
274	Then you came there; a divinity must have bid you,
275	one who wanted to hold out glory to the Trojans.
276	And godlike Deiphobus followed you as you went.
277	Three times you walked around the hollow ambush, feeling it,
278	and you named the best of the Danaans by name,
279	likening your voice to the wives of all the Argives.
280	But I and the son of Tydeus and godlike Odysseus
281	sitting in the middle heard you as you called.
282	We two both were eager, starting up,
283	either to come out, or to answer at once from inside.
284	But Odysseus held us back and checked us, eager as we were.
285	Then all the other sons of the Achaeans were silent,
286	but Anticlus alone wanted to answer you with words.
287	But Odysseus pressed on his mouth with his hands
288	hard, without stopping, and he saved all the Achaeans.
289	He held him until Pallas Athena led you away.
> (narrator)
290	Then clear-headed Telemachus spoke to him in turn:
> Telemachus
291	Son of Atreus, Zeus-nourished Menelaus, leader of the people,
292	it is more painful. For these things did not fend off baneful destruction from him,
293	not even if the heart inside him was of iron.
294	But come, turn us toward bed, so that at last
295	we may take our pleasure in sleep, gone to bed under sweet sleep.
> (narrator)
296	So he spoke, and Argive Helen told the serving-women
297	to set beds under the portico and to throw on beautiful
298	purple blankets and spread rugs on top,
299	and to put woolen cloaks on top to wear.
300	They went from the hall holding a torch in their hands,
301	and they spread the beds. And a herald led the strangers out.
302	They then slept right there in the forehall of the house,
303	the hero Telemachus and Nestor's shining son.
304	And the son of Atreus slept in the inner part of the high house,
305	and beside him long-robed Helen lay down, divine among women.
306	When early-born rose-fingered Dawn appeared,
307	Menelaus good at the war-cry rose from his bed
308	putting on his clothes, and he set a sharp sword about his shoulder,
309	and bound fine sandals under his shining feet,
310	and went out from the chamber, like a god to look at,
311	and he sat down beside Telemachus, and spoke a word and called him by name:
> Menelaus
312	What need has brought you here, hero Telemachus,
313	to bright Sparta, over the wide back of the sea?
314	Public or private? Tell me this so it does not miss.
> (narrator)
315	Then clear-headed Telemachus spoke to him in turn:
> Telemachus
316	Son of Atreus, Zeus-nourished Menelaus, leader of the people,
317	I have come, if you might tell me some rumor of my father.
318	My house is being eaten, and the rich lands are destroyed,
319	and the house is full of men who hate me, who always
320	slaughter my close-packed sheep and the shambling crumpled-horn cattle,
321	suitors of my mother, holding overbearing outrage.
322	Therefore now I come to your knees, if you should be willing
323	to tell that man's baneful destruction, if you have seen it somewhere
324	with your own eyes, or have heard the tale of another
325	who was driven off course. For his mother bore him to be beyond others in misery.
326	And do not, out of shame before me, sweeten it, nor out of pity,
327	but tell me well in order how you met with the sight.
328	I beg you, if ever my father, noble Odysseus,
329	undertook and finished some word or some deed for you
330	in the land of the Trojans, where you Achaeans suffered pains,
331	remember those things now for me, and tell me what does not miss.
> (narrator)
332	Then fair-haired Menelaus, greatly troubled, spoke to him:
> Menelaus
333	Oh, then truly they wanted to take to bed in the bed of a strong-hearted man,
334	themselves being cowards.
335	As when in the thicket of a strong lion a doe
336	has put her newborn milk-fed fawns to sleep
337	and goes searching the slopes and the grassy hollows
338	grazing, and then he comes into his own lair,
339	and on both of them he sends an unseemly fate,
340	so Odysseus will send an unseemly fate on those men.
341	If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
342	being such as he once was in well-built Lesbos
343	when he stood up and wrestled Philomeleides in a quarrel,
344	and threw him down hard, and all the Achaeans were glad —
345	being such, if Odysseus would come among the suitors.
346	They would all become short-lived and bitter-married.
347	But these things you ask me and beg, I would not
348	tell you other things aside, turning them aside, nor will I deceive you.
349	But what the old man of the sea told me, who does not miss,
350	of those I will hide no word from you, nor conceal it.
351	The gods still held me in Egypt, though I was eager to come here,
352	since I had not offered them perfect hecatombs.
353	And the gods always wanted their commands to be remembered.
354	Then there is an island in the much-splashing sea
355	in front of Egypt, and they call it Pharos,
356	as far away as a hollow ship in a whole day
357	can finish, when a shrill following wind blows behind it.
358	And in it there is a good harbor, from which they launch well-balanced ships
359	into the sea, after drawing black water.
360	There the gods held me twenty days, and never did the sea-winds
361	appear, blowing, the ones that are escorts
362	of ships over the wide back of the sea.
363	And now all the stores would have been used up, and the men's strength,
364	if one of the gods had not pitied me and saved me,
365	the daughter of mighty Proteus, the old man of the sea,
366	Eidothea. For I stirred her heart most of all.
367	She met me as I wandered alone, apart from my companions.
368	For they were always going around the island fishing
369	with bent hooks, and hunger was wearing the belly.
370	And she, standing near me, spoke a word and said:
> Eidothea
371	You are a fool, stranger, too much so, and slack-minded,
372	or do you hang back on purpose and take pleasure in suffering pains?
373	How is it you are held so long on the island, and you cannot
374	find any sign, and the heart of your companions is wasting?
> Menelaus
375	So she spoke, and I spoke to her in answer:
376	I will tell you out, whoever you are of the goddesses,
377	that I am not held back on purpose, but I must
378	have offended the deathless ones who hold the wide sky.
379	But you tell me — for the gods know all things —
380	which of the deathless ones shackles me and has bound me from my road,
381	and the homecoming, how I shall go over the fish-full sea.
382	So I spoke, and at once she answered, the divine among goddesses:
> Eidothea
383	Then I will tell you, stranger, quite exactly.
384	An old man of the sea comes here, who does not miss,
385	deathless Proteus the Egyptian, who knows the depths
386	of the whole sea, a servant of Poseidon.
387	And they say he is my father, and that he begot me.
388	If somehow you could set an ambush and catch him,
389	he would tell you the road and the measures of the way
390	and the homecoming, how you will go over the fish-full sea.
391	And he would tell you, Zeus-nourished man, if you are willing,
392	what evil and what good has been done in your halls
393	while you have been gone on a long and hard road.
> Menelaus
394	So she spoke, and I spoke to her in answer:
395	You yourself now think out the ambush of the divine old man,
396	so that he does not see me first or learn first and escape.
397	For a god is hard for a mortal man to master.
398	So I spoke, and at once she answered, the divine among goddesses:
> Eidothea
399	Then I will tell you, stranger, quite exactly.
400	When the sun has gone around the middle of the sky,
401	then the old man of the sea, who does not miss, comes out of the salt water
402	under the breath of Zephyr, covered in a black ripple,
403	and coming out he sleeps under the hollow caves.
404	And around him the seals, flipper-foot children of the beautiful sea-goddess,
405	sleep in a herd, having come up from the gray salt water,
406	breathing out the sharp smell of the deep-valleyed sea.
407	There I will take you at the appearing of dawn
408	and put you to bed in a row. And you pick well three companions
409	who are the best by your well-benched ships.
410	And I will tell you all the tricks of that old man.
411	First he will count the seals and go over them.
412	But when he has counted them all by fives and seen them,
413	he will lay himself down in the middle like a shepherd among flocks of sheep.
414	When you first see him laid to sleep,
415	then let strength and force be your care,
416	and hold him there, though he is eager and straining to escape.
417	He will try becoming all the things that on the earth
418	crawl, and water and wondrous-blazing fire.
419	But you hold him unshaken and press him still more.
420	But when he himself questions you with words,
421	being such as you saw him when he was laid to sleep,
422	then hold back your force and loose the old man,
423	hero, and ask him which of the gods is hard on you,
424	and the homecoming, how you will go over the fish-full sea.
> Menelaus
425	So speaking she went down under the swelling sea.
426	Then I went to the ships, where they stood in the sands,
427	and my heart was much troubled as I went.
428	But when I came down to the ship and the sea,
429	we made ready the meal, and ambrosial night came on.
430	Then we slept on the breaking-shore of the sea.
431	When early-born rose-fingered Dawn appeared,
432	then along the shore of the wide-wayed sea
433	I went, begging the gods much. And three companions
434	I led, whom I trusted most for every attempt.
435	Meanwhile she, going down under the wide gulf of the sea,
436	brought four sealskins from the sea.
437	They were all newly flayed. And she was devising a trick against her father.
438	She hollowed out beds in the sea-sands
439	and sat waiting. And we came very close to her.
440	She put us to bed in a row, and threw a skin over each.
441	There the ambush would have been most terrible. For it wore us terribly,
442	the most deadly smell of the sea-fed seals.
443	For who would sleep beside a sea-monster?
444	But she herself saved us and thought of a great help.
445	She put ambrosia under each man's nose,
446	sweet-smelling, and she destroyed the smell of the sea-monster.
447	All morning we waited with an enduring heart.
448	And the seals came from the salt water in a body. Then they
449	laid themselves down in a row along the breaking-shore of the sea.
450	At midday the old man came from the salt water, and he found the seals
451	well-fed, and he went over them all, and counted the number.
452	And he counted us first among the sea-creatures, and in his heart he did not
453	suspect there was a trick. Then he laid himself down too.
454	We rushed shouting, and we threw our hands
