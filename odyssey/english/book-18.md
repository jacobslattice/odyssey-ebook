# Odyssey Book 18

> (narrator)
1	A public beggar came on them, who through the town
2	of Ithaca used to beg, and he stood out by a ravenous belly
3	to eat and drink without stopping. He did not have force
4	or strength, but he was very large in look to see.
5	Arnaeus was his name. The lady mother set that
6	from birth. All the young men used to call him Irus,
7	because he would go and carry messages whenever someone told him.
8	Coming he was driving Odysseus from his own house,
9	and quarreling he addressed winged words to him:
> Irus
10	Yield, old man, from the doorway, so he does not soon drag you even by the foot.
11	Do you not notice that they are all winking at me
12	and telling me to drag you? I am ashamed all the same.
13	Get up, so strife and hands do not soon come for the two of us.
> (narrator)
14	Looking from under his brows Odysseus of many devices spoke to him:
> Odysseus
15	Strange man, I am not doing you any evil and I am not speaking any,
16	and I do not begrudge anyone giving, even taking much.
17	This threshold will hold both, and you should not
18	begrudge others' things. You seem to me to be a wanderer
19	just as I am, and the gods are going to grant wealth.
20	Do not challenge too much with your hands, so you do not anger me,
21	so that I, even being old, do not mix your chest and lips
22	with blood. Quiet would be even more for me
23	tomorrow. I do not think you will turn back
24	a second time into the hall of Odysseus son of Laertes.
> (narrator)
25	Angry, Irus the wanderer addressed him:
> Irus
26	Oh, how the glutton talks running on,
27	like an old woman at the oven. I would contrive evils for him
28	striking with both hands, and I would drive all the teeth
29	out of the jaws to the ground, like a crop-eating sow.
30	Gird now, so all these also may know
31	us fighting. How would you fight a younger man?
> (narrator)
32	So they in front of the high doors
33	on the polished threshold were growing jagged with all their heart.
34	The holy force of Antinous heard the two of them,
35	and laughing sweetly he spoke among the suitors:
> Antinous
36	Friends, no such thing has ever happened before,
37	what a delight a god has brought into this house.
38	The stranger and Irus are quarreling with one another
39	to fight with hands. Let us drive them together quickly.
> (narrator)
40	So he spoke, and they all sprang up laughing
41	and gathered around the badly-clothed beggars.
42	Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke among them:
> Antinous
43	Hear me, proud suitors, so I may say something.
44	These goat-bellies are lying in the fire, which for supper
45	we set, filling them with savor and blood.
46	Whichever of the two wins and becomes stronger,
47	let him stand up and take himself which of them he wishes.
48	He will always feast among us again, and we will not let any other
49	beggar mix inside asking.
> (narrator)
50	So Antinous spoke, and the word pleased them.
51	Odysseus of many devices spoke among them planning a trick:
> Odysseus
52	Friends, there is no way for an old man worn by misery
53	to fight a younger man. But a badly-working belly
54	urges me, so I may be broken by blows.
55	But come now, all of you swear a strong oath to me
56	that no one, bringing favor to Irus, with a heavy hand
57	hit me acting reckless and break me by force for this man.
> (narrator)
58	So he spoke, and they all swore as he told them.
59	When they had sworn and finished the oath,
60	the holy force of Telemachus spoke among them again:
> Telemachus
61	Stranger, if the heart and the proud spirit urge you
62	to defend yourself against this man, do not fear any other
63	of the Achaeans, since he who hits you will fight with more.
64	I am the host, and the kings praise it besides,
65	Antinous and Eurymachus, both thoughtful.
> (narrator)
66	So he spoke, and they all praised it. Odysseus
67	girded the rags around his genitals, and he showed thighs
68	beautiful and large, and his wide shoulders appeared
69	and his chest and sturdy arms. Athena
70	standing near made the limbs swell for the shepherd of the people.
71	All the suitors wondered overbearingly.
72	So one would say looking at another near:
> a suitor
73	Soon Irus Un-Irus will have a self-drawn evil,
74	such a thigh the old man shows out of the rags.
> (narrator)
75	So they spoke, and Irus's heart was stirred badly.
76	Even so the workers led him girded by force
77	afraid. The flesh was trembling around his limbs.
78	Antinous rebuked him and spoke a word and called him by name:
> Antinous
79	Now may you not be, boaster, and may you not have been born,
80	if you tremble at this man and are terribly afraid,
81	an old man worn by the misery that reaches him.
82	I will tell you, and this will also be finished:
83	if this man beats you and becomes stronger,
84	I will send you to the mainland, throwing you in a black ship,
85	to Echetus the king, destroyer of all mortals,
86	who would cut off nose and ears with pitiless bronze,
87	and drawing out the genitals would give them raw to the dogs to divide.
> (narrator)
88	So he spoke, and still more a trembling took the limbs under him.
89	They led them into the middle. Both held up their hands.
90	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus considered
91	whether he should drive so the life would leave him falling there,
92	or drive him lightly and stretch him on the earth.
93	As he was thinking it seemed more profitable
94	to drive lightly, so the Achaeans would not mark him.
95	Then holding up, the one drove at the right shoulder,
96	Irus, and the other drove at the neck under the ear, and the bones inside
97	he crushed. At once bloody blood came down through the mouth,
98	and he fell in the dust crying out, and he drove his teeth together
99	kicking the earth with his feet. The shining suitors
100	holding up their hands died of laughter. Odysseus
101	dragged him through the doorway taking him by the foot, until he reached the courtyard
102	and the doors of the porch. He set him against the courtyard wall
103	leaning him. He threw the staff into his hand,
104	and speaking he addressed winged words to him:
> Odysseus
105	Sit here now keeping off pigs and dogs,
106	and do not be lord of strangers and beggars
107	being wretched, so you do not somewhere take a share of some even greater evil.
> (narrator)
108	He spoke and threw the unseemly bag around his shoulders,
109	torn close, and there was a twisted strap on it.
110	Going back to the threshold he sat. They went inside
111	laughing sweetly and greeting him with words:
> a suitor
112	May Zeus give you, stranger, and the other deathless gods,
113	what you most wish and what has been dear to your heart,
114	you who stopped this unsated man from wandering
115	in the people. Soon we will lead him to the mainland
116	to Echetus the king, destroyer of all mortals.
> (narrator)
117	So they spoke, and godlike Odysseus was glad at the omen-word.
118	Antinous set a great belly beside him,
119	full of savor and blood. Amphinomus
120	lifting two loaves from the basket set them beside him
121	and pledged him with a gold cup, and he spoke:
> Amphinomus
122	Be glad, father stranger. May wealth come to you even afterward.
123	But now you are held in many evils.
> (narrator)
124	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
125	Amphinomus, you seem to me to be very thoughtful.
126	Such is your father too, since I heard a good fame,
127	that Nisus of Dulichium was both good and rich.
128	They say you were born from him, and you are like a reasonable man.
129	Therefore I will tell you, and you put it together and hear me.
130	The earth feeds nothing more feeble than a human,
131	of all things that breathe and creep on the earth.
132	He never says he will suffer evil afterward,
133	as long as the gods hold out excellence and the knees are raised.
134	But when the blessed gods also finish wretched things,
135	he bears those too, unwilling, with an enduring heart.
136	Such is the mind of humans on the earth
137	as the day the father of men and gods leads on.
138	I too was once going to be blessed among men,
139	and I did many reckless things yielding to force and strength,
140	trusting in my father and my own brothers.
141	Therefore let no man ever be wholly lawless,
142	but let him have the gifts of the gods in silence, whatever they give.
143	Such things as I see, the suitors contriving reckless things,
144	cutting the possessions and dishonoring the wife
145	of a man whom I say will no longer be away from his own people and fatherland
146	for long. Very near. But may a god
147	lead you home out from under, and may you not meet that man
148	whenever he comes home to his own fatherland.
149	I do not think they will be divided without blood,
150	the suitors and that man, when he comes under the roof-beam.
> (narrator)
151	So he spoke, and pouring a libation he drank honey-sweet wine
152	and put the cup back in the hands of the marshal of the people.
153	He went through the house with his own heart troubled,
154	nodding his head. His heart already saw an evil.
155	Even so he did not escape the fate. Athena bound even him
156	to be broken by force under the hands and the spear of Telemachus.
157	He sat down again on the throne from which he had stood.
158	The goddess, owl-eyed Athena, put it in the mind
159	of the daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope,
160	to appear to the suitors, so she might most stretch
161	the heart of the suitors and become more honored
162	toward her husband and son than she was before.
163	She laughed a useless laugh and spoke a word and called her by name:
> Penelope
164	Eurynome, my heart wishes, it did not before,
165	to appear to the suitors, hated as they are all the same.
166	I would say a word to my child that would be more profitable,
167	not to mix in all things with the overbearing suitors,
168	who speak well and think badly behind.
> (narrator)
169	Then Eurynome the housekeeper spoke a word to her:
> Eurynome
170	Yes, child, you have said all these things according to portion.
171	Go and speak the word to your child and do not hide it,
172	washing your skin and anointing your cheeks.
173	Do not go so mixed with tears around the face,
174	since it is worse to mourn unmixed always.
175	Your child is already of such an age, whom you most
176	prayed the deathless to see bearded.
> (narrator)
177	Then careful Penelope spoke to her:
> Penelope
178	Eurynome, do not talk me into these things, though you care,
179	to wash the skin and anoint it with oil.
180	The gods who hold Olympus destroyed my splendor
181	since that man went in the hollow ships.
182	Tell Autonoe and Hippodameia
183	to come, so they may stand beside me in the halls.
184	I will not go in alone among the men. I am ashamed.
> (narrator)
185	So she spoke, and the old woman went through the hall
186	to tell the women and urge them to come.
187	Then the goddess, owl-eyed Athena, thought another thing:
188	she poured sweet sleep on the daughter of Icarius,
189	and she slept leaning back, and all her joints were loosed
190	there on the couch. Meanwhile the bright one of goddesses
191	was giving deathless gifts, so the Achaeans would wonder at her.
192	First she cleaned the beautiful face with beauty,
193	deathless, with which well-crowned Cytherea
194	anoints herself when she goes to the desirable dance of the Graces.
195	She made her taller and thicker to see,
196	and she made her whiter than sawn ivory.
197	When she had done so the bright one of goddesses went away,
198	and the white-armed serving-women came from the hall
199	coming on a voice. Sweet sleep let her go,
200	and she wiped her cheeks with her hands and spoke:
> Penelope
201	A soft sleep covered me, who suffer terribly.
202	I wish holy Artemis would give me a soft death
203	at once now, so that no longer lamenting in my heart
204	I waste my life, missing my own husband
205	in every excellence, since he was outstanding of the Achaeans.
> (narrator)
206	So speaking she went down from the shining upper rooms,
207	not alone. Two serving-women followed with her.
208	When the bright one of women reached the suitors
209	she stood beside the post of the well-made roof,
210	holding the shining veil across her cheeks.
211	A trusted serving-woman stood on each side.
212	Their knees were loosed right there, and desire charmed their heart,
213	and they all prayed to lie beside her in the bed.
214	She addressed Telemachus, her own son:
> Penelope
215	Telemachus, your wits are no longer fixed, nor your thought.
216	When you were still a child you were even more turning profits in your mind.
217	Now, when you are large and you reach the measure of youth,
218	and someone would say you were the child of a blessed man
219	looking at the size and the beauty, a foreign man,
220	your wits are no longer right, nor your thought.
221	Such a work as this has been done in the halls,
222	you who let the stranger be treated so unseemly.
223	How now, if some stranger sitting in our house
224	should suffer this way from a painful dragging?
225	Shame and outrage would be yours among men.
> (narrator)
226	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite her:
> Telemachus
227	Mother of mine, I do not blame you for being angry.
228	I think in my heart and I know each thing,
229	the good and the worse. Before I was still a child.
230	I cannot think all thoughtful things.
231	These men sitting beside me from one side and another strike me
232	thinking evils, and I do not have helpers.
233	The struggle of the stranger and Irus was not done
234	by the will of the suitors. He was better by force.
235	I wish, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
236	that so now the suitors in our house
237	would nod their heads broken, some in the courtyard,
238	some inside the house, and the limbs of each would be loosed,
239	as now that Irus sits at the courtyard doors
240	nodding his head, like a drunk man,
241	and he cannot stand upright on his feet or go
242	home, wherever his homecoming is, since his own limbs are loosed.
> (narrator)
243	So they spoke such things to one another.
244	Eurymachus addressed Penelope with words:
> Eurymachus
245	Daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope,
246	if all the Achaeans through Iasian Argos saw you,
247	more suitors would feast in your house
248	from dawn, since you are beyond women
249	in look and size and the wits equal inside.
> (narrator)
250	Then careful Penelope answered him:
> Penelope
251	Eurymachus, the deathless destroyed my excellence and look and body
252	when the Argives were going up to Ilium,
253	and my husband Odysseus was among them.
254	If that man coming were going about my living,
255	my fame would be greater and more beautiful that way.
256	Now I grieve. A god has driven so many evils on me.
257	When he was going, leaving the fatherland,
258	taking my right hand at the wrist he addressed me:
259	Wife, I do not think the well-greaved Achaeans
260	will all come home well unharmed from Troy.
261	They say the Trojans are fighting men,
262	both spear-throwers and drawers of arrows
263	and riders of swift-footed horses, who would most quickly
264	decide the great quarrel of common war.
265	Therefore I do not know whether a god will bring me back, or I will be taken
266	there in Troy. Let all things here be a care to you.
267	Remember father and mother in the halls
268	as now, or still more with me being away.
269	When you see the child bearded,
270	marry whoever you wish, leaving your house.
271	That man spoke so. All these things are now being finished.
272	There will be a night when the hated marriage will meet
273	me accursed, from whom Zeus took away wealth.
274	But this terrible pain reaches the heart and the spirit:
275	this was not the suitors' right before.
276	Those who wish to woo a good woman and the daughter of a rich man
277	and compete with one another,
278	those men themselves lead cattle and strong flocks,
279	a feast of the girl for their own people, and they give shining gifts.
280	They do not eat another's living without payment.
> (narrator)
281	So she spoke, and much-enduring godlike Odysseus was glad,
282	because she was drawing gifts from them, and she was charming the heart
283	with honeyed words, and her mind was desiring other things.
284	Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to her:
> Antinous
285	Daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope,
286	gifts, whoever of the Achaeans wishes to bring them here,
287	accept. It is not beautiful to refuse a giving.
288	We will not go to our works before, or anywhere else,
289	until you marry whichever of the Achaeans is best.
> (narrator)
290	So Antinous spoke, and the word pleased them.
291	Each sent a herald to bring gifts.
292	To Antinous he brought a great very beautiful robe,
293	many-colored. There were twelve pins in it, all
294	gold, fitted with well-bent clasps.
295	To Eurymachus he brought at once a much-worked necklace,
296	gold, strung with pieces of amber like the sun.
297	To Eurydamas two attendants brought earrings,
298	three-dropped, mulberry-like. Much grace shone from them.
299	From Peisander, lord, son of Polyctor,
300	an attendant brought a necklace, a very beautiful delight.
301	Each of the other Achaeans brought a beautiful gift.
302	Then the bright one of women went up to the upper rooms,
303	and the serving-women carried the very beautiful gifts with her.
304	They turning to dance and desirable song
305	were taking pleasure, and they waited for evening to come.
306	While they were taking pleasure black evening came on them.
307	At once they set three lamps in the halls
308	so they would shine. They set dry wood around,
309	long dry, very dry, newly split with bronze,
310	and they mixed in pine-torches. By turns
311	the serving-women of enduring Odysseus were lighting them. He
312	himself, Zeus-born Odysseus of many devices, spoke among them:
> Odysseus
313	Serving-women of Odysseus, the lord long gone,
314	go to the rooms where the respected queen is.
315	Turn the spindle-wool beside her, and delight her
316	sitting in the hall, or comb the wool with your hands.
317	I will hold out light for all these men.
318	Even if they wish to wait for well-throned Dawn,
319	they will not beat me. I am very much-enduring.
> (narrator)
320	So he spoke, and they laughed and looked at one another.
321	Fair-cheeked Melantho rebuked him shamefully,
322	whom Dolius bore, and Penelope raised her
323	and cherished her as a child, and used to give playthings to her heart.
324	Even so she did not have the grief of Penelope in her mind,
325	but she used to mix with Eurymachus and love him.
326	She rebuked Odysseus with insulting words:
> Melantho
327	Miserable stranger, you are someone struck out of his wits,
328	and you do not wish to go to the bronze-worker's house and sleep,
329	or somewhere to a talk-place, but here you say many things
330	daringly among many men, and you are not at all
331	afraid in your heart. Wine holds your wits, or always
332	such is your mind, and you also say empty things.
333	Are you crazy because you beat Irus the wanderer?
334	May some other better than Irus not soon stand up,
335	who striking you around the head with sturdy hands
336	would send you out of the house, mixing you with much blood.
> (narrator)
337	Looking from under his brows Odysseus of many devices spoke to her:
> Odysseus
338	Soon I will tell Telemachus, dog, such things as you say,
339	going there, so he may cut you through limb from limb right there.
> (narrator)
340	So speaking he scared the women through with words.
341	They went to go through the house, and the limbs of each were loosed
342	under them in fear. They thought he had told true things.
343	He stood beside the burning lamps holding light
344	looking at all of them. His heart was rushing
345	other things in his mind, which did not become unfinished.
346	Athena would not let the proud suitors at all
347	hold back from heart-painful outrage, so that still more
348	pain would go down into the heart of Odysseus son of Laertes.
349	Eurymachus, child of Polybus, began to speak among them,
350	cutting Odysseus, and he was making laughter for his companions:
> Eurymachus
351	Hear me, suitors of the very famous queen,
352	so I may say what the heart in my chest tells me.
353	Not without a god this man comes into the house of Odysseus.
354	Anyway it seems to me the shine of the torches is from him
355	down from the head, since there are no hairs on him, not even a few.
356	He spoke, and at the same time he addressed Odysseus sacker of cities:
357	Stranger, would you be willing to work for hire, if I took you,
358	at the far end of the country — the wage will be sure —
359	gathering field-walls and planting tall trees?
360	There I would hold out bread without failing,
361	and I would put clothes around you and give shoes for the feet.
362	But since you have learned bad works, you will not be willing
363	to go after work, but you want to crouch through the people,
364	so you may have to feed your unsated belly.
> (narrator)
365	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
366	Eurymachus, I wish a strife of work would come for the two of us
367	in the spring season, when the days become long,
368	in the grass, and I would have a well-bent sickle,
369	and you would have such a one, so we might try the work
370	fasting until very dark, and there would be grass.
371	If again there were also cattle to drive, the best,
372	tawny, large, both well-fed on grass,
373	of the same age, equal-bearing, whose strength is not weak,
374	and there were a four-acre field, and the clod would yield under the plow,
375	then you would see me, whether I would cut a continuous furrow in front.
376	If again the son of Cronus also started a war from somewhere
377	today, and I had a shield and two spears
378	and a helmet all bronze, fitted on the temples,
379	then you would see me mixing among the first in the front-fighters,
380	and you would not speak blaming me for the belly.
381	You have much violence, and your mind is harsh.
382	You think you are great and strong somewhere,
383	because you mix with few and not good men.
384	If Odysseus came and reached his fatherland,
385	soon the doorposts, even being very wide,
386	would be narrow for you fleeing out through the doorway to the door.
> (narrator)
387	So he spoke, and Eurymachus was more angry in his heart,
388	and looking from under his brows he addressed winged words to him:
> Eurymachus
389	Ah wretched, I will soon finish an evil for you, such things as you say
390	daringly among many men, and you are not at all
391	afraid in your heart. Wine holds your wits, or always
392	such is your mind, and you also say empty things.
393	Are you crazy because you beat Irus the wanderer?
> (narrator)
394	So speaking he took a stool. Odysseus
395	sat at the knees of Amphinomus of Dulichium,
396	afraid of Eurymachus. He hit the wine-pourer's
397	right hand. The pitcher fell to the ground booming,
398	and he groaning fell on his back in the dust.
399	The suitors made a noise through the shadowy halls,
400	and so one would say looking at another near:
> a suitor
401	I wish the stranger had been lost wandering elsewhere
402	before he came. He would not have put so much noise among us.
403	Now we are quarreling about beggars, and there will be no
404	pleasure of a good feast, since the worse things win.
> (narrator)
405	The holy force of Telemachus also spoke among them:
> Telemachus
406	Strange men, you are raging and you no longer hide in your heart
407	food or drink. Some one of the gods is stirring you.
408	Dine well and lie down going home,
409	whenever the heart tells you. I drive no one.
> (narrator)
410	So he spoke, and they all growing with their teeth in their lips
411	wondered at Telemachus, who spoke daringly.
412	Amphinomus spoke to them and said among them,
413	the shining son of Nisus, lord son of Aretias:
> Amphinomus
414	Friends, no one would be angry laying hold with opposing words
415	after a thing said justly.
416	Do not drive the stranger or any other
417	of the slaves who are through the house of godlike Odysseus.
418	Come, let the wine-pourer begin with the cups,
419	so that pouring a libation we may lie down going home.
420	Let us leave the stranger in the halls of Odysseus
421	to be a care to Telemachus. He came to his own house.
> (narrator)
422	So he spoke, and he said a word pleasing to all of them.
423	The hero Moulius mixed a mixing-bowl for them,
424	a Dulichian herald. He was an attendant of Amphinomus.
425	He distributed to all standing by. They
426	pouring a libation to the blessed gods drank honey-sweet wine.
427	When they had poured and drunk as much as the heart wished,
428	they went to go lying down, each to his own house.
