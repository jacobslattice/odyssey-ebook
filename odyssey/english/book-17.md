# Odyssey Book 17

> (narrator)
1	When early-born Dawn appeared, the rose-fingered one,
2	then Telemachus, own son of godlike Odysseus,
3	bound beautiful sandals under his feet
4	and took a strong spear that fitted his palms,
5	eager for the town, and he spoke to his swineherd:
> Telemachus
6	Atta, I am going to the city, so my mother
7	may see me. I do not think she will stop before
8	the hateful weeping and the tearful lament
9	until she sees me myself. I give you this charge.
10	Lead the poor stranger to the city, so there
11	he may beg his supper. Whoever wants will give him
12	a bit of bread and a cup. I cannot somehow
13	endure all people, even though I have pains in my heart.
14	If the stranger is very angry, it will be more painful
15	for him. It is dear to me to tell true things.
> (narrator)
16	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
17	Friend, I myself do not want to be held back.
18	It is better for a beggar through the city than through the country
19	to beg his supper. Whoever wants will give me it.
20	I am no longer of an age to stay at the steadings
21	so as to obey everything a commander tells me.
22	Go. This man you tell will lead me
23	at once when I have warmed at the fire and there is warmth.
24	I have these bad clothes terribly. Let the morning frost
25	not overcome me. He said the town is far.
> (narrator)
26	So he spoke, and Telemachus went through the steading,
27	stepping quickly with his feet, and he was planting evils for the suitors.
28	When he reached the well-lived house
29	he set the spear, carrying it, against a tall pillar
30	and he himself went inside and crossed the stone threshold.
31	The nurse Eurycleia saw him far the first,
32	spreading fleeces on the patterned thrones.
33	She wept and then went straight. The other
34	serving-women of enduring Odysseus gathered around
35	and they kissed him, embracing his head and shoulders.
36	Careful Penelope came from the room,
37	like Artemis or golden Aphrodite,
38	and she threw her arms around her own child, weeping,
39	and she kissed his head and both his beautiful eyes,
40	and lamenting she said winged words:
> Penelope
41	You have come, Telemachus, sweet light. I no longer
42	thought I would see you, since you went in a ship to Pylos
43	in secret, against my will, after news of your own father.
44	But come, tell me how you met with a sight of him.
> (narrator)
45	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite her:
> Telemachus
46	Mother of mine, do not raise a lament for me and do not
47	stir the heart in my chest, even though I have escaped sheer destruction.
48	Wash, take clean clothes on your skin,
49	go up to the upper rooms with serving-women
50	and pray to all the gods that you will make perfect hecatombs
51	if somehow Zeus finishes works of payment.
52	I will go into the assembly, so I may call
53	a stranger who followed me from there coming here.
54	I sent him forward with the godlike companions,
55	and I told Peiraeus, leading him to his house,
56	to love him kindly and honor him until I come.
> (narrator)
57	So he spoke, and the word was wingless for her.
58	She washed, took clean clothes on her skin,
59	and she prayed to all the gods that she would make perfect hecatombs
60	if somehow Zeus finishes works of payment.
61	Then Telemachus went out through the hall
62	holding a spear. Two swift dogs followed with him.
63	Athena poured an unearthly grace on him.
64	All the people wondered at him as he came.
65	The proud suitors gathered around him
66	speaking good things, and they were plotting evils deep in their minds.
67	He then avoided their great crowd,
68	but where Mentor sat, and Antiphas and Halitherses,
69	who were his father's companions from the beginning,
70	he went and sat there. They asked each thing.
71	Peiraeus famous for the spear came near them
72	leading the stranger to the assembly through the city. Telemachus
73	did not turn far from the stranger any longer, but he stood beside him.
74	Peiraeus spoke a word to him first:
> Peiraeus
75	Telemachus, quickly urge the women to my house,
76	so I may send you the gifts Menelaus gave you.
> (narrator)
77	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
78	Peiraeus, we do not know how these works will be.
79	If the proud suitors in the halls
80	kill me in secret and divide all my father's things,
81	I want you yourself to have the use of them, rather than one of these.
82	If I plant death and doom for these men,
83	then bring them to the house glad, and I will be glad.
> (narrator)
84	So speaking he led the much-tried stranger to the house.
85	When they reached the well-lived house
86	they put their cloaks down on the couches and thrones
87	and going into well-polished bathtubs they washed.
88	When the serving-women had washed them and anointed them with oil
89	and thrown thick cloaks and tunics around them,
90	they came out of the bathtubs and sat on the couches.
91	A serving-woman brought water for the hands and poured it from a pitcher,
92	a beautiful gold one, over a silver basin,
93	to wash. She drew a polished table beside them.
94	The respected housekeeper brought and set out bread,
95	putting many dishes on it, giving freely of what was there.
96	The mother sat opposite beside the pillar of the hall
97	leaning on a couch, turning the fine spindle-wool.
98	They reached out their hands to the food lying ready.
99	When they had put away the desire for drink and food,
100	careful Penelope began the words:
> Penelope
101	Telemachus, I will go up to the upper room
102	and lie in the bed that has been made groaning for me,
103	always mixed with my tears, since Odysseus
104	went with the sons of Atreus to Ilium. You did not dare,
105	before the proud suitors come to this house,
106	to say clearly the homecoming of your father, if you heard it somewhere.
> (narrator)
107	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite her:
> Telemachus
108	I will tell you the truth, mother.
109	We went to Pylos and to Nestor, shepherd of the people.
110	He received me in his high halls
111	and loved me kindly, as a father his own son
112	coming late, new from elsewhere. So that man
113	cared for me kindly with his famous sons.
114	Of enduring Odysseus he said he had never
115	heard from anyone on the earth, living or dead.
116	He sent me forward to the son of Atreus, Menelaus famous for the spear,
117	with horses and fitted chariots.
118	There I saw Argive Helen, for whose sake many
119	Argives and Trojans suffered by the will of the gods.
120	At once then Menelaus, good at the war-cry, asked
121	wanting what I had come to holy Lacedaemon.
122	I told him the whole truth.
123	Then he answered me with words:
124	Oh, very much they wanted to lie in the bed of a strong-minded man,
125	being cowards themselves.
126	As when in a thicket a doe of a strong lion
127	has put her newborn milk-sucking fawns to sleep
128	and searches the ridges and the grassy glens
129	grazing, and he then comes into his own bed
130	and puts an unseemly fate on both of them,
131	so Odysseus will put an unseemly fate on those men.
132	I wish, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
133	that being such as he once was in well-built Lesbos
134	when he stood up from a quarrel and wrestled Philomeleides
135	and threw him down strongly, and all the Achaeans were glad,
136	being such Odysseus would mix with the suitors.
137	They would all become short-lived and bitter-married.
138	These things you ask me and beg, I would not
139	say other things besides, turning aside, and I will not deceive.
140	What the old man of the sea who does not miss told me,
141	of those I will hide no word from you and I will not conceal it.
142	He said he had seen him on an island having hard pains,
143	in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who holds him
144	by force. He cannot reach his own fatherland.
145	He does not have oared ships and companions
146	who would send him over the wide back of the sea.
147	So the son of Atreus spoke, Menelaus famous for the spear.
148	When I had finished these things I came home. The deathless
149	gave me a wind, who sent me quickly to my own fatherland.
> (narrator)
150	So he spoke, and he stirred the heart in her chest.
151	Godlike Theoclymenus also spoke among them:
> Theoclymenus
152	Respected wife of Odysseus, son of Laertes,
153	he does not know it clearly. Put together my word.
154	I will prophesy to you exactly and I will not hide it.
155	Let Zeus be witness first of the gods, and the guest-table
156	and the hearth of faultless Odysseus, which I have reached,
157	that Odysseus is already in his fatherland,
158	sitting or creeping, learning these bad works.
159	He is, and he is planting evil for all the suitors.
160	Such a bird-sign I marked sitting on the well-benched ship
161	and I called it out to Telemachus.
> (narrator)
162	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
163	I wish this word, stranger, were finished.
164	Then you would soon know friendship and many gifts
165	from me, so that someone meeting you would call you blessed.
> (narrator)
166	So they spoke such things to one another,
167	and the suitors in front of the hall of Odysseus
168	were taking pleasure with discs and throwing javelins
169	on the worked floor, where they had violence before.
170	When it was supper-time and the flocks came
171	from the country on every side, the men who used to brought them,
172	then Medon spoke to them. He most
173	pleased them of the heralds, and he was with them at the feast:
> Medon
174	Young men, since you have all delighted your minds in the contests,
175	go to the house, so we may make the feast.
176	It is no worse to take supper in season.
> (narrator)
177	So he spoke, and they stood up and went and obeyed the word.
178	When they reached the well-lived house
179	they put their cloaks down on the couches and thrones,
180	and they sacrificed great sheep and fat goats,
181	and they sacrificed fat hogs and a herd cow,
182	making the feast. From the country toward the city
183	Odysseus and the godlike swineherd were being urged to go.
184	The swineherd, leader of men, began the words:
> Eumaeus
185	Stranger, since you then want to go to the city
186	today, as my lord charged — I would have
187	wanted to leave you here as a keeper of the steading.
188	But I respect him and I am afraid he may afterward
189	quarrel with me. The shouts of lords are hard —
190	but come now, let us go. The day has gone
191	very much, and soon toward evening it will be colder for you.
> (narrator)
192	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
193	I know, I understand. You tell these things to one who already thinks them.
194	Let us go, and you lead all the way.
195	Give me, if you have a cut stick somewhere,
196	to lean on, since you said the way is very slippery.
> (narrator)
197	He spoke and threw the unseemly bag around his shoulders,
198	torn close, and there was a twisted strap on it.
199	Eumaeus gave him a staff that pleased his heart.
200	The two went, and the dogs and the herder men
201	guarded the steading, staying behind. He led his lord to the city
202	like a wretched beggar and an old man,
203	leaning on a staff. He wore wretched clothes on his skin.
204	When going along the rugged road
205	they were near the town and they reached a fountain
206	worked, fair-flowing, from which the townsmen drew water,
207	which Ithacus and Neritus and Polyctor had made.
208	Around it there was a grove of water-fed poplars,
209	circular all around, and cold water ran down
210	from above out of a rock. An altar was built above
211	of the nymphs, where all travelers used to make offerings.
212	There Melantheus, son of Dolius, met them
213	leading goats, which stood out among all the herds,
214	a supper for the suitors. Two herders followed with him.
215	Seeing them he quarreled and spoke a word and called them by name,
216	terribly and unseemly. He stirred the heart of Odysseus:
> Melanthius
217	Now very much a bad man leads a bad man,
218	as a god always leads like to like.
219	Where are you taking this glutton, unenviable swineherd,
220	a beggar, a pain, a spoiler of feasts?
221	who will stand at many doorposts and rub his shoulders,
222	begging scraps, not swords or basins.
223	If you gave him to me to be a keeper of the steading
224	and to be a dung-sweeper and to carry young shoots for the kids,
225	even drinking whey he would put on a great thigh.
226	But since he has learned bad works, he will not be willing
227	to go after work, but crouching through the people
228	he wants by begging to feed his unsated belly.
229	I will tell you, and this will also be finished:
230	if he comes to the house of godlike Odysseus,
231	many stools from the palms of men around his head
232	will rub his ribs, as he is thrown through the house.
> (narrator)
233	So he spoke, and going by he kicked him in his mindlessness
234	on the hip. He did not drive him off the path,
235	but he stayed firm. Odysseus considered
236	whether rushing he should take the life out of him with the stick,
237	or drive his head to the ground, lifting him by both ears.
238	He endured it, and he held himself in his mind. The swineherd
239	quarreled looking him in the face, and he prayed greatly, holding up his hands:
> Eumaeus
240	Nymphs of the fountain, daughters of Zeus, if ever Odysseus
241	burned thigh-bones for you, covering them with rich fat,
242	of lambs and kids, fulfill this wish for me,
243	that that man come, and a god bring him.
244	Then he would scatter all your splendors,
245	which you now carry in violence, always wandering
246	through the town. Bad herders ruin the flocks.
> (narrator)
247	Then Melanthius the goatherd spoke to him:
> Melanthius
248	Oh, what the dog has said, knowing destructive things,
249	whom I will someday take on a well-benched black ship
250	far from Ithaca, so he may earn me much living.
251	I wish silver-bowed Apollo would hit Telemachus
252	today in the halls, or he be broken under the suitors,
253	as Odysseus's day of homecoming has already been lost far away.
> (narrator)
254	So speaking he left them going gently there,
255	and he went, and very quickly he reached the lord's house.
256	At once he went inside and sat among the suitors,
257	opposite Eurymachus. He loved him most.
258	The men working set a portion of meats beside him,
259	and the respected housekeeper brought and set out bread
260	to eat. Near, Odysseus and the godlike swineherd
261	stood coming, and the voice of the hollow lyre
262	came around them. Phemius was throwing a song among them.
263	He took the swineherd's hand and spoke to him:
> Odysseus
264	Eumaeus, these are very beautiful, the house of Odysseus,
265	and they are easily known even among many to see.
266	One thing is on another, and the courtyard is fitted
267	with a wall and coping, and the doors are well-fenced
268	double. No man would outfight it.
269	I know that many men in it set a feast,
270	since the savor has gone up, and in it the lyre
271	is calling, which the gods made a companion of the feast.
> (narrator)
272	You answered him, swineherd Eumaeus:
> Eumaeus
273	You knew it easily, since you are not mindless in the other things either.
274	But come, let us consider how these works will be.
275	You go first into the well-lived house
276	and go among the suitors, and I will stay behind here.
277	If you wish, wait, and I will go in front.
278	Do not delay, so that someone seeing you outside
279	does not throw or drive you. I tell you to think of these things.
> (narrator)
280	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus answered him:
> Odysseus
281	I know, I understand. You tell these things to one who already thinks them.
282	Go in front, and I will stay behind here.
283	I am not unused to blows or throws.
284	My heart is daring, since I have suffered many bad things
285	in waves and in war. Let this also be among those.
286	There is no way to hide a belly that is eager,
287	accursed, which gives many evils to men,
288	for whose sake well-yoked ships are also fitted
289	over the barren sea, bringing evils to enemies.
> (narrator)
290	So they spoke such things to one another.
291	A dog lying lifted his head and ears,
292	Argos, of enduring Odysseus, whom he himself once
293	raised, and he had no use of him, but before that he went to holy Ilium.
294	Before, the young men used to take him
295	after wild goats and deer and hares.
296	Then he lay thrown away, his lord gone,
297	in much dung, which in front of the doors
298	of mules and cattle was piled enough, until
299	the slaves of Odysseus would take it to manure the great estate.
300	There the dog Argos lay, full of dog-ticks.
301	Then, when he marked Odysseus near,
302	he wagged with his tail and dropped both ears,
303	but he could no longer come nearer
304	to his own lord. Odysseus looking aside wiped away a tear,
305	easily escaping Eumaeus, and at once he asked with a word:
> Odysseus
306	Eumaeus, this is a great wonder, this dog lying in the dung.
307	He is beautiful in body, but this I do not know clearly,
308	whether he was also swift to run besides this look,
309	or only such as the table-dogs of men
310	become. Lords care for them for the sake of splendor.
> (narrator)
311	You answered him, swineherd Eumaeus:
> Eumaeus
312	This is very much the dog of a man who has died far away.
313	If he were such in body and in works
314	as Odysseus left him going to Troy,
315	you would soon wonder seeing the speed and the strength.
316	No beast used to escape him in the deep depths of the wood
317	that he chased. He was also knowing in tracks.
318	Now he is held in badness, and his lord elsewhere than his fatherland
319	is lost, and the careless women do not care for him.
320	Slaves, when lords no longer hold power,
321	are then no longer willing to work what is right.
322	Wide-voiced Zeus takes away half the excellence
323	of a man, when the slave day takes him.
> (narrator)
324	So speaking he went into the well-lived house
325	and went straight to the hall among the shining suitors.
326	Fate of black death took Argos,
327	at once, seeing Odysseus in the twentieth year.
328	Godlike Telemachus saw him first by far
329	coming through the house, the swineherd, and quickly then
330	he nodded, calling him to himself. Looking around he took a chair
331	lying where the carver used to sit cutting many meats
332	for the suitors feasting through the house.
333	He brought it and set it at Telemachus's table
334	opposite, and he sat there himself. A herald
335	taking a portion set it and lifting bread from the basket.
336	Near after him Odysseus went into the house,
337	like a wretched beggar and an old man,
338	leaning on a staff. He wore wretched clothes on his skin.
339	He sat on the ash threshold inside the doors,
340	leaning on a cypress post, which a carpenter once
341	had planed skillfully and straightened to the line.
342	Telemachus calling him to himself spoke to the swineherd,
343	taking a whole loaf from the very beautiful basket
344	and meat, as much as his hands held throwing it around:
> Telemachus
345	Give these to the stranger, taking them, and tell him himself
346	to beg going among all the suitors.
347	Shame is not a good thing to be beside a man in need.
> (narrator)
348	So he spoke, and the swineherd went when he heard the word,
349	and standing near he spoke winged words:
> Eumaeus
350	Telemachus gives you these, stranger, and he tells you
351	to beg going among all the suitors.
352	He says shame is not a good thing for a begging man.
> (narrator)
353	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
354	Lord Zeus, may Telemachus be blessed among men,
355	and may all things come to him that he desires in his mind.
> (narrator)
356	He spoke and took them with both hands and set them
357	there in front of his feet, on the unseemly bag,
358	and he ate while the singer sang in the halls.
359	When he had eaten, the godlike singer stopped.
360	The suitors made a noise through the halls. Athena
361	standing near Odysseus, son of Laertes,
362	urged him to gather bits among the suitors,
363	and to know which of them are right and which lawless.
364	But even so she was not going to keep any of them from badness.
365	He went to ask each man from the right,
366	stretching out his hand everywhere, as if he had long been a beggar.
367	They gave pitying him, and they wondered at him,
368	and they asked one another who he was and from where he had come.
369	Melanthius the goatherd also spoke among them:
> Melanthius
370	Hear me, suitors of the very famous queen,
371	about this stranger. I have seen him before.
372	The swineherd led him here,
373	but I do not know clearly himself from where he claims his birth is.
> (narrator)
374	So he spoke, and Antinous quarreled with the swineherd in words:
> Antinous
375	Very well-known swineherd, why did you
376	bring this man to the city? Are there not enough other wanderers for us,
377	painful beggars, spoilers of feasts?
378	Do you blame it that they eat up the lord's living
379	gathering here, and you have also called this one besides?
> (narrator)
380	You answered him, swineherd Eumaeus:
> Eumaeus
381	Antinous, you do not speak well, being also a good man.
382	Who himself going elsewhere calls another
383	stranger, except those who are public workers,
384	a seer or a healer of evils or a carpenter of woods,
385	or even a inspired singer, who would delight singing?
386	These are the invited of mortals over the boundless earth.
387	No one would call a beggar to wear himself out.
388	But you are always hard beyond all the suitors
389	to the slaves of Odysseus, and again beyond to me. I
390	do not care, as long as thoughtful Penelope
391	lives in the halls and godlike Telemachus.
> (narrator)
392	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
393	Be quiet. Do not answer this man many words for me.
394	Antinous is used always to stir badly
395	with hard words, and he urges the others too.
396	He spoke and addressed winged words to Antinous:
397	Antinous, you care for me well, like a father for a son,
398	you who tell the stranger to be driven from the hall
399	with a forced word. May a god not finish this.
400	Take and give to him. I do not begrudge you. I tell you.
401	Do not then respect my mother in this, or any other
402	of the slaves who are through the house of godlike Odysseus.
403	But you do not have such a thought in your chest.
404	You yourself want much more to eat than to give to another.
> (narrator)
405	Then Antinous answering spoke to him:
> Antinous
406	Telemachus high-talker, uncheckable in spirit, what have you said.
407	If all the suitors would hold out so much to him,
408	the house would keep him away for three months.
> (narrator)
409	So he spoke, and taking the stool he showed it from under the table
410	lying, with which he held his shining feet feasting.
411	All the others gave, and they filled the bag
412	with bread and meats. Soon Odysseus was going
413	going back to the threshold to taste the gift of the Achaeans.
414	He stood beside Antinous and spoke a word to him:
> Odysseus
415	Give, friend. You do not seem to me the worst of the Achaeans
416	to be, but the best, since you are like a king.
417	Therefore you should give even better than the others
418	of bread. I would make you famous over the boundless earth.
419	I too once lived a house among men,
420	blessed, rich, and I often used to give to a wanderer,
421	such as he was and needing whatever he came for.
422	There were very countless slaves and many other things
423	with which they live well and are called rich.
424	But Zeus son of Cronus wasted it — he wanted it somewhere —
425	who sent me with much-wandering raiders
426	to go to Egypt, a long road, so I would be lost.
427	I stood the double-curved ships in the river of Egypt.
428	There I told the faithful companions
429	to stay there beside the ships and guard the ships,
430	and I urged scouts to go to the lookouts.
431	They, yielding to violence, following their own spirit,
432	very quickly were ruining the very beautiful fields of Egyptian men
433	and they were leading out women and young children
434	and killing the men. Soon the cry reached the city.
435	They hearing the shout at the appearing dawn
436	came. The whole plain was filled with footmen and horses
437	and the flash of bronze. Zeus who delights in the thunderbolt
438	threw a bad panic among my companions, and no one dared
439	to stand opposite. Evils stood around from every side.
440	There they killed many of us with sharp bronze,
441	and they led others living, to work for them by force.
442	Me they gave to a stranger who met them, to Cyprus,
443	Dmetor son of Iasus, who ruled Cyprus by force.
444	From there I now come here suffering pains.
> (narrator)
445	Then Antinous answered him and spoke:
> Antinous
446	What god has brought this pain, a pain of the feast?
447	Stand so in the middle, away from my table,
448	so you do not soon reach bitter Egypt and Cyprus.
449	How daring and shameless you are, a beggar.
450	You stand beside all in turn. They give
451	in vain, since there is no holding back or pity
452	to give freely of others' things, since there is much beside each.
> (narrator)
453	Drawing back, Odysseus of many devices spoke to him:
> Odysseus
454	Oh, then there were not also wits on your look.
455	You would not even give salt from your own house to your own attendant,
456	you who now sitting beside others' things have not dared
457	to take bread away and give it to me. There is much here.
> (narrator)
458	So he spoke, and Antinous was more angry in his heart,
459	and looking from under his brows he addressed winged words to him:
> Antinous
460	Now I do not think you will go back well through the hall
461	withdrawing, when you also throw insults.
> (narrator)
462	So he spoke, and taking the stool he threw it at the right shoulder,
463	at the very base along the back. He stood like a rock
464	fixed, and the missile of Antinous did not trip him,
465	but silently he moved his head, plotting evils deep.
466	Going back to the threshold he sat, and he set the bag
467	down well-filled, and he spoke among the suitors:
> Odysseus
468	Hear me, suitors of the very famous queen,
469	so I may say what the heart in my chest tells me.
470	There is no pain in the mind nor any grief
471	when a man fighting about his own possessions
472	is hit, either about cattle or white sheep.
473	But Antinous hit me for the sake of a wretched belly,
474	accursed, which gives many evils to men.
475	If somewhere there are gods of beggars and Erinyes,
476	may the end of death reach Antinous before marriage.
> (narrator)
477	Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to him:
> Antinous
478	Eat in peace, stranger, sitting, or go away elsewhere,
479	so the young men do not drag you through the house, such things as you say,
480	by the foot or even the hand, and strip everything off.
> (narrator)
481	So he spoke, and they all were very angry at the outrage.
482	So one of the overbearing young men would say:
> a suitor
483	Antinous, you did not throw well at the miserable wanderer,
484	accursed, if somewhere there is some god in heaven.
485	The gods also, like foreign strangers,
486	becoming all kinds, turn through the cities
487	watching the violence and the good order of men.
> (narrator)
488	So the suitors spoke, and he did not care about the words.
489	Telemachus grew a great grief in his heart
490	for the man hit, and he did not throw a tear to the ground from his eyelids,
491	but silently he moved his head, plotting evils deep.
492	When careful Penelope heard him
493	hit in the hall, she spoke among the serving-women:
> Penelope
494	I wish famous-bowed Apollo would so hit you yourself.
> (narrator)
495	Then Eurynome the housekeeper spoke a word to her:
> Eurynome
496	I wish an end would come on our curses.
497	None of these would reach well-throned Dawn.
> (narrator)
498	Then careful Penelope spoke to her:
> Penelope
499	Nurse, they are all enemies, since they contrive evils.
500	Antinous most is like black death-fate.
501	Some miserable stranger is wandering through the house
502	begging men. Need drives him.
503	There all the others filled him and gave,
504	and this man hit the base of the right shoulder with a stool.
> (narrator)
505	So she spoke among the serving-women,
506	sitting in the room. Godlike Odysseus was eating.
507	Calling him to herself she addressed the godlike swineherd:
> Penelope
508	Go, godlike Eumaeus, going tell the stranger
509	to come, so I may greet him and ask him
510	if he has heard of enduring Odysseus somewhere
511	or seen him with his eyes. He is like a much-wandered man.
> (narrator)
512	You answered her, swineherd Eumaeus:
> Eumaeus
513	I wish the Achaeans would be silent for you, queen.
514	Such things as he tells, your own heart would be charmed.
515	I held him three nights, and I kept him three days
516	in the hut. He reached me first, running from a ship.
517	He has not yet finished the badness that he tells.
518	As when a man looks at a singer, who from the gods
519	has learned and sings desirable words to mortals,
520	and they are eager without tiring to hear him whenever he sings,
521	so that man charmed me sitting beside me in the halls.
522	He says he is a guest-friend of Odysseus's father,
523	living in Crete, where the birth of Minos is.
524	From there he now came here suffering pains,
525	rolling on and on. He claims to have heard of Odysseus
526	near, in the rich people of Thesprotian men,
527	alive. He is bringing many stored things to his own house.
> (narrator)
528	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
529	Go, call him here, so he may tell it himself opposite.
530	These men can play sitting at the doors
531	or here through the house, since their heart is cheerful.
532	Their own possessions lie untouched in the house,
533	bread and sweet wine. The household men eat those,
534	and these coming to ours all the days,
535	sacrificing cattle and sheep and fat goats,
536	feast and drink flaming wine
537	in vain. Many things are used up. There is no man any longer
538	such as Odysseus was, to ward a curse from the house.
539	If Odysseus came and reached his fatherland,
540	soon with his child he would pay back the violences of the men.
> (narrator)
541	So she spoke, and Telemachus sneezed greatly, and around the house
542	it rang terribly. Penelope laughed,
543	and at once she addressed winged words to Eumaeus:
> Penelope
544	Go for me, call the stranger here opposite.
545	Do you not see that my son sneezed at all the words?
546	So death would not be unfinished for the suitors
547	all of them, and no one would escape death and the fates.
548	I will tell you another thing, and you put it in your mind.
549	If I know him telling all things that do not miss,
550	I will put a cloak and a tunic on him, beautiful clothes.
> (narrator)
551	So she spoke, and the swineherd went when he heard the word,
552	and standing near he spoke winged words:
> Eumaeus
553	Father stranger, careful Penelope calls you,
554	the mother of Telemachus. Her heart tells her to ask something
555	about her husband, even having suffered cares.
556	If she knows you telling all things that do not miss,
557	she will put a cloak and a tunic on you, which you most
558	need. Bread, also begging through the people,
559	you will feed your belly. Whoever wishes will give to you.
> (narrator)
560	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus spoke to him:
> Odysseus
561	Eumaeus, I would soon tell all things that do not miss
562	to the daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope.
563	I know well about that man, and we took on the same misery.
564	But I am afraid under the crowd of the hard suitors,
565	whose violence and force reach iron heaven.
566	Even now, when this man going through the house
567	hit me doing no evil and gave me to pains,
568	neither Telemachus helped that nor anyone else.
569	Therefore now tell Penelope in the halls
570	to wait, though she is eager, until the sun goes down.
571	Then let her ask me about the husband's day of homecoming,
572	sitting nearer beside the fire. I have
573	wretched clothes. You yourself know, since I first begged you.
> (narrator)
574	So he spoke, and the swineherd went when he heard the word.
575	Penelope addressed him as he went over the threshold:
> Penelope
576	You are not bringing him, Eumaeus. What did this wanderer think?
577	Is he afraid of someone extraordinary, or otherwise
578	is he ashamed through the house? A wanderer who is ashamed is a bad one.
> (narrator)
579	You answered her, swineherd Eumaeus:
> Eumaeus
580	He tells it according to portion, what another would also think,
581	avoiding the violence of overbearing men.
582	He tells you to wait until the sun goes down.
583	It is also much more beautiful for you yourself, queen,
584	alone to speak a word to the stranger and to hear.
> (narrator)
585	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
586	The stranger is not mindless. He thinks how it would be.
587	There are not somewhere men of mortal humans
588	who so have violence, contriving reckless things.
> (narrator)
589	So she spoke, and the godlike swineherd went
590	to the crowd of the suitors, when he had told all things.
591	At once he addressed winged words to Telemachus,
592	holding his head near, so the others would not learn:
> Eumaeus
593	Dear, I am going away, to guard the pigs and those things,
594	your living and mine. Let all things here be a care to you.
595	Save yourself first, and think in your heart
596	that you suffer nothing. Many of the Achaeans think evils,
597	whom may Zeus destroy before it becomes a pain for us.
> (narrator)
598	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
599	It will be so, atta. You go after your afternoon meal.
600	At dawn go and bring beautiful sacrificial animals.
601	These things will be a care to me and to the deathless.
> (narrator)
602	So he spoke, and he sat again on the well-polished chair,
603	and filling his heart with food and drink
604	he went to go after the pigs, and he left the enclosures and the hall
605	full of diners. They were taking pleasure in dance and song.
606	The afternoon day had already come.
