# Odyssey Book 6

> (narrator)
1	So he slept there, much-enduring godlike Odysseus,
2	worn down by sleep and toil. But Athena
3	went to the land and city of the Phaeacian men,
4	who once before lived in wide-spaced Hypereia,
5	near the Cyclopes, men of over-pride,
6	who used to harm them, and were stronger in force.
7	From there godlike Nausithous made them rise and led them,
8	and settled them in Scheria, far from men who work for their bread,
9	and he drove a wall around the city, and built houses,
10	and made temples of the gods, and divided the plowlands.
11	But he had already been overcome by death and gone to Hades,
12	and Alcinous was then king, who knew designs from the gods.
13	To his house the goddess went, owl-eyed Athena,
14	planning a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus.
15	She went to go into the richly-worked chamber, where a girl
16	was sleeping, like the immortals in build and in look,
17	Nausicaa, daughter of great-hearted Alcinous,
18	and beside her two serving-women, who had beauty from the Graces,
19	on either side of the doorposts. And shining doors were shut on it.
20	And she rushed to the girl's bed like a breath of wind,
21	and stood over her head, and spoke a word to her,
22	likened to the daughter of Dymas, famous for ships,
23	who was of her same age, and was dear to her heart.
24	Likening herself to that girl, owl-eyed Athena addressed her:
> Athena
25	Nausicaa, why has your mother borne you so careless?
26	Your shining clothes lie neglected,
27	and your marriage is near, when you yourself must wear beautiful things
28	and give others to those who will lead you away.
29	For from these things a good report goes up among people,
30	and your father and honored mother rejoice.
31	But let us go washing when dawn appears.
32	And I will go with you as a fellow-worker, so that as quickly as possible
33	you may get ready, since you will not still be a girl much longer.
34	Already the best men throughout the land are courting you,
35	of all the Phaeacians, where your own family is too.
36	But come, urge your famous father at first light
37	to fit out mules and a wagon, which will carry
38	sashes and robes and shining blankets.
39	And this way is much finer for you yourself than going
40	on foot. For the washing-troughs are far from the city.
> (narrator)
41	So speaking, owl-eyed Athena went away
42	to Olympus, where they say the gods' seat is always sure.
43	It is never shaken by winds, nor is it ever wet
44	with rain, nor does snow come near it, but a very clear sky
45	is spread cloudless, and a white brightness has run over it.
46	In that the blessed gods take their pleasure all their days.
47	There owl-eyed Athena went, when she had made it clear to the girl.
48	At once Dawn of the fine throne came, who woke her,
49	Nausicaa of the fine robe. And at once she marveled at the dream,
50	and she went through the house, so she might tell her parents,
51	her dear father and her mother. She found them inside.
52	Her mother sat at the hearth with the serving-women
53	turning the sea-purple distaff-wool. She met him going outdoors
54	as he was going outdoors among the famous kings
55	to the council, where the proud Phaeacians were calling him.
56	Standing very near she addressed her dear father:
> Nausicaa
57	Dear papa, would you fit out a wagon for me,
58	high and well-wheeled, so I may take the famous clothes
59	to the river to wash them, the ones lying dirty on me?
60	And it is fitting for you yourself, being among the first,
61	to take counsel wearing clean clothes on your skin.
62	Five dear sons of yours have been born in the halls,
63	two of them married, three flourishing as unmarried young men.
64	They always want, wearing newly washed clothes,
65	to go to the dance. All of this is a care in my mind.
> (narrator)
66	So she spoke. She was ashamed to name her ripe marriage
67	to her dear father. He understood it all and answered with a word:
> Alcinous
68	I do not grudge you the mules, child, nor any other thing.
69	Go. The house-workers will fit out a wagon for you,
70	high and well-wheeled, fitted with a body on top.
> (narrator)
71	So speaking he called to the house-workers, and they obeyed.
72	Outside they fitted a well-wheeled mule-wagon,
73	led the mules under, and yoked them to the wagon.
74	The girl brought shining clothing out of the chamber.
75	She laid it down on the well-polished wagon,
76	and her mother put heart-pleasing food in a chest,
77	all kinds, and put in cooked meats, and poured wine
78	into a goatskin bag. The girl stepped up onto the wagon.
79	She gave her liquid oil in a golden flask,
80	so she could anoint herself with the serving-women.
81	She took the whip and the shining reins,
82	and whipped them to go. There was a clatter from the two mules.
83	They stretched on without stopping, and carried the clothes and her,
84	not her alone: other serving-women went with her as well.
85	And when they came to the beautiful stream of the river,
86	where the washing-troughs were unfailing, and much water,
87	beautiful, ran out from below, enough to clean even very dirty things,
88	there they unyoked the mules and let them go from the wagon.
89	They drove them along the swirling river
90	to crop honey-sweet grass. And the girls from the wagon
91	took the clothes in their hands and carried them into the dark water,
92	and trod them in the pits quickly, putting a contest forward.
93	But when they had washed them and cleaned off all the dirt,
94	they spread them in a row along the shore of the sea, where most of all
95	the sea washed the pebbles up onto the dry land.
96	They bathed and rubbed themselves richly with oil
97	and then took their meal beside the banks of the river,
98	and waited for the clothes to dry in the ray of the sun.
99	But when the serving-women and she herself had had their fill of food,
100	they played at ball, throwing their head-veils off.
101	And white-armed Nausicaa led them in the song-and-dance.
102	As Artemis the arrow-pourer goes down the mountains,
103	either down very tall Taygetus or Erymanthus,
104	taking her pleasure in boars and swift deer,
105	and with her the nymphs, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus,
106	who range the fields, play, and Leto is glad in her heart:
107	she holds her head and her brow above them all,
108	and she is easily known, and they are all beautiful —
109	so she stood out among the serving-women, the unbroken girl.
110	But when she was about to go back home again,
111	having yoked the mules and folded the beautiful clothes,
112	then the goddess thought of something else, owl-eyed Athena:
113	that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl,
114	who would lead him to the city of the Phaeacian men.
115	Then the princess threw the ball at a serving-woman.
116	She missed the serving-woman, and threw it into a deep eddy.
117	They cried out loud. And godlike Odysseus woke,
118	and sitting up he turned it over in his mind and in his heart:
> Odysseus
119	Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time?
120	Are they violent and wild and not just,
121	or friendly to strangers, and is their mind god-fearing?
122	How a female cry of girls has come around me —
123	of nymphs, who hold the steep peaks of the mountains
124	and the springs of rivers and the grassy water-meadows.
125	Or am I somewhere near people who have speech?
126	But come, I myself will try, and I will see.
> (narrator)
127	So speaking, godlike Odysseus came out from under the bushes,
128	and from the dense wood he broke a branch with his thick hand,
129	a branch of leaves, so he might cover a man's genitals about his skin.
130	He went to go like a mountain-bred lion trusting in his force,
131	who goes rained on and blown on, and in him the eyes
132	are burning. He goes among cattle or among sheep
133	or after wild deer. And his belly drives him
134	to try the flocks and even to go into the close-built fold.
135	So Odysseus was about to go among the girls of the beautiful hair,
136	naked as he was. For need had come on him.
137	He appeared terrible to them, ruined by the brine,
138	and they shrank in fear, one one way and one another, along the jutting beaches.
139	Only the daughter of Alcinous stayed. For in her Athena
140	had put courage in her mind and taken the fear from her limbs.
141	She stood facing him, holding her ground. And Odysseus pondered
142	whether he should clasp the fair-faced girl's knees and beg,
143	or standing off as he was, with gentle words
144	beg her, if she would show the city and give him clothes.
145	As he thought it out, it seemed more profitable to him
146	to beg with gentle words, standing off,
147	for fear the girl would be angry in her heart if he took her knees.
148	At once he spoke a word both gentle and profitable.
> Odysseus
149	I grasp your knees, queen — are you some god, or are you mortal?
150	If you are one of the gods who hold the wide sky,
151	I liken you most nearly to Artemis, daughter of great Zeus,
152	in look and in size and in build.
153	But if you are one of the mortals who live on the earth,
154	three times blessed are your father and honored mother,
155	three times blessed your brothers. Surely their heart
156	is always warmed with gladness because of you,
157	when they look at such a shoot going into the dance.
158	And that man in turn is blessed in his heart beyond all others,
159	who loads you with bride-gifts and leads you home.
160	For I have never yet seen with my eyes such a mortal,
161	neither man nor woman. Awe holds me as I look at you.
162	At Delos once I saw such a thing, beside the altar of Apollo,
163	the young shoot of a palm coming up.
164	For I went there too, and a great host followed me,
165	on that road which was going to be many bad griefs for me.
166	Just so, seeing that too, I was stunned in my heart
167	a long time, since never yet had such a shaft come up from the earth,
168	as I marvel at you, lady, and I am stunned, and I am terribly afraid
169	to touch your knees. A hard grief has come on me.
170	Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I escaped the wine-faced sea.
171	All that time the wave and the rushing storms kept carrying me
172	from the island of Ogygia. Now a god has thrown me down here,
173	so that I may suffer some evil in this place too. For I do not think
174	it will stop, but the gods will still finish many things before that.
175	But have pity, queen. After suffering many evils I have come
176	to you first, and I do not know any other
177	of the people who hold this city and land.
178	Show me the town, and give me a rag to throw around me,
179	if you had any wrapping of cloths at all when you came here.
180	And may the gods give you as much as you desire in your mind,
181	a husband and a house, and may they grant like-mindedness,
182	a good one. For nothing is stronger and better than this,
183	than when two, like-minded in their thoughts, keep a house,
184	a man and a woman. Many pains for those who wish them ill,
185	and joys for those who wish them well, and they themselves hear it most of all.
> (narrator)
186	Then white-armed Nausicaa spoke in answer to him:
> Nausicaa
187	Stranger, since you do not seem like a bad man or a mindless one —
188	Zeus himself, the Olympian, allots prosperity to people,
189	to the good and to the bad, to each as he wishes.
190	And he has given you this, I suppose, and you must endure it all the same.
191	Now, since you have come to our city and our land,
192	you will not go short of clothing, nor of anything else
193	that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has met us.
194	I will show you the town, and I will tell you the name of the people.
195	The Phaeacians hold this city and land,
196	and I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous,
197	and on him the force and the strength of the Phaeacians depend.
> (narrator)
198	So she spoke, and she ordered the serving-women of the beautiful hair:
> Nausicaa
199	Stand, serving-women. Where are you running, now that you have seen a man?
200	You do not think he is some man of those who wish us ill?
201	There is no such man, no living mortal, nor will there be,
202	who would come to the land of the Phaeacian men
203	bringing war. For they are very dear to the immortals.
204	We live far off in the much-washed sea,
205	at the edge, and no other mortal mixes with us.
206	But this is some wretched man who has come here wandering,
207	and now we must care for him. For from Zeus are all
208	strangers and beggars, and a gift is small and welcome.
209	But give the stranger food and drink, serving-women,
210	and wash him in the river, where there is shelter from the wind.
> (narrator)
211	So she spoke, and they stood and called to one another,
212	and they sat Odysseus down in the shelter, as
213	Nausicaa, daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, had ordered.
214	Beside him they set a cloak and a tunic as clothes,
215	and they gave him liquid oil in a golden flask,
216	and they told him to wash in the streams of the river.
217	Then godlike Odysseus spoke among the serving-women:
> Odysseus
218	Serving-women, stand off that far, so that I myself
219	may wash the brine from my shoulders, and anoint myself
220	with oil. For a long time now the oil has been off my skin.
221	But I will not wash in front of you. For I feel shame
222	to be naked, coming among girls of the beautiful hair.
> (narrator)
223	So he spoke, and they went off apart, and they told the girl.
224	Then godlike Odysseus washed his skin in the river,
225	the brine that covered his back and his broad shoulders,
226	and from his head he scrubbed the scurf of the barren sea.
227	But when he had washed everything and rubbed himself richly,
228	he put on the clothes the unbroken girl had given him.
229	Athena, born of Zeus, made him
230	taller to look at and thicker, and from his head
231	she sent down curling hair, like the hyacinth flower.
232	As when a man pours gold around silver,
233	a knowing man, whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught
234	every kind of craft, and he finishes graceful work,
235	so she poured grace down over his head and his shoulders.
236	He went and sat down apart on the shore of the sea,
237	shining with beauty and graces. And the girl gazed at him.
238	Then she spoke among the serving-women of the beautiful hair:
> Nausicaa
239	Hear me, white-armed serving-women, so I may say something.
240	It is not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus
241	that this man mixes with the godlike Phaeacians.
242	Before this he seemed to me to be unseemly,
243	but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky.
244	If only such a one might be called my husband,
245	living here, and it might please him to remain right here.
246	But give the stranger food and drink, serving-women.
> (narrator)
247	So she spoke, and they heard her well and obeyed,
248	and they set food and drink beside Odysseus.
249	Then he drank and ate, much-enduring godlike Odysseus,
250	eagerly. For a long time he had been without a taste of food.
251	But white-armed Nausicaa thought of something else.
252	Folding the clothes she laid them on the beautiful wagon,
253	and she yoked the strong-hoofed mules, and she herself stepped up,
254	and she urged Odysseus, and spoke a word and called him by name:
> Nausicaa
255	Rise now, stranger, to go to the city, so I may send you
256	to the house of my war-minded father, where I say
257	you will come to know of all the Phaeacians, as many as are the best.
258	But do just this way — and you do not seem to me to be mindless.
259	While we go through the fields and the works of men,
260	for that time with the serving-women, behind the mules and the wagon,
261	go quickly. And I will lead the way.
262	But when we set foot on the city, around which a wall
263	high stands, and a beautiful harbor is on either side of the city,
264	and the entrance is narrow, and the ships curved at both ends
265	are drawn up along the road. For there is a slip for each and all.
266	And there is their gathering-place around the beautiful precinct of Poseidon,
267	fitted with dragged stones set down in the earth.
268	There they tend the gear of the black ships,
269	cables and sails, and they sharpen the oars.
270	For the Phaeacians do not care for the bow or the quiver,
271	but masts and oars of ships and well-balanced ships,
272	in which they take their delight and cross the gray sea.
273	I avoid their unkind talk, lest someone afterward
274	find fault. They are very overproud throughout the land.
275	And some worse man, meeting us, might say this:
> (a worse man)
276	Who is this who follows Nausicaa, handsome and large,
277	this stranger? Where did she find him? He will be her husband.
278	Some man driven off course, I suppose, she brought from his ship,
279	of men from far away, since there are none near here.
280	Or some much-prayed-for god came to her when she prayed,
281	coming down from the sky, and he will have her all her days.
282	Better if she herself, going about, found a husband
283	from elsewhere. For she dishonors these here throughout the land,
284	the Phaeacians, who court her, many and good men.
> Nausicaa
285	So they will say, and these things would be a reproach to me.
286	And I too feel righteous anger at another woman, whoever would do such things,
287	who against the will of her dear ones, her father and mother still living,
288	mixes with men, before the open marriage has come.
289	Stranger, you quickly understand my word, so that as quickly as possible
290	you may get escort and homecoming from my father.
291	You will find a shining grove of Athena near the road,
292	of poplars. A spring flows in it, and around it a meadow.
293	There is my father's precinct and a flourishing orchard,
294	as far from the city as a shout carries when a man has called out.
295	Sitting there, wait a time, until we
296	go to the town and reach my father's house.
297	But when you guess we have reached the house,
298	then go into the city of the Phaeacians and ask
299	for the house of my father, great-hearted Alcinous.
300	It is easily known, and even a child could lead you
301	a child-minded one. For the houses of the Phaeacians are not made like it,
302	such is the house of Alcinous
303	the hero. But when the house and courtyard cover you,
304	go very quickly through the hall, until you reach
305	my mother. She sits at the hearth in the gleam of the fire,
306	turning the sea-purple distaff-wool, a wonder to look at,
307	leaning against a pillar. And serving-women sit behind her.
308	And there my father's throne is set against hers,
309	and on it he sits drinking wine, like an immortal.
310	Passing him by, around our mother's knees your hands
311	cast, so you may see the day of coming home
312	rejoicing, quickly, even if you are from very far away.
313	If she thinks kindly of you in her heart,
314	then there is hope for you to see your friends and to reach
315	your well-built house and your fatherland.
> (narrator)
316	So speaking she whipped the mules with the shining whip,
317	and they quickly left the streams of the river.
318	They trotted well, and they pranced well with their feet,
319	and she drove very carefully, so that the ones on foot could follow along,
320	the serving-women and Odysseus, and she laid the whip on with judgment.
321	The sun set, and they reached the famous grove,
322	the holy one of Athena, where godlike Odysseus sat down.
323	At once then he prayed to the daughter of great Zeus:
> Odysseus
324	Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Unwearied one.
325	Now at last hear me, since before this you never heard me
326	when I was being smashed, when the famous earth-shaker was smashing me.
327	Grant that I come among the Phaeacians as one loved and pitied.
> (narrator)
328	So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him.
329	But she did not yet appear facing him. For she felt shame
330	before her father's brother. And he was furiously angry
331	at godlike Odysseus, before he reached his own land.
