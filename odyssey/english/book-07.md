# The Odyssey: Book VII

> (narrator)
1	So he prayed there, much-enduring godlike Odysseus,
2	and the force of the two mules carried the girl toward the city.
3	And when she reached her father's far-famed house,
4	she stopped in the forecourt, and her brothers around her
5	stood, like the immortals, and they from under the wagon
6	loosed the mules and carried the clothes inside.
7	She herself went into her own chamber; and a fire was kindled for her
8	by an old woman of Apeira, the chamber-attendant Eurymedusa,
9	whom once from Apeira the ships curved at both ends had brought;
10	and they chose her as a prize of honor for Alcinous, because over all
11	the Phaeacians he was lord, and the people listened to him as to a god;
12	she had nursed white-armed Nausicaa in the halls.
13	She kindled the fire for her and set the supper in order inside.
14	And then Odysseus rose to go toward the city; and around him Athena
15	poured a thick mist, thinking kindly of Odysseus,
16	so that no one of the great-hearted Phaeacians, meeting him,
17	would taunt him with words and ask who he was.
18	But when he was about to enter the lovely city,
19	then the goddess met him, owl-eyed Athena,
20	likened to a young girl, a maiden, holding a water-jar.
21	She stood in front of him, and godlike Odysseus asked her:
> Odysseus
22	Child, would you not lead me to the house of a man,
23	Alcinous, who is lord among these people here?
24	For I come here as a stranger, much-suffering,
25	from far away, from a distant land; therefore I know no one
26	of the people who hold this city and land.
> (narrator)
27	Then the goddess spoke to him, owl-eyed Athena:
> Athena
28	Then I will, stranger-father, the house you bid me,
29	show you, since it lives near my own blameless father's.
30	But go just so, in silence, and I will lead the way,
31	and do not look at any of the people, nor question them.
32	For these people do not much endure strangers,
33	nor do they welcome and befriend whoever comes from elsewhere.
34	They, trusting in their swift ships, the fast ones,
35	cross the great gulf, since the earth-shaker gave it to them;
36	their ships are fast as a wing or a thought.
> (narrator)
37	So speaking, Pallas Athena led the way
38	quickly; and he then walked in the footsteps of the god.
39	And the Phaeacians famed for ships did not notice him
40	going through the city among them; for Athena
41	did not allow it, the fair-haired one, a dread goddess, who poured over him a mist
42	marvelous, thinking kindly in her heart.
43	And Odysseus wondered at the harbors and the well-balanced ships
44	and at the gathering-places of the heroes themselves and the long walls
45	high, fitted with palisades, a wonder to look at.
46	But when they reached the king's far-famed house,
47	then the goddess began the talk among them, owl-eyed Athena:
> Athena
48	This, stranger-father, is the house you bid me
49	point out; and you will find Zeus-nourished kings
50	feasting at a feast; but you go inside, and in your heart do not
51	be afraid; for a bold man is better in all
52	deeds, even if he should come from somewhere else.
53	The mistress you will come upon first in the halls;
54	Arete is the name given her, and from parents
55	the same ones who bore king Alcinous.
56	Nausithous first the earth-shaker Poseidon
57	begot, and Periboea did, finest of women in form,
58	youngest daughter of great-hearted Eurymedon,
59	who once was king over the overbearing Giants.
60	But he destroyed his people of reckless transgression, and he himself was destroyed;
61	and Poseidon lay with her and begot a child,
62	great-hearted Nausithous, who was lord among the Phaeacians;
63	and Nausithous begot Rhexenor and Alcinous.
64	The one, still without a male heir, silver-bowed Apollo struck
65	as a bridegroom in the hall, leaving only one child,
66	Arete; and Alcinous made her his wife,
67	and he honors her as no other woman on earth is honored,
68	as many women as now keep house under their husbands.
69	So she is honored beyond others in the heart, and she is honored,
70	both from her dear children and from Alcinous himself
71	and from the people, who looking on her as on a god
72	greet her with words whenever she walks through the city.
73	For she herself is not at all lacking in a good mind;
74	and for those she thinks well of she even dissolves quarrels among men.
75	If she should think kindly of you in her heart,
76	then there is hope for you to see your friends and to reach
77	your high-roofed house and your own fatherland.
> (narrator)
78	So speaking, owl-eyed Athena went away
79	over the barren sea, and she left lovely Scheria,
80	and she came to Marathon and wide-wayed Athens,
81	and she entered the close-built house of Erechtheus. But Odysseus
82	went toward the famous house of Alcinous; and much in his heart
83	he turned over as he stood there, before he reached the bronze threshold.
84	For as the brightness of the sun it was, or of the moon,
85	through the high-roofed house of great-hearted Alcinous.
86	For bronze walls had been driven this way and that,
87	from the threshold to the inner room, and around them a cornice of dark-blue enamel;
88	and golden doors shut in the close-built house;
89	and silver doorposts stood on the bronze threshold,
90	and a silver lintel on it, and a golden door-ring.
91	And golden and silver dogs were on either side,
92	whom Hephaestus had made with knowing mind
93	to guard the house of great-hearted Alcinous,
94	immortal they were, and ageless all their days.
95	And thrones were set against the wall this way and that,
96	from the threshold straight through to the inner room, and on them robes
97	fine, well-spun, had been thrown, the work of women.
98	There the leaders of the Phaeacians sat
99	drinking and eating; for they had it in unfailing supply.
100	And golden young men on well-built pedestals
101	stood holding blazing torches in their hands,
102	lighting the nights through the house for the feasters.
103	And fifty serving-women he had through the house:
104	some grind on the millstones the apple-colored grain,
105	and others weave at the looms and twist the spindles
106	sitting, like the leaves of a tall poplar;
107	and from the close-woven linens liquid oil drips off.
108	As much as the Phaeacians are skilled beyond all men
109	at driving a swift ship on the sea, so the women
110	are skilled at the loom; for Athena gave them beyond others
111	to know beautiful work, and good minds.
112	Outside the courtyard a great orchard near the doors
113	of four measures; and a fence has been driven around it on both sides.
114	There tall trees have grown, flourishing,
115	pears and pomegranates and apple trees of shining fruit
116	and sweet fig trees and olives flourishing.
117	Of these the fruit never perishes or fails
118	in winter or in summer, year-round; but always
119	the West Wind blowing makes some grow and ripens others.
120	Pear upon pear grows old, and apple upon apple,
121	and grape upon grape, and fig upon fig.
122	And there a fruit-heavy vineyard is rooted for him,
123	of which one part, a drying-ground in a level place,
124	is dried by the sun, and they are gathering others,
125	and they are treading still others; and in front are the unripe grapes
126	shedding their blossom, and others are turning dark.
127	And there ordered garden-beds beside the last row of trees
128	of all kinds have grown, gleaming year-round;
129	and in it two springs: one through the whole garden
130	is scattered, and the other from the other side under the courtyard threshold sends
131	toward the high house, from which the townspeople drew water.
132	Such then in Alcinous's house were the shining gifts of the gods.
133	There standing, much-enduring godlike Odysseus gazed.
134	But when he had gazed at everything in his own heart,
135	quickly he stepped over the threshold into the house.
136	And he found the leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians
137	pouring libations from their cups to the sharp-sighted slayer of Argus,
138	to whom they poured last, whenever they thought of bed.
139	But he went through the house, much-enduring godlike Odysseus,
140	holding the thick mist that Athena had poured around him,
141	until he reached Arete and king Alcinous.
142	Then Odysseus threw his hands around Arete's knees,
143	and then the god-spoken mist poured back from him.
144	And they grew silent, seeing a man through the house;
145	they wondered as they looked. And Odysseus made his prayer:
> Odysseus
146	Arete, daughter of godlike Rhexenor,
147	I come to your husband and to your knees, having suffered many things,
148	and to these feasters; may the gods give them blessings
149	to live, and may each man hand on to his children
150	the possessions in his halls and the honor the people gave him;
151	but for me, urge on an escort, so I may reach my fatherland
152	sooner, since now for a long time I suffer pains far from my friends.
> (narrator)
153	So speaking he sat down at the hearth in the ashes
154	beside the fire; and they all grew still in silence.
155	Late then the old hero Echeneus spoke among them,
156	who was older than the men of the Phaeacians
157	and excelled in words, knowing many old things;
158	he spoke in the assembly with good intent for them, and said:
> Echeneus
159	Alcinous, this is not finer for you, nor is it fitting,
160	that a stranger sit on the ground at the hearth in the ashes,
161	and these men hold back, waiting for your word.
162	But come now, seat the stranger on a silver-studded throne,
163	raising him up, and you order the heralds
164	to mix wine on top, so that also to Zeus who delights in thunder
165	we may pour, who goes with suppliants worthy of regard;
166	and let the housekeeper give the stranger supper from what is inside.
> (narrator)
167	But when the holy strength of Alcinous heard this,
168	taking by the hand Odysseus the war-minded, of changeful counsel,
169	he raised him from the hearth and seated him on a shining throne,
170	raising up his son, man-loving Laodamas,
171	who sat near him, and he loved him most of all.
172	And a serving-woman brought washing-water and poured it from a pitcher
173	beautiful, golden, over a silver basin,
174	to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside him.
175	And the respected housekeeper set bread beside him, bringing it,
176	putting many dishes on it, giving freely of what was there.
177	Then he drank and ate, much-enduring godlike Odysseus.
178	And then the strength of Alcinous addressed the herald:
> Alcinous
179	Pontonous, mix a bowl and serve the wine
180	to all through the hall, so that also to Zeus who delights in thunder
181	we may pour, who goes with suppliants worthy of regard.
> (narrator)
182	So he spoke, and Pontonous mixed the honey-hearted wine,
183	and he served it to all, beginning the rite in the cups.
184	But when they had poured and drunk as much as the heart wanted,
185	then Alcinous spoke in the assembly among them and said:
> Alcinous
186	Hear me, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians,
187	so I may say what the heart in my chest bids me.
188	Now, having feasted, go lie down, going home;
189	and at dawn, calling more of the old men
190	we will host the stranger in the halls and to the gods
191	we will perform beautiful sacred rites, and then also of the escort
192	we will take thought, so that the stranger without labor and without grief
193	under our escort may reach his own fatherland
194	rejoicing, quickly, even if it is very far away,
195	and so that he may not suffer any evil or pain on the way,
196	at least until he sets foot on his own land; and there then
197	he will suffer whatever fate and the heavy Spinners
198	spun with their thread for him at his birth, when his mother bore him.
199	But if some one of the immortals has come down from the sky,
200	then this is something else the gods are contriving around us.
201	For always before this the gods appear in clear sight
202	to us, whenever we perform far-famed hecatombs,
203	and they feast beside us, sitting in the same place we do.
204	And if some traveler going alone even meets them,
205	they do not hide at all, since we are near them,
206	as the Cyclopes are, and the wild tribes of the Giants.
> (narrator)
207	Then Odysseus of many designs spoke in answer to him:
> Odysseus
208	Alcinous, let something else be a care in your mind; for I
209	do not resemble the immortals who hold the wide sky,
210	not in body or in growth, but like mortal men.
211	Whomever you know who most of all bear up under misery
212	among people, with those I would match myself in pains.
213	And I could still tell even more evils,
214	all that I have suffered by the will of the gods.
215	But let me eat my supper, though I am in grief;
216	for there is nothing more doglike than the hateful belly,
217	which bids a man remember it by compulsion
218	even when he is worn down and has grief in his mind,
219	as I too have grief in my mind, but it always
220	bids me eat and drink, and from all things
221	it makes me forget how much I have suffered, and it orders me to be filled.
222	But you, be ready at the appearing of dawn,
223	so that you may set me, the wretched one, on my own fatherland
224	even after I have suffered many things; and once I have seen
225	my possessions and my serving-people and my high-roofed great house, let life leave me.
> (narrator)
226	So he spoke, and they all approved and urged
227	that the stranger be sent on, since he had spoken in due measure.
228	But when they had poured and drunk as much as the heart wanted,
229	they went to lie down, each to his own house,
230	but he was left behind in the hall, godlike Odysseus,
231	and beside him Arete and godlike Alcinous
232	sat; and serving-women cleared away the gear of the feast.
233	Then white-armed Arete began the talk among them;
234	for she knew the cloak and the tunic, seeing the clothes
235	the beautiful ones, which she herself had made with her serving-women;
236	and speaking she addressed him with winged words:
> Arete
237	Stranger, this I myself will ask you first:
238	Who are you, and from where among men? Who gave you these clothes?
239	Did you not say you came here wandering over the sea?
> (narrator)
240	Then Odysseus of many designs spoke in answer to her:
> Odysseus
241	It is a hard thing, queen, to tell out all the way
242	my griefs, since the gods of the sky have given me many;
243	but this I will tell you, what you ask and inquire of me.
244	There is a certain island, Ogygia, that lies far off in the salt water;
245	there the daughter of Atlas, wily Calypso,
246	lives, the fair-haired one, a dread goddess; and no one with her
247	mixes, neither of the gods nor of mortal people.
248	But me, the wretched one, a divinity brought to her hearth
249	alone, after my swift ship with a bright thunderbolt
250	Zeus, striking it, smashed in the middle of the wine-faced sea.
251	There all the others perished, my good companions,
252	but I, taking in my arms the keel of the ship curved at both ends,
253	was carried nine days; and on the tenth, in the black night,
254	the gods brought me near the island Ogygia, where Calypso
255	lives, the fair-haired one, a dread goddess, who taking me
256	loved me kindly and fed me and said
257	she would make me immortal and ageless all my days;
258	but she never persuaded the heart in my chest.
259	There I stayed seven years without moving, and the clothes always
260	I kept wetting with tears, the immortal ones Calypso gave me;
261	but when the eighth year came rolling on for me,
262	then she bid me go, urging me on,
263	under a message from Zeus, or even her own mind turned.
264	And she sent me on a raft of many bindings, and she gave me much,
265	bread and sweet wine, and she put immortal clothes on me,
266	and she sent forth a wind, harmless and warm.
267	Seventeen days I sailed, crossing the sea,
268	and on the eighteenth the shadowy mountains appeared
269	of your land, and my own heart was glad
270	in my ill fate; for I was still going to keep company with misery
271	much of it, which the earth-shaker Poseidon raised against me,
272	who, setting the winds on me, bound my way,
273	and he stirred the sea past telling, and no wave
274	would he let me be carried on, on the raft, groaning thickly.
275	Then a storm-wind scattered it; but I
276	swimming cut through this gulf, until to your land
277	the wind and the water, carrying me, brought me near.
278	There the wave would have forced me, as I came out, onto the dry land,
279	hurling me against the great rocks and a joyless place;
280	but drawing back I swam again, until I came
281	to a river, where the place seemed to me the best,
282	smooth of rocks, and there was shelter from the wind.
283	And I fell out rallying my spirit, and immortal night
284	came on. And I, away from the Zeus-fallen river,
285	stepping out, slept in the bushes, and leaves around me
286	I scooped; and a god poured sleep down over me, boundless.
287	There in the leaves, my own heart heavy,
288	I slept all night and through the dawn and the middle of the day.
289	The sun was declining, and sweet sleep let me go.
290	And I noticed your daughter's serving-women on the shore
291	playing, and she herself was among them, likened to the goddesses;
292	I came as a suppliant to her; and she did not miss a good thought,
293	as you would not hope a younger person meeting you
294	would do; for younger people are always thoughtless.
295	She gave me bread enough and sparkling wine
296	and washed me in the river and gave me these clothes.
297	These things, though I am in pain, I have told you as the truth.
> (narrator)
298	Then Alcinous answered him and spoke:
> Alcinous
299	Stranger, this at least my child did not think of in the right way,
300	that she did not bring you at all with her serving-women
301	to our house, and you were the first to come as a suppliant to her.
> (narrator)
302	Then Odysseus of many designs spoke in answer to him:
> Odysseus
303	Hero, do not for this quarrel with your blameless girl;
304	for she did bid me follow with the serving-women,
305	but I was not willing, afraid and also ashamed,
306	lest somehow your heart too might grow angry at the sight;
307	for we are jealous tribes of people on the earth.
> (narrator)
308	Then Alcinous answered him and spoke:
> Alcinous
309	Stranger, the dear heart in my chest is not such
310	as to grow angry for nothing; due measure is better in all things.
311	If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
312	being such as you are, and thinking the things I myself think,
313	you might have my daughter and be called my son-in-law
314	staying right here; and I would give you a house and possessions,
315	if you should stay willing; but against your will no one will hold you
316	of the Phaeacians; may that not be dear to father Zeus.
317	And the escort I mark out to this, so you may know it well:
318	to tomorrow; then you, overcome by sleep,
319	will lie down, and they will drive the calm sea, until you reach
320	your fatherland and house, and if somewhere it is dear to you,
321	even if it is very much farther than Euboea,
322	which they say is farthest away, those who saw it
323	of our people, when they took fair-haired Rhadamanthys
324	to visit Tityus, the son of Earth.
325	And they went there and without labor they finished it
326	on that same day and accomplished the way home again.
327	And you yourself will know in your mind how far the best
328	my ships are, and my young men at tossing the sea with the oar-blade.
> (narrator)
329	So he spoke, and much-enduring godlike Odysseus was glad,
330	and praying he spoke, and he uttered a word and called him by name:
> Odysseus
331	Father Zeus, if only he would bring to completion all that he said,
332	Alcinous; for him then over the grain-giving earth
333	there would be unquenchable glory, and I would reach my fatherland.
> (narrator)
334	So they spoke such things to one another;
335	and white-armed Arete called to the serving-women
336	to set a bed under the portico and to throw on beautiful blankets
337	purple ones, and to spread rugs on top
338	and to put in woolly cloaks to wear over it.
339	They went from the hall holding a torch in their hands;
340	and when they had spread the close-built bed, bustling,
341	they urged Odysseus, standing beside him with words:
> serving-women
342	Rise and go lie down, stranger; the bed has been made for you.
> (narrator)
343	So they spoke, and it seemed welcome to him to go to sleep.
344	So he slept there, much-enduring godlike Odysseus,
345	in a corded bed under the loud-echoing portico;
346	and Alcinous lay down in the inner part of the high house,
347	and beside him the lady his wife made ready the bed and the bedding.
