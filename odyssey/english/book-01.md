# The Odyssey: Book I

> (narrator)
1	Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turnings, who was driven
2	far off course after he sacked the holy city of Troy.
3	He saw the towns of many people and came to know their mind,
4	and on the sea he suffered many pains, in his own heart,
5	trying to win his life and his companions' way home.
6	Even so he did not save his companions, much as he wanted to.
7	They died of their own reckless doing —
8	fools, who ate the cattle of Hyperion the Sun.
9	And the Sun took from them their day of coming home.
10	Of these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, start from some point and tell us too.
11	Then all the others, as many as had escaped sheer destruction,
12	were at home, having fled both the war and the sea.
13	But him alone, longing for his homecoming and his wife,
14	the lady nymph Calypso, shining among goddesses, held back
15	in her hollow caves, longing for him to be her husband.
16	But when the year came, as the years circled round,
17	the one in which the gods had spun it for him to go homeward
18	to Ithaca — not even there was he free of trials
19	even among his own dear ones. And all the gods pitied him
20	except Poseidon. He raged without pause
21	at godlike Odysseus, before he reached his own land.
22	But he had gone among the Ethiopians who live far off —
23	Ethiopians split in two, the farthest of men,
24	some at Hyperion's setting, some at his rising —
25	to meet a hecatomb of bulls and rams.
26	There he sat taking pleasure in the feast. And the others
27	were all together in the halls of Olympian Zeus.
28	And among them the father of men and of gods began the talk.
29	For he remembered in his heart blameless Aegisthus,
30	whom far-famed Orestes, Agamemnon's son, had killed.
31	Remembering him, he spoke these words among the deathless ones:
> Zeus
32	Look now, how mortals blame the gods.
33	They say evils come from us, but they themselves
34	by their own reckless doing have pains beyond what is portioned,
35	as now Aegisthus, beyond what was portioned, married the wedded wife
36	of Atreus's son, and killed him when he came home,
37	knowing sheer destruction, since we told him beforehand,
38	sending Hermes, the sharp-eyed slayer of Argus,
39	not to kill the man himself and not to court his wife.
40	For from Orestes there will be payment for Atreus's son,
41	whenever he comes of age and longs for his own land.
42	So Hermes spoke, but he did not persuade Aegisthus's mind,
43	though he meant what was good. And now he has paid for it all together.
> (narrator)
44	Then the goddess, owl-eyed Athena, answered him:
> Athena
45	Father of ours, son of Cronus, highest of rulers,
46	that man at least lies in a doom that fits him.
47	So may anyone else perish who does such things.
48	But my heart is torn for Odysseus of the wise mind,
49	the ill-fated one, who far from his friends suffers pains so long
50	on an island ringed by sea, where the navel of the sea is.
51	The island is wooded, and a goddess lives in a house there,
52	daughter of Atlas of the ruinous mind, who knows
53	the depths of the whole sea, and himself holds the tall pillars
54	that keep earth and sky apart.
55	His daughter holds back that wretched man as he weeps,
56	and always with soft and wheedling words
57	she charms him, so he will forget Ithaca. But Odysseus,
58	longing even to see the smoke leaping up
59	from his own land, wants to die. And even your dear heart
60	is not turned, Olympian. Did not Odysseus
61	win favor beside the Argives' ships, making offerings
62	in wide Troy? Why then are you so angry at him, Zeus?
> (narrator)
63	Then cloud-gatherer Zeus spoke in answer to her:
> Zeus
64	My child, what kind of word has escaped the barrier of your teeth?
65	How then could I forget godlike Odysseus,
66	who is beyond mortals in mind, and beyond others has given offerings
67	to the deathless gods who hold the wide sky?
68	But earth-holder Poseidon is always unrelentingly
69	angry over the Cyclops, whose eye he blinded —
70	godlike Polyphemus, whose strength is greatest
71	among all the Cyclopes. The nymph Thoosa bore him,
72	daughter of Phorcys, lord of the unharvested sea,
73	when she lay with Poseidon in the hollow caves.
74	From that time earth-shaker Poseidon
75	does not kill Odysseus at all, but drives him off course from his fatherland.
76	But come, let all of us here consider carefully
77	his homecoming, how he may come. And Poseidon will let go
78	his anger. For he will not be able, against all
79	the deathless ones, to keep up the quarrel alone, against the gods' will.
> (narrator)
80	Then the goddess, owl-eyed Athena, answered him:
> Athena
81	Father of ours, son of Cronus, highest of rulers,
82	if this is now dear to the blessed gods,
83	that Odysseus of many thoughts come home to his own house,
84	then let us send Hermes the guide, slayer of Argus,
85	to the island Ogygia, so that as quickly as possible
86	he may tell the fair-haired nymph the will that does not miss:
87	the homecoming of Odysseus of the enduring heart, that he may go.
88	And I myself will go to Ithaca, so that I may urge his son
89	on more, and put force in his chest,
90	to call the long-haired Achaeans to assembly
91	and speak out to all the suitors, who always
92	slaughter his huddled sheep and his shambling curved-horn cattle.
93	And I will send him to Sparta and to sandy Pylos
94	to ask after the homecoming of his dear father, if he hears of it somewhere,
95	and so that he may have noble glory among men.
> (narrator)
96	So she spoke, and under her feet she bound the fine sandals,
97	deathless, golden, that carried her both over the wet sea
98	and over the endless earth with the breaths of the wind.
99	And she took her strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze,
100	heavy, huge, solid, with which she beats down the ranks of men
101	who are heroes, those at whom the daughter of a mighty father is angry.
102	She went darting down from the peaks of Olympus,
103	and she stood in the district of Ithaca at the gates of Odysseus's house,
104	on the courtyard threshold. In her palm she held a bronze spear,
105	likened to a stranger, Mentes, leader of the Taphians.
106	She found the proud suitors. They then
107	were taking pleasure in their hearts at draughts in front of the doors,
108	sitting on the hides of cattle they themselves had killed.
109	Heralds and busy servants were with them —
110	some were mixing wine and water in bowls,
111	and others were wiping the tables with porous sponges
112	and setting them out, and they were carving up many meats.
113	Godlike Telemachus saw her far the first,
114	for he was sitting among the suitors, his own dear heart grieving,
115	seeing his good father in his mind, if from somewhere he should come
116	and make a scattering of the suitors through the house,
117	and he himself have honor and be lord in his own halls.
118	Thinking these things as he sat among the suitors, he saw Athena.
119	He went straight to the gateway, and righteous anger took him in his heart
120	that a stranger should stand so long at the doors. Standing near
121	he took her right hand and received the bronze spear,
122	and speaking he addressed her with winged words:
> Telemachus
123	Welcome, stranger. You will be treated as a friend among us. And then,
124	when you have tasted dinner, you will tell what you need.
> (narrator)
125	So he spoke and led the way, and she followed, Pallas Athena.
126	And when they were inside the high-roofed house,
127	he carried the spear and set it against a tall pillar
128	inside the well-smoothed spear-stand, where many other
129	spears of Odysseus of the enduring heart stood.
130	And he led her herself to a chair and seated her, spreading a linen cloth under her,
131	fine, richly worked. And a footstool was under her feet.
132	Beside her he himself set a painted chair, apart from the others,
133	the suitors, so that the stranger, distressed by the din,
134	would not be put off his dinner, coming among overbearing men,
135	and so that he might ask him about his father who was away.
136	A serving-woman brought water for the hands and poured it from a pitcher,
137	a fine gold one, over a silver basin,
138	to wash. And she drew up a polished table beside them.
139	The respected housekeeper brought and set out bread,
140	putting many dishes on it, giving freely of what was there.
141	A carver lifted and set beside them platters of meats
142	of all kinds, and set golden cups beside them.
143	A herald went back and forth pouring wine for them.
144	The proud suitors came in. They then
145	sat down in order on chairs and thrones,
146	and the heralds poured water over their hands,
147	and serving-women heaped bread beside them in baskets,
148	and young men crowned the mixing-bowls with drink.
149	They reached out their hands to the food lying ready.
150	But when the suitors had put from them the desire of drink and food,
151	other things were a care in their minds —
152	song and dance. For these are the adornments of a feast.
153	A herald put a very beautiful lyre in the hands
154	of Phemius, who sang among the suitors under compulsion.
155	He then struck up on the lyre, beginning a fine song.
156	But Telemachus spoke to owl-eyed Athena,
157	holding his head close, so the others would not hear:
> Telemachus
158	Dear stranger, will you be angry at me for what I say?
159	These things are a care to them, the lyre and the song,
160	easily, since they eat another man's livelihood without payment —
161	a man's whose white bones, I suppose, are rotting in the rain
162	lying on the mainland, or the wave rolls them in the salt water.
163	If they saw that man come home to Ithaca,
164	all of them would pray to be lighter on their feet
165	rather than richer in gold and clothing.
166	But now he has died in this way, an evil fate, and there is no
167	comfort for us, even if some one of the people on earth
168	says he will come. His day of coming home is gone.
169	But come, tell me this and recount it truly:
170	Who are you among men, and from where? Where is your city and your parents?
171	On what kind of ship did you come? How did the sailors
172	bring you to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be?
173	For I do not think you reached here on foot.
174	And tell me this truly, so I may know well,
175	whether you are a new visitor or also a guest-friend
176	of my father's, since many men used to come to our house
177	as others, since that man too went about among people.
> (narrator)
178	Then the goddess, owl-eyed Athena, spoke to him in turn:
> Athena
179	Then I will tell you this very truly.
180	I claim to be Mentes, son of Anchialus of the wise mind,
181	and I am lord over the oar-loving Taphians.
182	And now I have come here with a ship and companions
183	sailing the wine-faced sea to people of another speech,
184	to Temese after bronze, and I carry flashing iron.
185	My ship stands there in the country away from the city,
186	in the harbor Rheithron under wooded Neion.
187	We claim to be guest-friends of one another's fathers
188	from the start, if you go and ask the old man
189	the hero Laertes, who they say no longer comes
190	to the city, but far off in the country suffers pains
191	with an old woman servant, who sets food and drink
192	beside him, when weariness takes hold of his limbs
193	as he creeps along the rise of his wine-growing orchard.
194	I have come now. For they said he was at home,
195	your father. But the gods are hindering that man from the road.
196	For godlike Odysseus has not yet died on the earth,
197	but still somewhere alive he is held back on the wide sea
198	on an island ringed by sea, and hard men have him,
199	wild men, who I think are holding him against his will.
200	But now I will prophesy to you, as the deathless ones
201	cast it in my heart and as I think it will be accomplished,
202	though I am no seer and do not know birds clearly.
203	He will not be much longer away from his own dear fatherland,
204	not even if iron bonds hold him.
205	He will contrive a way to go, since he is a man of many devices.
206	But come, tell me this and recount it truly,
207	whether you are really so much that man's child, Odysseus's.
208	You are terribly like him in your head and your fine eyes,
209	since we used to meet often with one another like that,
210	before he went up to Troy, where the others too
211	the best of the Argives went in the hollow ships.
212	From that time I have not seen Odysseus, and he has not seen me.
> (narrator)
213	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
214	Then I will tell you this very truly, stranger.
215	My mother says I am his, but I
216	do not know. For no one has ever known his own parentage for himself.
217	I wish I were the son of some blessed man
218	whom old age met among his own possessions.
219	But now, of the man who was born most luckless of mortal people —
220	they say I am from him, since you ask me this.
> (narrator)
221	Then the goddess, owl-eyed Athena, spoke to him in turn:
> Athena
222	The gods have not made your line nameless hereafter,
223	since Penelope bore you to be such as you are.
224	But come, tell me this and recount it truly:
225	What feast, what gathering is this? What need is on you?
226	A banquet or a wedding? For this is no shared meal.
227	They seem to me to be feasting through the house
228	in overbearing outrage. A man would feel shame
229	seeing many ugly things, anyone sound-minded who came among them.
> (narrator)
230	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
231	Stranger, since you ask me these things and question me,
232	this house was once going to be rich and blameless
233	as long as that man was still at home.
234	But now the gods have willed it otherwise, planning evils,
235	who have made that man vanish beyond all
236	people — I would not grieve so even for him dead,
237	if he had been beaten down with his companions in the land of the Trojans,
238	or in the hands of his friends, after he had wound up the war.
239	Then the whole Achaeans would have made him a tomb,
240	and he would have won great fame hereafter for his son as well.
241	But now the Harpies have snatched him away without fame.
242	He is gone, unseen, unheard-of, and to me pains and laments
243	he has left. I do not groan mourning that man
244	alone, since the gods have made other evil cares for me as well.
245	For all the best men who hold power on the islands,
246	Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus,
247	and all who are lords over rocky Ithaca,
248	so many are courting my mother, and they wear down the house.
249	She neither refuses the hateful marriage nor can she
250	bring an end. And they waste away my house eating it.
251	Soon they will smash me myself to pieces too.
> (narrator)
252	Then Pallas Athena spoke to him, growing indignant:
> Athena
253	Look now — you are much in need of Odysseus, who is gone,
254	the man who would lay hands on the shameless suitors.
255	If only he would come now and stand in the front doors of the house
256	wearing a helmet and a shield and two spears,
257	being such as I first saw him
258	in our house, drinking and taking his pleasure,
259	coming up from Ephyra, from Ilus son of Mermerus —
260	for Odysseus had gone there too on a swift ship
261	looking for a man-killing drug, so he might have it
262	to smear on his bronze-tipped arrows. But that man did not
263	give it, since he stood in awe of the gods who are forever.
264	But my father gave it to him. For he loved him terribly —
265	if Odysseus, being such, should come among the suitors,
266	they would all become short-lived, and bitter in their marriage.
267	But these things lie on the knees of the gods,
268	whether he will come home and pay them back, or not,
269	in his own halls. I bid you take thought
270	how you may drive the suitors from the hall.
271	Come now, take in and heed my words.
272	Tomorrow call the Achaean heroes to assembly
273	and make your word known to all, and let the gods be witnesses.
274	Order the suitors to scatter, each to his own,
275	and as for your mother, if her heart is set on marrying,
276	let her go back to the hall of her father, who has great power.
277	They will make the marriage and arrange the bride-gifts,
278	very many, as many as it is right should follow a dear daughter.
279	And to you yourself I will give close counsel, if you will listen.
280	Fit out a ship with twenty rowers, whichever is best,
281	and go to ask after your father who has been gone so long,
282	if some mortal will tell you, or you hear a voice
283	from Zeus, which most of all carries fame among people.
284	First go to Pylos and ask godlike Nestor,
285	and from there to Sparta, to fair-haired Menelaus.
286	For he came home last of the bronze-shirted Achaeans.
287	If you hear of your father's life and homecoming,
288	then, worn down as you are, you would still endure for a year.
289	But if you hear he is dead and no longer living,
290	then come home to your own dear fatherland
291	and heap a mound for him and give him funeral rites
292	in plenty, as many as are right, and give your mother to a man.
293	But when you have finished these things and done them,
294	then take thought in your mind and in your heart
295	how you may kill the suitors in your halls
296	either by a trick or openly. You must not
297	keep carrying childish ways, since you are no longer of that age.
298	Or do you not hear what fame godlike Orestes won
299	among all people, when he killed his father's murderer,
300	Aegisthus of the tricky mind, who killed his famous father?
301	You too, dear one — for I see you are fine and tall —
302	be strong, so that a man even among those born later may speak well of you.
303	But I will go down now to my swift ship
304	and my companions, who I think are growing impatient waiting.
305	Let this be a care to you yourself, and heed my words.
> (narrator)
306	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
307	Stranger, you speak these things meaning what is dear,
308	as a father to his son, and I will never forget them.
309	But come now, stay, though you are eager for the road,
310	so that when you have bathed and delighted your own heart
311	you may go to your ship with a gift, glad in your spirit,
312	a prized one, very fine, which will be a treasure
313	from me, such as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends.
> (narrator)
314	Then the goddess, owl-eyed Athena, answered him:
> Athena
315	Do not hold me back any longer now, eager as I am for the road.
316	Whatever gift your dear heart bids you give me,
317	give it when I come back again, to carry home,
318	and take a very fine one. It will be worth a return gift to you.
> (narrator)
319	So she spoke and went away, owl-eyed Athena,
320	and like a bird she flew upward, unseen.
321	In his heart she put force and courage, and she reminded him of his father
322	even more than before. He noticed it in his mind
323	and was amazed in his heart. For he thought it was a god.
324	At once he went among the suitors, a man like the gods.
325	The famous singer was singing to them, and they sat
326	in silence listening. He was singing the Achaeans' homecoming,
327	the bitter one, which Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy.
328	From the upper room she took in the god-spoken song in her mind,
329	the daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope.
330	She came down the high stairway of her house,
331	not alone: two serving-women went with her.
332	And when she, shining among women, reached the suitors,
333	she stood by the doorpost of the densely built roof,
334	holding her shining veil across her cheeks.
335	A trusted serving-woman stood on either side of her.
336	Then in tears she spoke to the godlike singer:
> Penelope
337	Phemius, you know many other things that charm mortals,
338	deeds of men and of gods, which singers make famous.
339	Sit and sing one of those to them, and let them
340	drink their wine in silence. But stop this song,
341	this bitter one, which always wears my own dear heart
342	in my chest, since an unforgettable grief has come down on me most of all.
343	For I long for such a head, remembering always,
344	a man whose fame is wide through Hellas and mid-Argos.
> (narrator)
345	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
346	Mother of mine, why do you grudge the loyal singer
347	pleasing us in whatever way his mind is stirred? Singers
348	are not to blame, but Zeus is to blame somewhere, who gives
349	to grain-eating men, however he wishes, to each one.
350	There is no righteous anger at this man for singing the Danaans' evil fate.
351	For people give more fame to the song
352	that comes newest to those who hear it.
353	Let your heart and spirit endure to listen.
354	For Odysseus was not the only one who lost his day of coming home
355	at Troy. Many other men died too.
356	But go into the house and see to your own work,
357	the loom and the distaff, and order the serving-women
358	to go about their work. Speech will be a care to the men,
359	all of them, and most of all to me. For the power in the house is mine.
> (narrator)
360	She was amazed and went back to the house.
361	For she took her son's sound-minded word into her heart.
362	She went up to the upper room with her serving-women
363	and then wept for Odysseus, her dear husband, until owl-eyed Athena
364	threw sweet sleep on her eyelids.
365	The suitors made a din through the shadowy halls,
366	and they all prayed to lie beside her in bed.
367	Then sound-minded Telemachus began to speak among them:
> Telemachus
368	Suitors of my mother, who have overbearing outrage,
369	now let us take pleasure in the feast, and let there be no shouting,
370	since it is a fine thing to listen to a singer
371	such as this man is, like the gods in his voice.
372	At dawn let us all go and sit in the assembly,
373	so that I may speak my word to you outright —
374	get out of the halls. See to other feasts,
375	eating your own possessions, taking turns from house to house.
376	But if this seems better and finer to you
377	to be — that one man's livelihood be destroyed without payment —
378	then cut it down. I will call on the gods who are forever,
379	in the hope that Zeus may grant that the deeds be paid back.
380	Then you would die inside the house without payment.
> (narrator)
381	So he spoke, and they all bit their lips
382	in amazement at Telemachus, that he spoke so boldly.
383	Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to him:
> Antinous
384	Telemachus, surely the gods themselves are teaching you
385	to be a high-talker and to speak boldly.
386	May the son of Cronus not make you king in sea-girt Ithaca,
387	which is your father's by birth.
> (narrator)
388	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
389	Antinous, will you be angry at me for what I say?
390	I would even wish to take this, if Zeus should grant it.
391	Do you say this is the worst thing that has been made among people?
392	It is no evil at all to be king. A man's house
393	quickly becomes rich, and he himself more honored.
394	But there are other kings of the Achaeans too,
395	many in sea-girt Ithaca, young and old.
396	One of them may have this, since godlike Odysseus has died.
397	But I will be lord of our own house
398	and of the slaves whom godlike Odysseus took as plunder for me.
> (narrator)
399	Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, spoke in answer:
> Eurymachus
400	Telemachus, these things lie on the knees of the gods,
401	who will be king of the Achaeans in sea-girt Ithaca.
402	But may you yourself keep your possessions and be lord in your own halls.
403	May no man come who would by force, against your will,
404	shear away your goods, while Ithaca is still inhabited.
405	But I want to ask you, best of men, about the stranger —
406	where this man is from, what land he claims
407	to be from, where his family is and his native fields.
408	Does he bring some news of your father coming,
409	or does he come here wanting some business of his own?
410	How he started up and is gone at once, and did not wait
411	to be known. He did not look like a bad man in the face.
> (narrator)
412	Then sound-minded Telemachus spoke in answer:
> Telemachus
413	Eurymachus, my father's homecoming is gone.
414	I no longer trust a message, if one should come from somewhere,
415	nor do I heed a prophecy, whatever one my mother
416	may call a seer into the hall and question.
417	This stranger is a guest-friend of my father's from Taphos.
418	He claims to be Mentes, son of Anchialus of the wise mind,
419	and he is lord over the oar-loving Taphians.
> (narrator)
420	So Telemachus spoke, but in his mind he knew the deathless goddess.
421	They turned to dance and to lovely song
422	and took their pleasure, and waited for evening to come.
423	And as they took their pleasure, black evening came on them.
424	Then they went each to his house, to go to bed.
425	But Telemachus, where his chamber in the beautiful courtyard
426	had been built high, in a place seen from all sides,
427	went there to bed, turning many things over in his mind.
428	With him a woman of careful mind carried burning torches,
429	Eurycleia, daughter of Ops, son of Pisenor,
430	whom Laertes once bought with his own possessions
431	when she was still in first youth, and he gave the price of twenty oxen,
432	and he honored her equally with his trusted wife in the halls,
433	but he never lay with her, and he avoided the wife's anger.
434	She carried burning torches with him, and she loved him most
435	of the serving-women, and she had raised him when he was small.
436	He opened the doors of the densely built chamber
437	and sat down on the bed, and took off his soft tunic,
438	and put it in the hands of the close-thinking old woman.
439	She folded the tunic and smoothed it,
440	and hung it on a peg beside the corded bed,
441	then went out of the chamber, and pulled the door to by the silver
442	door-hook, and drew the bolt home with the strap.
443	There all night long, covered in the wool of a sheep,
444	he planned in his mind the journey Athena had shown him.
