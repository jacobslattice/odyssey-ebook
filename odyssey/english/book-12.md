# The Odyssey: Book XII

> Odysseus
1	But when the ship left the stream of the river Ocean
2	and came to the wave of the wide-wayed sea
3	and the island of Aeaea, where early-born Dawn
4	has her house and her dancing-floors and the risings of the Sun,
5	coming there we beached the ship in the sands,
6	and we ourselves went out onto the shore of the sea.
7	There we fell asleep and waited for bright Dawn.
8	When early-born Dawn appeared, the rose-fingered one,
9	then I sent companions to the house of Circe
10	to bring the dead man, Elpenor who had died.
11	Cutting logs at once, where the headland juts highest,
12	we buried him, grieving, shedding thick tears.
13	When the body and the dead man's armor had burned,
14	heaping a mound and dragging a stone on it,
15	we planted a well-fitted oar on the top of the mound.
16	We were finishing each of these things. We did not
17	escape Circe's notice coming from Hades. Very quickly
18	she came, dressed. Her serving-women carried with her
19	bread and much meat and sparkling red wine.
20	Standing in the middle the bright goddess spoke:
> Circe
21	Hard men, who went under the house of Hades alive,
22	twice-dead, when other people die once.
23	But come, eat food and drink wine
24	here all day. At the appearing of dawn
25	you will sail. I will show the road and mark
26	each thing, so that you will not by some painful stitching of evils
27	suffer pain on the sea or on the land.
> (narrator)
28	So she spoke, and our proud heart was persuaded.
29	So then all day long until the sun went down
30	we sat, feasting on endless meat and sweet wine.
31	When the sun went down and dusk came on,
32	they lay down to sleep beside the stern-cables of the ship,
33	and she, taking me by the hand apart from my own companions,
34	sat me down and lay beside me and asked each thing.
35	I told her everything in due measure.
36	Then the lady Circe spoke to me with words:
> Circe
37	All this has been tried out so. You listen,
38	and I will tell you, and a god himself will remind you.
39	First you will reach the Sirens, who charm
40	all people, whoever comes to them.
41	Whoever in ignorance draws near and hears the voice
42	of the Sirens, for him no wife and infant children
43	stand by and are glad when he comes home.
44	The Sirens charm him with their clear song
45	sitting in a meadow, and around them is a great heap of bones
46	of rotting men, and the skins shrink around them.
47	Row past them, and smear your companions' ears
48	with honey-sweet wax you have kneaded, so no one else
49	may hear. You yourself may listen if you want,
50	but let them bind you in the fast ship, hands and feet,
51	upright at the mast-step, and let the ropes be fastened from it,
52	so that you may hear the voice of the two Sirens and take pleasure.
53	If you beg the companions and tell them to loose you,
54	then let them bind you in still more bonds.
55	When your companions have driven past them,
56	I will not then tell you continuously
57	which road will be yours. You yourself
58	must plan it in your heart. I will tell you both ways.
59	On one side there are overhanging rocks, and against them
60	the great wave of dark-eyed Amphitrite roars.
61	The blessed gods call them the Wanderers.
62	There not even winged things pass, not even the timid
63	doves that bring ambrosia to father Zeus.
64	The smooth rock always takes one of them,
65	but the father sends another in to keep the number.
66	No ship of men has yet escaped there, whichever comes,
67	but the waves of the sea and storms of deadly fire
68	carry the ships' planks and the bodies of men together.
69	Only that one seagoing ship sailed past,
70	the Argo, a care to all, sailing from Aeetes.
71	Waves would have thrown even her quickly against the great rocks,
72	but Hera sent her past, since Jason was dear to her.
73	The two cliffs: one reaches the wide sky
74	with a sharp peak, and a dark cloud stands around it.
75	That never leaves, and the clear sky never
76	holds that peak, not in summer and not in harvest.
77	A mortal man could not climb it or set foot on it,
78	not if he had twenty hands and feet.
79	The rock is smooth, like something polished all around.
80	In the middle of the cliff there is a misty cave
81	turned toward the dark, toward Erebus, the way you
82	will steer the hollow ship, shining Odysseus.
83	A strong man from the hollow ship
84	could not reach the hollow cave shooting with a bow.
85	There Scylla lives, barking terribly.
86	Her voice is like a newborn puppy's,
87	but she herself is an evil monster. No one would
88	be glad to see her, not even if a god should meet her.
89	She has twelve feet, all dangling,
90	and six necks, very long, and on each
91	a terrible head, and in each three rows of teeth
92	close and thick, full of black death.
93	She is sunk to the middle in the hollow cave,
94	and she holds her heads out of the terrible pit,
95	fishing there, feeling around the cliff,
96	for dolphins and seals, and if somewhere she can catch a larger
97	sea-beast, such as groaning Amphitrite feeds in thousands.
98	No sailors yet boast they have escaped her
99	unharmed with their ship. She carries off a man
100	with each head, snatching him from the dark-prowed ship.
101	You will see the other cliff lower, Odysseus.
102	They are near each other. You could shoot an arrow across.
103	On it there is a great fig tree, flourishing with leaves.
104	Under it divine Charybdis sucks down the black water.
105	Three times a day she sends it up, and three times she sucks it
106	terribly. Do not happen to be there when she sucks.
107	The earth-shaker himself could not save you from that evil.
108	Hug Scylla's cliff hard and drive
109	the ship past quickly, since it is much better
110	to long for six companions in the ship than for all together.
> (narrator)
111	So she spoke, and I answered her:
> Odysseus
112	Come then, goddess, tell me this so it does not miss:
113	if somehow I might escape deadly Charybdis
114	and fight the other off when she harms my companions.
> (narrator)
115	So I spoke, and the bright goddess answered at once:
> Circe
116	Hard man, war work is on your mind again
117	and toil. Will you not yield even to the deathless gods?
118	She is not mortal. She is a deathless evil,
119	terrible and hard and wild, and not to be fought.
120	There is no defense. The strongest thing is to flee from her.
121	If you linger arming yourself beside the rock,
122	I am afraid she will rush out again and reach you
123	with so many heads, and take so many men.
124	Drive very hard, and call on Crataiis,
125	the mother of Scylla, who bore her as a pain to mortals.
126	She will then stop her from rushing out later.
127	You will reach the island of Thrinacia. There many
128	cattle of Helios graze, and fat sheep,
129	seven herds of cattle, and as many fine flocks of sheep,
130	fifty in each. There is no birth from them,
131	and they never waste away. Goddesses are their shepherds,
132	fair-haired nymphs, Phaethusa and Lampetie,
133	whom divine Neaera bore to Helios Hyperion.
134	When their lady mother had raised them and borne them
135	she settled them on the island of Thrinacia to live far away,
136	to guard their father's sheep and the spiral-horned cattle.
137	If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming,
138	you may still reach Ithaca, though you suffer evils.
139	If you harm them, then I mark destruction for you,
140	for the ship and the companions. If you yourself should escape,
141	you will come late and badly, having lost all your companions.
> (narrator)
142	So she spoke, and at once gold-throned Dawn came.
143	Then the bright goddess went away up the island.
144	I went to the ship and urged my companions
145	to go aboard themselves and to loose the stern-cables.
146	They went aboard at once and sat at the benches.
147	Sitting in order they struck the gray sea with the oars.
148	For us, behind the dark-prowed ship,
149	she sent a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion,
150	fair-haired Circe, a dread goddess with a human voice.
151	At once we made each piece of tackle ready through the ship
152	and sat. The wind and the steersman kept her straight.
153	Then I spoke among the companions, grieving in my heart:
> Odysseus
154	Friends, it is not right that only one or two alone
155	should know the oracles the bright goddess Circe told me.
156	I will tell them, so that knowing we may either die
157	or, avoiding death and doom, escape.
158	She first bids us avoid the voice of the unearthly
159	Sirens and their flowery meadow.
160	She bids me alone to hear the voice. Bind me
161	in a hard bond, so I stay firm right there,
162	upright at the mast-step, and let the ropes be fastened from it.
163	If I beg you and tell you to loose me,
164	then press me with still more bonds.
> (narrator)
165	I was showing the companions each of these things, telling them.
166	Meanwhile the well-made ship came quickly
167	to the island of the two Sirens. A harmless wind was driving her.
168	At once then the wind stopped and there was a windless
169	calm. A god put the waves to sleep.
170	The companions stood up and furled the ship's sails
171	and put them in the hollow ship, and sitting at the oars
172	they whitened the water with the polished fir.
173	I cut a great wheel of wax with the sharp bronze
174	in small pieces and pressed it with my strong hands.
175	The wax warmed at once, since the great force compelled it,
176	and the ray of Helios, lord son of Hyperion.
177	I smeared it on all the companions' ears in order.
178	They bound me in the ship, hands and feet together,
179	upright at the mast-step, and they fastened the ropes from it.
180	They themselves sat and struck the gray sea with the oars.
181	When we were as far away as a man shouting can be heard,
182	driving quickly, the fast ship did not escape their notice
183	coming near, and they struck up their clear song:
> Sirens
184	Come here, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans.
185	Stop the ship, so you may hear our voice.
186	No one has ever yet driven past here in a black ship
187	before hearing the honey-voiced song from our mouths,
188	but he goes on having taken pleasure, and knowing more.
189	We know everything the Argives and Trojans suffered
190	in wide Troy by the will of the gods,
191	and we know whatever happens on the much-nourishing earth.
> (narrator)
192	So they sang, sending their beautiful voice. My heart
193	wanted to listen, and I told the companions to loose me,
194	nodding with my brows. They fell forward and rowed.
195	At once Perimedes and Eurylochus stood up
196	and bound me in more bonds and pressed me harder.
197	When they had driven past them, and we no longer
198	heard the voice of the Sirens or the song,
199	my faithful companions quickly took off the wax
200	that I had smeared on their ears, and they loosed me from the bonds.
201	When we had left the island, at once then
202	I saw smoke and a great wave and I heard a roar.
203	The oars flew from their hands in their fear,
204	and they all hummed down the current. The ship stopped
205	right there, since they no longer drove the long oars with their hands.
206	I went through the ship and urged the companions
207	with honeyed words, standing beside each man:
> Odysseus
208	Friends, we are not yet unused to evils.
209	This is not a greater evil coming than when the Cyclops
210	penned us in the hollow cave with his strong force.
211	Even from there, by my excellence, by counsel and mind,
212	we escaped, and I think we will remember these things too.
213	Now come, as I say, let us all obey.
214	You strike the deep surf of the sea with the oars
215	sitting at the benches, if somehow Zeus
216	may grant us to escape this destruction and get clear.
217	To you, steersman, I give this charge. Put it
218	in your heart, since you handle the steering-oars of the hollow ship.
219	Keep the ship outside this smoke and wave,
220	and make for the cliff, or she may rush out there
221	unseen and you throw us into evil.
> (narrator)
222	So I spoke, and they quickly obeyed my words.
223	I no longer spoke of Scylla, a pain there was no way to stop,
224	so the companions would not somehow stop
225	rowing in fear and huddle themselves inside.
226	Then I forgot Circe's painful charge,
227	since she had not told me to arm at all.
228	I put on my famous armor and taking two
229	long spears in my hands I went onto the deck
230	at the prow. I expected to see her first from there,
231	rocky Scylla, who was bringing pain to my companions.
232	I could not look anywhere, and my eyes grew tired
233	peering every way toward the misty rock.
234	We sailed up the strait groaning.
235	On one side Scylla, and on the other divine Charybdis
236	terribly sucked down the salt water of the sea.
237	When she vomited it up, like a cauldron on a great fire
238	she would seethe all stirred, and the spray fell high
239	on the tops of both cliffs.
240	When she swallowed the salt water of the sea,
241	she showed all stirred inside, and the rock
242	roared terribly around, and the earth showed underneath
243	dark with sand. Pale fear took them.
244	We looked toward her, fearing destruction.
245	Meanwhile Scylla took from the hollow ship
246	six of my companions, who were best in hands and force.
247	Looking into the fast ship and after the companions at once
248	I already marked their feet and hands above
249	as they were lifted high. They called me, speaking
250	by name, then for the last time, grieving in their hearts.
251	As when a fisherman on a jutting rock with a very long rod
252	throws bait as a trick to the little fish
253	and sends the horn of a field-ox into the sea,
254	and taking them gasping he throws them out,
255	so they gasped as they were lifted toward the rocks.
256	There at the doors she ate them screaming,
257	stretching their hands toward me in the terrible slaughter.
258	That was the most pitiable thing I saw with my eyes
259	of all I suffered, searching out the ways of the sea.
260	When we had escaped the rocks and terrible Charybdis
261	and Scylla, at once then we reached the faultless island
262	of the god. There the beautiful wide-browed cattle were,
263	and many fat sheep of Hyperion the Sun.
264	Then, still on the sea in the black ship,
265	I heard the lowing of cattle stalled
266	and the bleating of sheep. The word fell into my heart
267	of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias,
268	and of Circe of Aeaea, who charged me very much
269	to avoid the island of Helios who delights mortals.
270	Then I spoke among the companions, grieving in my heart:
> Odysseus
271	Hear my words, companions, though you suffer evils,
272	so I may tell you the prophecies of Teiresias
273	and of Circe of Aeaea, who charged me very much
274	to avoid the island of Helios who delights mortals.
275	There, she said, the most terrible evil would be for us.
276	Drive the black ship past the island.
> (narrator)
277	So I spoke, and their own heart was broken.
278	At once Eurylochus answered me with a hateful word:
> Eurylochus
279	You are a hard man, Odysseus. Your force is beyond us, and your limbs
280	do not tire at all. You are made all of iron, I suppose.
281	You do not let companions worn out with toil and with sleep
282	set foot on land, where we could
283	make a pleasant supper on a sea-girt island,
284	but you bid us wander just like this through the fast night,
285	driven off the island on the misty sea.
286	From the nights hard winds are born, destructions of ships.
287	How could anyone escape sheer destruction,
288	if somehow a storm of wind should come suddenly,
289	either Notus or hard-blowing Zephyrus, which most of all
290	shatter a ship against the will of the ruling gods?
291	But now let us obey the black night
292	and make supper, staying beside the fast ship,
293	and at dawn go aboard and put out on the wide sea.
> (narrator)
294	So Eurylochus spoke, and the other companions agreed.
295	Then I knew that a god was plotting evils,
296	and speaking I said winged words to him:
> Odysseus
297	Eurylochus, you are forcing me very much, I being alone.
298	But come now, all of you swear me a strong oath:
299	if we find some herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep,
300	no one in bad reckless doing
301	is to kill an ox or any sheep. At your ease
302	eat the food the deathless Circe gave.
> (narrator)
303	So I spoke, and they swore at once, as I told them.
304	When they had sworn and finished the oath,
305	we stood the well-made ship in a hollow harbor
306	near sweet water, and the companions went out
307	of the ship, and then they made supper skillfully.
308	When they had put away the desire for drink and food,
309	they remembered and then wept for their own companions
310	whom Scylla had eaten, taking them from the hollow ship.
311	Sweet sleep came on them as they wept.
312	When it was the third part of the night, and the stars had gone,
313	cloud-gatherer Zeus raised a hard-blowing wind
314	with an unearthly squall, and he covered with clouds
315	the earth together with the sea. Night rose from the sky.
316	When early-born Dawn appeared, the rose-fingered one,
317	we hauled the ship and moored her, dragging her into a hollow cave.
318	There the nymphs had beautiful dancing-floors and seats.
319	Then I made an assembly and spoke a word among them:
> Odysseus
320	Friends, there is food and drink in the fast ship,
321	so let us keep away from the cattle, or we may suffer something.
322	These cattle and fat sheep belong to a terrible god,
323	Helios, who looks on all things and hears all things.
> (narrator)
324	So I spoke, and their proud heart was persuaded.
325	A whole month Notus blew without a break, and no other
326	wind came then except Eurus and Notus.
327	As long as they had grain and red wine,
328	they kept away from the cattle, longing for life.
329	When all the ship's provisions were gone,
330	they went after game, wandering by necessity,
331	fish and birds, whatever came into their own hands,
332	with bent hooks, and hunger wore the belly.
333	Then I went up the island, so I might
334	pray to the gods, if one would show me a road home.
335	When going through the island I had escaped the companions,
336	washing my hands where there was a shelter from the wind,
337	I prayed to all the gods who hold Olympus.
338	They poured sweet sleep on my eyelids.
339	Eurylochus began a bad counsel for the companions:
> Eurylochus
340	Hear my words, companions, though you suffer evils.
341	All deaths are hateful to poor mortals,
342	but to die by hunger and meet that fate is most pitiable.
343	Come, let us drive off the best of Helios's cattle
344	and sacrifice to the deathless who hold the wide sky.
345	If we should reach Ithaca, our fatherland,
346	we would at once build a rich temple to Helios Hyperion
347	and put many fine offerings in it.
348	If he is angry about the straight-horned cattle
349	and wants to destroy the ship, and the other gods follow,
350	I would rather lose my life once, gaping at the wave,
351	than be drained for a long time on a desert island.
> (narrator)
352	So Eurylochus spoke, and the other companions agreed.
353	At once they drove off the best of Helios's cattle
354	from nearby, for not far from the dark-prowed ship
355	the beautiful spiral-horned wide-browed cattle were grazing.
356	They stood around them and prayed to the gods,
357	plucking the tender leaves of a high-crowned oak,
358	for they had no white barley on the well-benched ship.
359	When they had prayed and slaughtered and flayed them,
360	they cut out the thigh-bones and covered them with fat
361	making a double fold, and they laid raw pieces on them.
362	They had no wine to pour on the burning offerings,
363	but pouring a drink-offering of water they roasted all the innards.
364	When the thigh-bones were burned and they had tasted the inner parts,
365	they cut up the rest and pierced it on spits.
366	Then sweet sleep rushed from my eyelids,
367	and I went toward the fast ship and the shore of the sea.
368	When I was near, going toward the double-ended ship,
369	the sweet breath of the fat came around me.
370	I groaned and cried out loud to the deathless gods:
> Odysseus
371	Father Zeus and you other blessed gods who are forever,
372	you put me to sleep for ruin with a pitiless sleep.
373	The companions staying behind plotted a great work.
> (narrator)
374	A messenger went quickly to Helios Hyperion,
375	Lampetie of the long robe, that we had killed his cattle.
376	At once he spoke among the deathless, angry in his heart:
> Helios
377	Father Zeus and you other blessed gods who are forever,
378	punish the companions of Odysseus, son of Laertes,
379	who killed my cattle beyond measure, the ones I
380	used to take joy in going toward the starry sky
381	and when I turned back from the sky toward the earth.
382	If they will not pay me a fitting exchange for the cattle,
383	I will go down to Hades and shine among the dead.
> (narrator)
384	Cloud-gatherer Zeus answered him:
> Zeus
385	Helios, you shine among the deathless
386	and among mortal people on the grain-giving plowland.
387	I will soon hit their fast ship with a bright thunderbolt
388	and shatter it to bits in the middle of the wine-faced sea.
> (narrator)
389	I heard these things from fair-haired Calypso.
390	She said she herself had heard them from Hermes the guide.
391	When I had gone down to the ship and the sea,
392	I rebuked them one from one side and one from another, standing by,
393	and we could find no remedy. The cattle were already dead.
394	The gods at once showed signs to them.
395	The hides crawled, and the meat on the spits bellowed,
396	both roasted and raw, and there was a voice as of cattle.
397	For six days then my faithful companions
398	feasted, driving off the best of Helios's cattle.
399	When Zeus, son of Cronus, set the seventh day on us,
400	then the wind stopped raging with the squall,
401	and we went aboard at once and put out on the wide sea,
402	setting the mast and raising the white sails.
403	When we had left the island, and no other
404	land appeared, only sky and sea,
405	then the son of Cronus set a dark cloud
406	over the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark under it.
407	She did not run for very long. At once
408	Zephyrus came screaming, raging with a great squall.
409	The storm of wind broke both forestays
410	of the mast. The mast fell backward, and all the tackle
411	poured into the hold. It struck the steersman's head
412	in the stern of the ship, and it smashed the bones
413	of his head all together. Like a diver
414	he fell from the deck, and the proud heart left his bones.
415	Zeus thundered at the same time and threw a thunderbolt into the ship.
416	She spun all through, struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus,
417	and filled with sulphur, and the companions fell from the ship.
418	Like sea-crows they were carried on the waves
419	around the black ship, and a god took away their homecoming.
420	I went through the ship, until a wave loosed the sides
421	from the keel, and the wave carried her stripped,
422	and it broke the mast toward the keel. On it
423	the backstay was thrown, made of oxhide.
424	With that I bound both together, the keel and the mast,
425	and sitting on them I was carried by the deadly winds.
426	Then Zephyrus stopped raging with the squall,
427	and Notus came quickly, bringing pains to my heart,
428	so I would measure deadly Charybdis again.
429	All night I was carried, and with the rising sun
430	I came to Scylla's cliff and terrible Charybdis.
431	She sucked down the salt water of the sea.
432	I was lifted high toward the tall fig tree
433	and clinging to it I held on like a bat. I had no way
434	either to plant my feet firmly or to climb on.
435	The roots held far off, and the branches hung out,
436	long and great, and they shadowed Charybdis.
437	I held on without a break, until she vomited back
438	the mast and the keel again. They came to me as I longed for them,
439	late. When a man stands up from the assembly for supper
440	judging many quarrels of young men at law,
441	then those timbers appeared from Charybdis.
442	I let my feet and hands go to be carried down from above,
443	and I dropped in the middle past the very long timbers,
444	and sitting on them I rowed with my hands.
445	The father of men and gods no longer let Scylla
446	see me. I would not have escaped sheer destruction.
447	From there I was carried nine days, and on the tenth night
448	the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso
449	lives, the fair-haired one, a dread goddess with a human voice,
450	who loved me and cared for me. Why should I tell you these things?
451	I already told them yesterday in the house
452	to you and your strong wife. It is hateful to me
453	to tell again things said clearly.
