# The Odyssey: Book XIX

> (narrator)
1	Godlike Odysseus was left in the hall
2	considering death for the suitors with Athena.
3	At once he addressed winged words to Telemachus:
> Odysseus
4	Telemachus, we must put the war gear away inside
5	all of it. Talk the suitors around with soft words
6	whenever they miss it and ask you:
7	I put it down out of the smoke, since it no longer looked like
8	what Odysseus left going to Troy,
9	but it has been damaged, as far as the breath of the fire reached.
10	And still a god put this greater thing in my mind,
11	so that somehow drunk, setting a quarrel among you,
12	you do not wound one another and shame the feast
13	and the wooing. Iron itself draws a man.
> (narrator)
14	So he spoke, and Telemachus obeyed his own father.
15	Calling her out he spoke to the nurse Eurycleia:
> Telemachus
16	Nurse, come, keep the women in the halls
17	until I put away in the room my father's beautiful
18	gear, which the smoke in the house is wasting uncared-for
19	with my father gone. I was still a child.
20	Now I want to put it away where the breath of the fire will not reach.
> (narrator)
21	Then the own nurse Eurycleia spoke to him:
> Eurycleia
22	I wish someday, child, you would take on the good sense
23	to care for the house and guard all the possessions.
24	But come, who then going with you will carry the light?
25	You would not let the serving-women come forward, who would have shone it.
> (narrator)
26	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite her:
> Telemachus
27	This stranger. I will not put up with a man idle who takes hold
28	of my measure of grain, even having come from far away.
> (narrator)
29	So he spoke, and the word was wingless for her.
30	She locked the doors of the well-lived halls.
31	Odysseus and the shining son springing up
32	were carrying in helmets and bossed shields
33	and sharp spears. In front Pallas Athena,
34	holding a gold lamp, was making a very beautiful light.
35	Then Telemachus at once addressed his father:
> Telemachus
36	Father, this is a great wonder I see with my eyes.
37	Anyway the walls of the halls and the beautiful cross-beams
38	and the fir roof-beams and the pillars holding up
39	appear to my eyes as if of burning fire.
40	Surely some god is inside, who hold the wide heaven.
> (narrator)
41	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
42	Be quiet and hold it in your mind and do not ask.
43	This is the way of the gods who hold Olympus.
44	You go to bed, and I will stay behind here,
45	so I may still stir the serving-women and your mother.
46	She, lamenting, will ask me each thing apart.
> (narrator)
47	So he spoke, and Telemachus went through the hall
48	to lie down to the room, under shining torches,
49	where he used to sleep before whenever sweet sleep reached him.
50	There he then also lay and waited for bright Dawn.
51	Godlike Odysseus was left in the hall
52	considering death for the suitors with Athena.
53	Careful Penelope came from the room,
54	like Artemis or golden Aphrodite.
55	They set a chair for her beside the fire, where she sat,
56	turned with ivory and silver, which a carpenter
57	once made, Icmalius, and he put a footstool under the feet
58	joined from it, where a great fleece was thrown.
59	There careful Penelope sat then.
60	The white-armed serving-women came from the hall.
61	They took up much bread and the tables
62	and the cups from which the over-strong men had been drinking.
63	They threw the fire from the lamps to the ground, and on them
64	they piled many woods, to be light and to be warm.
65	Melantho rebuked Odysseus a second time again:
> Melantho
66	Stranger, will you still even now pain us through the night
67	turning through the house, and will you stare at the women?
68	Go out to the door, miserable, and have the use of the feast.
69	Or soon you will go to the door hit even with a firebrand.
> (narrator)
70	Looking from under his brows Odysseus of many devices spoke to her:
> Odysseus
71	Strange woman, why do you hold so against me with an angry heart?
72	Is it because I am dirty, and I wear bad clothes on my skin,
73	and I beg through the people? Need drives me.
74	Such are beggars and wandering men.
75	I too once lived a house among men,
76	blessed, rich, and I often used to give to a wanderer,
77	such as he was and needing whatever he came for.
78	There were very countless slaves and many other things
79	with which they live well and are called rich.
80	But Zeus son of Cronus wasted it. He wanted it somewhere.
81	Therefore now, woman, may you never also lose all
82	the splendor in which you now stand out among the serving-women,
83	so that the mistress somehow angry does not make it hard,
84	or Odysseus come. There is still a portion of hope.
85	If he has been lost this way and is no longer of homecoming,
86	already the child is such by the will of Apollo,
87	Telemachus. No one of the women in the halls
88	escapes him acting reckless, since he is no longer of that age.
> (narrator)
89	So he spoke, and careful Penelope heard him
90	and rebuked the serving-woman and spoke a word and called her by name:
> Penelope
91	Wholly, daring, fearless dog, you do not escape me at all
92	doing a great work, which you will wipe off on your own head.
93	You knew all well, since you heard from me myself
94	that I was going to ask the stranger in my halls
95	about the husband, since I am closely grieved.
96	She spoke and said a word to Eurynome the housekeeper:
97	Eurynome, bring a chair and a fleece on it,
98	so sitting the stranger may speak a word and hear
99	from me. I want to ask him out.
> (narrator)
100	So she spoke, and she very quickly brought and set
101	a well-polished chair and threw a fleece on it.
102	There much-enduring godlike Odysseus sat then.
103	Careful Penelope began the words:
> Penelope
104	Stranger, I myself will ask you this first.
105	Who of men are you and from where? Where are your city and parents?
> (narrator)
106	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
107	Wife, no one of mortals over the boundless earth
108	would quarrel with you. Your fame reaches the wide heaven,
109	as of some faultless king, who god-fearing
110	ruling among many and strong men
111	holds up good judgments, and the black earth bears
112	wheat and barley, and the trees are heavy with fruit,
113	and the flocks bear steadily, and the sea holds out fish
114	from good leadership, and the people flourish under him.
115	Therefore now ask me the other things in your house,
116	and do not ask out my birth and fatherland,
117	so you do not more fill my heart with pains
118	remembering. I am very much-groaning. I should not
119	sit in another's house wailing and weeping,
120	since it is worse to mourn unmixed always.
121	Someone of the slaves may be angry at me, or you yourself,
122	and say I am floating in tears, heavy in my wits with wine.
> (narrator)
123	Then careful Penelope answered him:
> Penelope
124	Stranger, the deathless destroyed my excellence and look and body
125	when the Argives were going up to Ilium,
126	and my husband Odysseus was among them.
127	If that man coming were going about my living,
128	my fame would be greater and more beautiful that way.
129	Now I grieve. A god has driven so many evils on me.
130	As many as are the best who hold power on the islands,
131	Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus,
132	and those who live around Ithaca itself good to see,
133	those woo me unwilling, and they wear out the house.
134	Therefore I do not care about strangers or suppliants
135	or anything of heralds, who are public workers.
136	Missing Odysseus I waste away my own heart.
137	They hurry the marriage. I wind tricks.
138	A god first breathed a web into my mind,
139	setting up a great loom, to weave in the halls
140	fine and very large. At once I said among them:
141	Young men, my suitors, since godlike Odysseus has died,
142	wait, hurrying my marriage, until I finish the web —
143	so my threads do not perish empty —
144	a burial cloth for the hero Laertes, for when
145	the destroying fate of long-lamented death takes him down.
146	Let no one of the Achaean women through the people be angry at me
147	if he lies without a wrapping, having got many possessions.
148	So I spoke, and their proud heart obeyed.
149	Then by day I used to weave the great loom,
150	and at night I used to loosen it, when I had set torches beside me.
151	So for three years I escaped notice and persuaded the Achaeans.
152	But when the fourth year came and the seasons came on,
153	the months wasting, and many days were finished,
154	then through serving-women, dogs who do not care,
155	they came on me and caught me and shouted at me with words.
156	So I finished it, even unwilling, under force.
157	Now I cannot escape the marriage and I no longer find
158	any other device. My parents very much urge me
159	to marry, and the child is distressed as they eat up the living,
160	knowing. He is already a man most able
161	to care for the house, and Zeus grants him fame.
162	But even so tell me your birth, from where you are.
163	You are not from an oak of old speech or from a rock.
> (narrator)
164	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
165	Respected wife of Odysseus son of Laertes,
166	will you not leave off asking out my birth?
167	I will tell you. You will give me still more pains
168	than I have. That is the way, whenever a man
169	is away from his own fatherland as long a time as I am now,
170	wandering over many towns of mortals, suffering pains.
171	Even so I will say what you ask me and inquire.
172	There is a land, Crete, in the middle of the wine-looking sea,
173	beautiful and rich, flowed around. In it there are men
174	many, countless, and ninety cities.
175	One language mixed with another. In it are Achaeans,
176	and great-hearted Eteocretans, and Cydonians,
177	and Dorians with three crests, and godlike Pelasgians.
178	Among them is Cnossos, a great city, where Minos
179	used to be king at nine years, companion of great Zeus,
180	the father of my father, great-hearted Deucalion.
181	Deucalion bore me and the lord Idomeneus.
182	He went in the curved ships into Ilium
183	with the sons of Atreus, and my famous name is Aethon,
184	younger in birth. He was earlier and better.
185	There I saw Odysseus and I gave guest-gifts.
186	The force of the wind led him down to Crete
187	wanting Troy, driving him off course past Malea.
188	It stood him in Amnisus, where the cave of Eileithyia is,
189	in hard harbors, and he barely escaped the storms.
190	At once going up to the town he asked for Idomeneus.
191	He said he was a dear and respected guest-friend to him.
192	The tenth or eleventh dawn was already there
193	with him gone in the curved ships into Ilium.
194	I leading him to the house guest-hosted him well,
195	loving him kindly, many things being in the house.
196	To the other companions who followed with him
197	I gave barley-meal from the people and flaming wine, gathering it,
198	and cattle to sacrifice, so they might fill their heart.
199	There the godlike Achaeans stayed twelve days.
200	A great north wind penned them and would not let them
201	stand on the land, and some hard god had risen.
202	On the thirteenth the wind fell, and they put out.
> (narrator)
203	He made many lies speaking, like true things.
204	As she heard, tears ran, and her skin was wasting.
205	As snow wastes on the high-going mountains,
206	which the east wind has wasted after the west wind pours it,
207	and as it wastes the rivers flowing are full,
208	so her beautiful cheeks were wasting as she poured a tear,
209	crying for her own husband sitting beside her. Odysseus
210	in his heart pitied his own wife groaning,
211	but his eyes stood as if horn or iron
212	unmoving in the lids. By a trick he hid the tears.
213	When she had had her fill of the much-wept lament
214	she answered him again with words:
> Penelope
215	Now I think I will try you, stranger,
216	if truly there with the godlike companions
217	you guest-hosted my husband in the halls, as you say.
218	Tell me what sort of clothes he wore on his skin,
219	and what he himself was like, and the companions who followed him.
> (narrator)
220	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
221	Wife, it is hard to say, being apart so long a time.
222	It is already the twentieth year for him
223	since he went from there and has left my fatherland.
224	I will tell you as it appears to my heart.
225	Godlike Odysseus had a purple thick cloak,
226	double. A pin of gold was made for him
227	with double pipes. In front there was worked work:
228	a dog held a many-colored fawn in its front feet,
229	grasping it gasping. All used to wonder at it,
230	how being gold the one was grasping the fawn throttling it,
231	and the other eager to escape was gasping with its feet.
232	I marked the tunic on his skin shining,
233	like the skin on a dried onion.
234	So it was soft, and it was bright as the sun.
235	Many women wondered at it.
236	I will tell you another thing, and you put it in your mind.
237	I do not know whether Odysseus wore these on his skin at home,
238	or some one of the companions gave them going on the swift ship,
239	or some stranger somewhere, since Odysseus was dear
240	to many. Few of the Achaeans were like him.
241	I gave him a bronze sword and a double
242	beautiful purple cloak and a fringe-ending tunic,
243	and I sent him off with respect onto the well-benched ship.
244	A herald a little older than he
245	followed him. I will tell you that man too, what he was like.
246	Round in the shoulders, dark-skinned, woolly-headed,
247	Eurybates was his name. Odysseus honored him outstanding of the others
248	of his companions, because he knew fitting things in his mind.
> (narrator)
249	So he spoke, and he still more raised a desire of lament under her,
250	as she knew the signs that Odysseus had shown her fixed.
251	When she had had her fill of the much-wept lament
252	then answering she spoke to him with words:
> Penelope
253	Now, stranger, though you were pitiable before,
254	you will be dear and respected in my halls.
255	I myself gave these clothes, such as you say,
256	folding them from the room, and I put a shining pin on them
257	to be a delight for that man. I will not receive him again
258	coming home to his own fatherland.
259	By a bad portion Odysseus went on a hollow ship
260	to look on Bad-Ilium, not to be named.
> (narrator)
261	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
262	Respected wife of Odysseus son of Laertes,
263	do not any longer kill the beautiful skin, and do not waste
264	the heart at all, lamenting the husband. I do not blame it.
265	Anyone else also mourns, having lost
266	a wedded man with whom she bore children mixing in love,
267	or Odysseus, whom they say is like the gods.
268	Stop the lament, and put together my word.
269	I will tell you without missing and I will not hide
270	that I have already heard about the homecoming of Odysseus
271	near, in the rich people of Thesprotian men,
272	alive. He is bringing many good stored things
273	begging through the people. The faithful companions
274	he lost and the hollow ship in the wine-looking sea
275	going from the island of Thrinacia. Zeus and the Sun
276	were angry at him. His companions killed the Sun's cattle.
277	They all were lost in the much-washed sea.
278	A wave threw him on the keel of the ship onto dry land,
279	to the land of the Phaeacians, who have been born near the gods,
280	who honored him around the heart like a god
281	and gave him many things and themselves wanted to send him
282	home unharmed. Odysseus would have been here long ago.
283	But this seemed more profitable to his heart,
284	to gather goods going over much land.
285	So Odysseus knows many profits of mortal humans
286	beyond others, and no other mortal would compete.
287	So Pheidon king of the Thesprotians told me.
288	He swore to me myself, pouring a libation in the house,
289	that a ship had been drawn down and companions were ready
290	who will send him to his own fatherland.
291	But he sent me off before. A ship happened to be going
292	of Thesprotian men to Dulichium of much grain.
293	He showed me the possessions, as many as Odysseus had gathered.
294	They would still feed another even to the tenth generation,
295	as many stored things as lay in the halls of the lord.
296	He said he had gone to Dodona, so he might hear
297	the plan of the god from the high-haired oak of Zeus,
298	how he would come home to his own fatherland
299	already long away, openly or in secret.
300	So he is safe this way and he will come already
301	very near, and he will no longer be away from his own people and fatherland
302	for long. All the same I will give you oaths.
303	Let Zeus be witness first, highest and best of the gods,
304	and the hearth of faultless Odysseus, which I have reached:
305	all these things will be finished as I say.
306	In this same lycabas Odysseus will come here,
307	as one month is wasting and the other is being set up.
> (narrator)
308	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
309	I wish this word, stranger, were finished.
310	Then you would soon know friendship and many gifts
311	from me, so that someone meeting you would call you blessed.
312	But it thinks this way in my heart, as it will be:
313	Odysseus will no longer come to the house, and you will not
314	get an escort, since there are not such directors in the house
315	as Odysseus was among men, if he ever was,
316	to send off respected strangers and to receive them.
317	But, serving-women, wash him, and put a bed,
318	bedding and cloaks and shining blankets,
319	so that well warmed he may reach gold-throned Dawn.
320	At dawn very early wash him and anoint him,
321	so that inside beside Telemachus he may care for supper
322	sitting in the hall. It will be more painful for whoever of those
323	pains this man, heart-destroying. He will no longer
324	do any work here, even being terribly angry.
325	How will you, stranger, learn of me if I am beyond
326	other women at all in mind and thoughtful device,
327	if you dine in the halls dry and badly clothed?
328	Humans become short-lived.
329	Whoever himself is harsh and knows harsh things,
330	all mortals curse pains afterward on him
331	while he lives, and when he is dead they all mock him.
332	Whoever himself is faultless and knows faultless things,
333	strangers carry his fame widely through
334	all humans, and many have said he is good.
> (narrator)
335	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
336	Respected wife of Odysseus son of Laertes,
337	cloaks and shining blankets were hateful to me
338	when first I left the snowy mountains of Crete
339	going on a long-oared ship,
340	and I will lie as I used to sleep sleepless nights.
341	I have already passed many nights in an unseemly bed
342	and I have waited for well-throned bright Dawn.
343	Foot-washings of the feet are not pleasing to my heart.
344	No woman will take hold of our foot
345	of those who are workers through your house,
346	unless there is some old woman, old, knowing trusted things,
347	who has endured in her mind as many things as I have.
348	I would not begrudge her taking hold of my feet.
> (narrator)
349	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
350	Dear stranger, no man so thoughtful
351	of far-coming strangers has ever yet reached my house
352	as you, speaking all thoughtful things very clearly.
353	I have an old woman who has close plans in her mind,
354	who raised that miserable man well and cherished him,
355	taking him in her hands when the mother first bore him.
356	She will wash your feet, though she is weak all the same.
357	Come now, stand up, careful Eurycleia,
358	wash one of the same age as your own lord. Odysseus
359	is already somewhere such in feet and such in hands.
360	Mortals grow old quickly in badness.
> (narrator)
361	So she spoke, and the old woman held her face with her hands,
362	and she threw out hot tears, and she said a lamenting word:
> Eurycleia
363	Woe is me for you, child, helpless. Zeus has hated you
364	beyond men, though you have a god-fearing heart.
365	No one of mortals yet has burned so many rich thigh-bones
366	for Zeus who delights in the thunderbolt, or chosen hecatombs,
367	as many as you used to give him, praying that you would reach
368	a shining old age and raise a shining son.
369	Now he has taken the day of homecoming away from you wholly, alone.
370	So somewhere women also used to mock that man
371	of far-coming strangers, whenever he reached someone's famous house,
372	as these dogs all mock you,
373	whose outrage and many shames you now avoiding
374	do not let them wash. She tells me, not unwilling,
375	the daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope.
376	Therefore I will wash your feet both for Penelope herself
377	and for your sake, since the heart inside me is stirred
378	with cares. Come now, put together the word I say.
379	Many much-tried strangers have reached here,
380	but I say I have never yet seen anyone so like
381	as you are like Odysseus in body and voice and feet.
> (narrator)
382	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
383	Old woman, so say all who have seen with their eyes
384	the two of us, that we are very like one another
385	to be, as you yourself speak thinking it.
> (narrator)
386	So he spoke, and the old woman took a shining basin
387	from which she used to wash the feet, and she poured in much water
388	cold, then she poured hot on it. Odysseus
389	sat at the hearth, and he turned at once toward the dark.
390	At once he thought in his heart, so that taking hold
391	she would not mark the scar and the works become open.
392	She came nearer and was washing her own lord. At once she knew
393	the scar, which a boar once drove into him with a white tooth
394	when he went to Parnassus after Autolycus and his sons,
395	the good father of his mother, who stood out among humans
396	in stealing and in oath. The god himself gave it to him,
397	Hermes. He used to burn pleasing thigh-bones for him
398	of lambs and kids. He followed with him readily.
399	Autolycus coming to the rich people of Ithaca
400	met the newly born child of his own daughter.
401	Eurycleia put him on his own knees
402	as he was finishing supper, and she spoke a word and called him by name:
> Eurycleia
403	Autolycus, you yourself now find the name you will set
404	on your child's own child. He is much-prayed-for for you.
> (narrator)
405	Then Autolycus answered her and spoke:
> Autolycus
406	My daughter's husband and daughter, set the name I say.
407	I come here having given pain to many
408	men and women over the much-feeding earth.
409	Therefore let his name be Odysseus, named from that. I
410	when he has come of age to the great house of the mother's line
411	and comes to Parnassus, where I have possessions somewhere,
412	I will give him of those and I will send him off glad.
> (narrator)
413	For these things Odysseus came, so Autolycus would give him shining gifts.
414	Autolycus and the sons of Autolycus
415	greeted him with hands and honeyed words.
416	The mother Amphithea wrapping herself around Odysseus
417	kissed his head and both beautiful eyes.
418	Autolycus called to his famous sons
419	to make supper. They heard him urging,
420	and at once they led in a male five-year-old ox.
421	They skinned it and worked around it, and they cut it all apart,
422	and they sliced it skillfully and pierced it on spits,
423	and they roasted it carefully, and they divided the portions.
424	Then all day until the sun went down
425	they feasted, and the heart was not lacking an equal feast.
426	When the sun went down and dusk came,
427	then they lay down and took the gift of sleep.
428	When early-born rose-fingered Dawn appeared,
429	they went to go to the hunt, both the dogs and themselves
430	the sons of Autolycus. With them godlike Odysseus
431	went. They went up the steep mountain clothed in wood,
432	Parnassus, and soon they reached the windy folds.
433	The Sun then was newly throwing on the fields
434	from the quietly-flowing deep-flowing Ocean,
435	and the hunters reached a glen. In front of them
436	the dogs went searching tracks, and behind
437	the sons of Autolycus. With them godlike Odysseus
438	went near the dogs, brandishing a long-shadowed spear.
439	There in a close thicket a great boar was lying.
440	The force of the wet-blowing winds did not blow through it,
441	and shining Sun did not throw on it with rays,
442	and rain did not go through all the way. So close
443	it was, and there was a pouring of leaves utterly many.
444	The noise of the feet of men and dogs came around him
445	as they came on driving. He opposite from the wood-thicket
446	bristling his mane well, looking fire with his eyes,
447	stood near them. Odysseus first of all
448	rushed holding up the long spear in a thick hand,
449	eager to wound. The boar reaching him first drove
450	above the knee, and he drew much flesh with the tooth
451	rushing slantwise, and he did not reach the man's bone.
452	Odysseus wounded him hitting the right shoulder,
453	and the point of the shining spear went through opposite.
454	He fell in the dust crying out, and the spirit flew away.
455	The own children of Autolycus worked around him,
456	and the wound of faultless godlike Odysseus
457	they bound skillfully, and with a charm they held the dark blood,
458	and they soon reached the house of the own father.
459	Autolycus and the sons of Autolycus
460	healing him well and giving shining gifts
461	quickly sent him glad to his own fatherland
462	to Ithaca. His father and lady mother
463	were glad at his coming home and asked each thing,
464	what he had suffered, the scar. He told them well
465	how a boar drove him hunting with a white tooth,
466	when he went to Parnassus with the sons of Autolycus.
467	The old woman taking it with the hands face-down
468	knew it feeling it, and she let the foot go to be carried.
469	The shin fell in the basin, and the bronze rang,
470	and it tipped back the other way. The water poured out on the ground.
471	Joy and pain together took her mind, and both her eyes
472	were filled with tears, and the blooming voice was held.
473	Taking hold of his chin she spoke to Odysseus:
> Eurycleia
474	You are very much Odysseus, own child. I
475	did not know you before, before I felt all my own lord.
> (narrator)
476	She spoke and looked at Penelope with her eyes,
477	wanting to show that her own husband was inside.
478	She could not look opposite or mark it.
479	Athena had turned her mind. Odysseus
480	feeling with his hand took her throat with the right
481	and with the other he drew her nearer to himself and spoke:
> Odysseus
482	Nurse, why do you want to destroy me? You yourself raised me
