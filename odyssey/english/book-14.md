# Odyssey Book 14

> (narrator)
1	He went from the harbor up a rough path
2	through wooded country over the ridges, where Athena
3	had marked for him the godlike swineherd, who of the household
4	cared most for the livelihood godlike Odysseus had won.
5	He found him sitting in the porch, where his courtyard
6	had been built high, in a place open to view,
7	beautiful and great, with a walk around. The swineherd
8	himself had built it for the pigs while the lord was away,
9	apart from the lady and old Laertes,
10	with dragged stones, and he had coped it with wild pear.
11	He had driven stakes outside all the way this way and that,
12	close and thick, splitting the black of the oak.
13	Inside the courtyard he made twelve pigsties
14	near one another, beds for the pigs. In each
15	fifty pigs that sleep on the ground were shut in,
16	breeding sows. The males slept outside,
17	far fewer. Those who ate them were wasting them,
18	the godlike suitors, since the swineherd always sent
19	the best of all the well-fed fat hogs.
20	There were three hundred and sixty of them.
21	Beside them dogs like wild beasts always slept,
22	four, which the swineherd, leader of men, had raised.
23	He himself was fitting sandals around his feet,
24	cutting a well-colored oxhide. The others
25	had gone this way and that with the gathered pigs,
26	three of them. He had sent the fourth to the city
27	to take a pig to the overbearing suitors by necessity,
28	so they might sacrifice it and fill their hearts with meat.
29	Suddenly the barking dogs saw Odysseus.
30	They ran at him screaming. Odysseus
31	sat down in cunning, and the staff fell from his hand.
32	There he would have suffered an unseemly pain beside his own steading.
33	But the swineherd went after them quickly with his swift feet
34	and rushed through the doorway, and the hide fell from his hand.
35	Shouting at them he drove the dogs this way and that
36	with thick showers of stones. He spoke to his lord:
> Eumaeus
37	Old man, the dogs almost tore you apart
38	suddenly, and you would have poured shame on me.
39	The gods have given me other pains and groanings too.
40	I sit here lamenting and grieving for a godlike lord
41	and I raise fat hogs for others
42	to eat. He, wanting food somewhere,
43	wanders through the people and city of men of another speech,
44	if he still lives and sees the light of the sun.
45	But come, let us go to the hut, old man, so you yourself,
46	when you have filled your heart with food and wine,
47	may say where you are from and how many cares you have endured.
> (narrator)
48	So speaking the godlike swineherd led him to the hut.
49	He brought him in and sat him down and threw thick brush under him
50	and spread on it the skin of a shaggy wild goat,
51	his own bed-skin, great and thick. Odysseus was glad
52	that he received him so, and he spoke a word and called him by name:
> Odysseus
53	May Zeus give you, stranger, and the other deathless gods
54	what you most want, because you received me willingly.
> (narrator)
55	You answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
> Eumaeus
56	Stranger, it is not right for me, even if one worse than you should come,
57	to dishonor a stranger. All strangers and beggars
58	are from Zeus. Our gift is small and dear.
59	That is the way of slaves, always
60	afraid, when young lords have the power.
61	The gods have bound the homecoming of that man
62	who would have loved me kindly and given me a holding,
63	such as a good-hearted lord gives his house-man,
64	a house and a lot and a much-courted woman,
65	for one who works much for him, and a god makes the work grow,
66	as this work of mine grows too, which I stay at.
67	My lord would have helped me much, if he had grown old here.
68	But he died — I wish the race of Helen had died
69	outright, since she loosed the knees of many men.
70	He too went for the honor of Agamemnon
71	to Ilium of the good horses, to fight the Trojans.
> (narrator)
72	So speaking he quickly bound his tunic with a belt
73	and went to the sties, where the tribes of the young pigs were shut.
74	Taking two from there he brought them and sacrificed both,
75	singed them and cut them up and pierced them on spits.
76	When he had roasted it all he brought it and set it beside Odysseus
77	hot on the spits themselves. Odysseus sprinkled white barley.
78	He mixed honey-sweet wine in a ivy-wood bowl
79	and sat opposite and urging him he spoke:
> Eumaeus
80	Eat now, stranger, what is here for slaves,
81	young pig. The suitors eat the fat hogs,
82	having no regard in their minds, and no pity.
83	The blessed gods do not love reckless works,
84	but they honor justice and the due works of people.
85	Even enemies and hostile men, who go onto
86	another's land and Zeus gives them spoil,
87	when they have filled their ships go home,
88	and a strong fear of regard falls in their minds.
89	These men know something, and they have heard some voice of a god,
90	of that man's grim death, since they are not willing justly
91	to court or to go to their own, but at their ease
92	they devour the possessions beyond measure, and there is no sparing.
93	As many nights and days as come from Zeus,
94	they never sacrifice one victim, or even two only.
95	They waste the wine, drawing it beyond measure.
96	His livelihood was endless. No one of the hero men
97	had so much, not on the black mainland
98	and not on Ithaca itself. Not even twenty men together
99	have so much wealth. I will tell it out for you.
100	Twelve herds on the mainland. As many flocks of sheep.
101	As many droves of pigs, as many wide herds of goats
102	strangers and his own herdsmen graze.
103	Here eleven wide herds of goats in all
104	graze at the edge, and good men watch them.
105	Each of them always brings them a goat a day,
106	whichever of the well-fed goats appears the best.
107	I guard these pigs and I protect them,
108	and I pick the best of the pigs well and send it to them.
> (narrator)
109	So he spoke, and Odysseus ate the meat kindly and drank the wine
110	greedily, in silence, and he was planting evils for the suitors.
111	When he had dined and fitted his heart with food,
112	Eumaeus filled and gave him the cup he himself drank from,
113	full of wine. Odysseus took it and was glad in his heart,
114	and speaking he said winged words to him:
> Odysseus
115	Friend, who bought you with his own possessions,
116	so very rich and strong as you say?
117	You said he died for the honor of Agamemnon.
118	Tell me, if somehow I might know him, being such a man.
119	Zeus knows that, I suppose, and the other deathless gods,
120	whether I might report him if I saw him. I have wandered much.
> (narrator)
121	Then the swineherd, leader of men, answered him:
> Eumaeus
122	Old man, no man coming from wandering
123	with a report would persuade his wife and his own son.
124	Wanderers in need of care
125	lie otherwise, and they are not willing to tell the truth.
126	Whoever comes wandering to the people of Ithaca
127	goes to my lady and speaks deceiving things.
128	She receives him well and loves him and asks each thing,
129	and tears fall from her eyelids as she laments,
130	as is the way of a woman, when her husband has died elsewhere.
131	You too, old man, would soon carpenter a word,
132	if someone gave you a cloak and a tunic as clothes.
133	Already the dogs and the fast birds are about
134	to drag the skin from his bones, and the soul has left.
135	Or the fish ate him in the sea, and his bones
136	lie on the mainland wrapped in much sand.
137	So he has died there, and cares afterward have been made
138	for all his own, and most for me. I will not again
139	find another lord so gentle, wherever I go,
140	not even if I come again to the house of my father and mother,
141	where I was first born and they themselves raised me.
142	I do not now grieve for them so much, though I want
143	to see them with my eyes, being in my fatherland.
144	Longing for Odysseus, who is gone, takes me.
145	Him, stranger, even though he is not here, I am ashamed
146	to name. He loved me beyond others and cared for me in his heart.
147	I call him elder brother even though he is away.
> (narrator)
148	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus spoke to him:
> Odysseus
149	Friend, since you refuse it altogether, and you no longer say
150	that man will come, and your heart is always unbelieving,
151	I will not speak just so, but with an oath,
152	that Odysseus is coming home. Let there be a good-news gift for me
153	at once, when that man comes and reaches his own house.
154	Put a cloak and a tunic on me, fine clothes.
155	Before that, even though I am very much in need, I would not take anything.
156	That man is hateful to me like the gates of Hades
157	who, yielding to poverty, speaks deceiving things.
158	Let Zeus be witness first of the gods, and the guest-table,
159	and the hearth of faultless Odysseus, which I have reached.
160	These things will all be finished as I say.
161	Odysseus will come here in this same year.
162	As this month wanes and the next one starts,
163	he will come home, and he will pay whoever
164	here dishonors his wife and his shining son.
> (narrator)
165	You answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
> Eumaeus
166	Old man, I will not pay this good-news gift,
167	and Odysseus will not come to the house again. At your ease
168	drink, and let us remember other things besides. Do not
169	remind me of these. The heart in my chest
170	grieves whenever someone mentions my careful lord.
171	We will leave the oath. May Odysseus
172	come as I want, and Penelope
173	and old Laertes and godlike Telemachus.
174	Now I lament the child Odysseus fathered, with a grief that does not forget,
175	Telemachus. When the gods raised him equal to a young shoot,
176	I said he would be among men not at all worse
177	than his own dear father, admirable in body and form.
178	Some one of the deathless harmed the balanced mind inside him,
179	or some man. He went after news of his father
180	to holy Pylos. The proud suitors
181	lie in wait as he comes home, so the race
182	of godlike Arceisius may die nameless out of Ithaca.
183	We will leave that one, whether he is taken
184	or escapes and the son of Cronus holds a hand over him.
185	But come, old man, tell your own cares
186	and tell me this truly, so I may know it well.
187	Who are you among men, and from where? Where is your city and your parents?
188	On what kind of ship did you come? How did the sailors
189	bring you to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be?
190	I do not think you reached here on foot.
> (narrator)
191	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
192	I will tell you these things very exactly.
193	If we two now had food for a time
194	and sweet wine, being inside the hut,
195	to feast in silence, and others went after the work,
196	easily then even for a whole year
197	I would not finish telling the cares of my heart,
198	all I have suffered altogether by the will of the gods.
199	I claim my birth from wide Crete,
200	the child of a rich man. Many other
201	sons were raised and born in the hall
202	lawful from a wife. A bought mother bore me,
203	a concubine, but Castor son of Hylax, whose race I claim,
204	honored me equal to his true-born sons.
205	He was then honored like a god among the Cretans in the people
206	for his blessedness and wealth and his famous sons.
207	The death-spirits went carrying him
208	to the house of Hades. The high-hearted children divided the livelihood
209	and cast lots for the holdings.
210	They gave me very few things and assigned me a house.
211	I took a wife from people of many holdings
212	because of my excellence, since I was not a useless man
213	and not a shirker of war. Now already everything has left me.
214	Even so I think you know the stubble when you look at it.
215	A very great misery holds me.
216	Ares and Athena gave me daring
217	and the breaking of men. Whenever I picked the best men
218	for an ambush, planting evils for enemies,
219	my proud heart never looked toward death,
220	but leaping far first I would take with the spear
221	whichever of the enemy men yielded to me with his feet.
222	I was such in war. Work was not dear to me
223	nor household-help, which raises shining children.
224	Oared ships were always dear to me
225	and wars and well-polished spears and arrows,
226	grim things, which are shuddering to others.
227	Those were dear to me. A god put them in my mind, I suppose.
228	One man takes pleasure in one kind of work, another in another.
229	Before the sons of the Achaeans set foot on Troy
230	I led men nine times and in fast-going ships
231	against men of other lands, and very many things came to me.
232	I would pick out what pleased my heart, and much afterward
233	I got by lot. The house grew at once, and then
234	I was made terrible and respected among the Cretans.
235	When far-seeing Zeus planned that hateful road
236	which loosed the knees of many men,
237	then they bid me and far-famed Idomeneus
238	to lead in the ships to Ilium. There was no way
239	to refuse. A hard rumor of the people held us.
240	There we sons of the Achaeans fought for nine years.
241	In the tenth we sacked the city of Priam and went
242	home with the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans.
243	Counsellor Zeus plotted evils for me, poor man.
244	I stayed only a month, taking my fill of my children
245	and my wedded wife and my possessions. Then
246	my heart bid me sail to Egypt,
247	fitting ships well with godlike companions.
248	I fitted nine ships, and the people gathered quickly.
249	For six days then my faithful companions
250	feasted. I provided many victims
251	to sacrifice to the gods and to make a feast for themselves.
252	On the seventh we went aboard from wide Crete
253	and sailed with a fair, fresh-blowing Boreas
254	easily, as if down a current. None of my
255	ships was harmed. We sat unhurt and without sickness,
256	and the wind and the steersmen kept them straight.
257	On the fifth day we reached well-flowing Egypt,
258	and I stood the double-ended ships in the river of Egypt.
259	I told my faithful companions
260	to stay right there by the ships and to guard the ships,
261	and I urged lookouts to go to the watch-places.
262	They, yielding to violence, following their own force,
263	at once began to sack the very beautiful fields of Egyptian men
264	and they led off women and infant children
265	and killed the men. The war-cry soon reached the city.
266	They, hearing the shout, at the appearing of dawn
267	came. The whole plain filled with foot-soldiers and horses
268	and the flash of bronze. Zeus who delights in thunder
269	threw a bad panic into my companions, and no one dared
270	stay opposite. Evils stood around from every side.
271	There they killed many of us with the sharp bronze
272	and they led others away alive, to work for them by force.
273	Zeus himself made this thought in my mind —
274	I wish I had died and met my fate
275	right there in Egypt, for still more pain received me —
276	I at once put the well-made helmet from my head
277	and the shield from my shoulders, and I threw the spear out of my hand.
278	I went opposite the king's horses
279	and I took his knees and kissed them. He pulled me up and pitied me,
280	and setting me in the chariot he led me home shedding tears.
281	Very many rushed at me with ash spears,
282	wanting to kill me — they were too angry —
283	but he held them off, and he regarded the wrath of Zeus
284	of strangers, who is most angry at bad works.
285	I stayed right there for seven years, and I gathered many
286	goods among Egyptian men. They all gave.
287	When the eighth year came rolling on for me,
288	then a Phoenician man came, knowing deceiving things,
289	a gnawer, who had done many evils to people.
290	He led me, persuading me with his mind, until we reached
291	Phoenicia, where his house and possessions lay.
292	I stayed with him there through a year that brings fulfillment.
293	When the months and the days were completed
294	as the year turned back and the seasons came,
295	he set me on a seagoing ship for Libya
296	planning lies, so I would bring cargo with him,
297	and there he would sell me and take an endless price.
298	I followed him on the ship, though I guessed it, by necessity.
299	She ran with a fair, fresh-blowing Boreas
300	midway over Crete. Zeus plotted destruction for them.
301	When we had left Crete, and no other
302	land appeared, only sky and sea,
303	then the son of Cronus set a dark cloud
304	over the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark under it.
305	Zeus thundered at the same time and threw a thunderbolt into the ship.
306	She spun all through, struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus,
307	and filled with sulphur. They all fell from the ship.
308	Like sea-crows they were carried on the waves
309	around the black ship. A god took away their homecoming.
310	Zeus himself, though I had pains in my heart,
311	put the irresistible mast of the dark-prowed ship
312	in my hands, so I might still escape the pain.
313	Clinging around it I was carried by the deadly winds.
314	I was carried nine days, and on the tenth black night
315	a great rolling wave brought me to the land of the Thesprotians.
316	There the king of the Thesprotians, the hero Pheidon,
317	took me in without a price. His own son came
318	and led me to the house, overcome by cold and toil,
319	raising me by the hand, until he reached his father's halls.
320	He put a cloak and a tunic around me as clothes.
321	There I heard of Odysseus. That man said
322	he had received him as a guest and loved him as he went to his fatherland,
323	and he showed me the goods Odysseus had gathered,
324	bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil.
325	It would still feed another man to the tenth generation.
326	So many stored things of the lord lay in the halls.
327	He said he had gone to Dodona, so he might hear
328	the will of Zeus from the god's high-crowned oak,
329	how he should come home to the rich people of Ithaca
330	having been away a long time, openly or in secret.
331	He swore to me myself, pouring a drink-offering in the house,
332	that a ship had been hauled down and companions were ready
333	who would send him to his own dear fatherland.
334	But he sent me off first. A ship of Thesprotian men
335	happened to be going to Dulichium of much grain.
336	He told them to send me kindly to king Acastus.
337	A bad counsel about me pleased their minds,
338	so I would still come altogether to the pain of misery.
339	When the seagoing ship had sailed far from land,
340	they at once began to contrive a day of slavery for me.
341	They stripped the cloak and the tunic from me as clothes
342	and threw around me another bad rag and a tunic,
343	torn, the ones you yourself see with your eyes.
344	In the evening they reached the lands of clear Ithaca.
345	They bound me in the well-benched ship
346	firmly with a well-twisted rope, and they themselves went off
347	in a hurry and took supper beside the shore of the sea.
348	The gods themselves bent the bond back for me
349	easily. Wrapping the rag around my head
350	I went down the polished steering-oar and brought my chest
351	to the sea, then I rowed with both hands
352	swimming, and very quickly I was outside, away from them.
353	I went up where there was a thicket of flowery wood
354	and I lay crouched. They went about groaning loudly.
355	It did not seem more profitable to them
356	to search further. They went back again
357	onto the hollow ship. The gods themselves hid me
358	easily, and leading me they brought me to the steading
359	of a man who knows. It is still my lot to live.
> (narrator)
360	You answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
> Eumaeus
361	Poor man of strangers, you have very much stirred my heart
362	telling each of these things, how much you suffered and how much you wandered.
363	I do not think those things are in order, and you will not persuade me
364	speaking about Odysseus. Why should you, being such a man,
365	lie idly? I myself know well
366	the homecoming of my lord, that he was hated by all the gods
367	altogether, because they did not overcome him among the Trojans
368	or in the hands of his own, when he had wound up the war.
369	Then the Panachaeans would have made him a tomb,
370	and he would have won great fame afterward for his child.
371	Now the Harpies have snatched him without fame.
372	I stay apart by the pigs. I do not go to the city
373	unless careful Penelope
374	urges me to come, when some message arrives from somewhere.
375	They sit by and ask each thing,
376	both those who grieve that the lord has been gone long
377	and those who are glad eating the livelihood without payment.
378	It is not dear to me to inquire and ask,
379	since an Aetolian man deceived me with a story,
380	who had killed a man and wandered over much land
381	and came to my house. I loved him all around.
382	He said he had seen him among the Cretans with Idomeneus
383	mending ships the storms had shattered for him,
384	and he said he would come either in summer or in harvest
385	bringing many goods, with godlike companions.
386	You too, old man of many griefs, since a god brought you to me,
387	do not do me a favor with lies and do not charm me.
388	I will not respect you or love you for that,
389	but fearing Zeus of strangers and pitying you yourself.
> (narrator)
390	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
391	Your heart in your chest is very unbelieving,
392	such that I did not lead you even by swearing, and I do not persuade you.
393	But come now, let us make an agreement. Afterward
394	let the gods who hold Olympus be witnesses for both.
395	If your lord comes home to this house,
396	put a cloak and a tunic on me as clothes and send me
397	to go to Dulichium, where it is dear to my heart.
398	If your lord does not come as I say,
399	set the slaves on me and throw me down from a great rock,
400	so another beggar will keep from deceiving.
> (narrator)
401	The godlike swineherd answered him:
> Eumaeus
402	Stranger, that is how fame and excellence would be
403	for me among people, both at once and afterward,
404	if after I led you to the hut and gave you guest-gifts
405	I then killed you and took your own dear heart.
406	I would then pray to Zeus, son of Cronus, willingly.
407	Now it is time for supper. May my companions be inside
408	very quickly, so we may make a pleasant supper in the hut.
> (narrator)
409	So they spoke such things to one another,
410	and the pigs and the swineherd men came near.
411	They shut the sows in their places to sleep,
412	and an endless cry rose of the pigs being stalled.
413	The godlike swineherd called to his companions:
> Eumaeus
414	Bring the best of the pigs, so I may sacrifice it for the stranger
415	from far away. We ourselves will have use of it too, we who have
416	misery for a long time, suffering for the white-tusked pigs.
417	Others eat our work without payment.
> (narrator)
418	So speaking he split wood with the pitiless bronze,
419	and they brought in a very fat five-year-old pig.
420	They then stood it at the hearth. The swineherd did not
421	forget the deathless. He had a good mind.
422	Beginning he threw hairs from the head into the fire
423	of the white-tusked pig, and he prayed to all the gods
424	that thoughtful Odysseus come home to his own house.
425	He raised himself and struck with a split of oak he had left as he split.
426	The soul left it. They slaughtered it and singed it.
427	They quickly cut it up. The swineherd laid raw pieces,
428	beginning from all the limbs, into the rich fat,
429	and he threw them in the fire, sprinkling them with the grain of barley.
430	They cut up the rest and pierced it on spits
431	and roasted it carefully and pulled it all off,
432	and they threw it together on platters. The swineherd stood up
433	to carve. He knew due things in his mind.
434	He divided it all cutting it into seven shares.
435	He set one, praying, for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia,
436	and he distributed the others to each.
437	He honored Odysseus with the long-cut back
438	of the white-tusked pig, and he made the heart of his lord proud.
439	Odysseus of many devices spoke to him:
> Odysseus
440	I wish you would become so dear to father Zeus, Eumaeus,
441	as you are to me, because you honor me with good things, being such as I am.
> (narrator)
442	You answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
> Eumaeus
443	Eat, strange man of strangers, and take pleasure in these things,
444	such as are here. A god will give one thing and let another go,
445	whatever he wants in his heart. He can do everything.
> (narrator)
446	He burned the first pieces to the gods who are forever,
447	and pouring sparkling wine he put it in the hands
448	of Odysseus the sacker of cities. Odysseus sat by his own share.
449	Mesaulius distributed bread to them, whom the swineherd
450	had bought himself alone while the lord was away,
451	apart from the lady and old Laertes.
452	He had bought him from the Taphians with his own possessions.
453	They reached out their hands to the food lying ready.
454	When they had put away the desire for drink and food,
455	Mesaulius took the bread from them, and they hurried to bed
456	filled with food and meat.
457	A bad moonless night came on, and Zeus rained
458	all night, and a great Zephyrus blew, always wet.
459	Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd,
460	whether he might somehow strip off his cloak and give it, or urge
461	some other of the companions, since he cared for him very much:
> Odysseus
462	Hear me now, Eumaeus and all you other companions.
463	I will say a word making a wish. Wine bids me,
464	the crazy one, which sends even a very thoughtful man to sing
465	and to laugh softly, and it lets him dance,
466	and it sends out a word that is better unsaid.
467	But since I first cried out, I will not hide it.
468	I wish I were as young and my force were as firm
469	as when we led an ambush under Troy, having fitted it.
470	Odysseus and Menelaus son of Atreus were the leaders,
471	and I was the third commander with them. They themselves bid me.
472	When we reached the city and the steep wall,
473	we lay around the town in the thick brush,
474	among the reeds and the marsh, crouched under our armor.
475	A bad night came on as Boreas fell,
476	freezing. Snow came from above like frost,
477	cold, and ice formed around the shields.
478	All the others had cloaks and tunics
479	and they slept at their ease, their shoulders wrapped in shields.
480	I had left my cloak with the companions as I went,
481	in folly, since I did not think I would be cold anyway,
482	but I followed holding only a shield and a shining belt.
483	When it was the third part of the night, and the stars had gone,
484	then I spoke to Odysseus who was near,
485	nudging him with my elbow. He listened at once:
486	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
487	I will no longer be among the living. The winter

497	tell Agamemnon son of Atreus, shepherd of the people,

503	I wish I were as young now and my force as firm.
504	Someone in the swineherds' steading would give a cloak,
