# The Odyssey: Book X

> Odysseus
1	We came to the Aeolian island. There lived
2	Aeolus, son of Hippotas, dear to the deathless gods,
3	on a floating island. All around it a wall
4	of bronze, unbreakable, and a smooth rock runs up.
5	Twelve children have been born to him in the halls,
6	six daughters and six sons in their youth.
7	He gave the daughters to the sons to be their wives.
8	They always feast beside their dear father and their careful mother,
9	and countless good things lie beside them.
10	The house is full of the smell of fat and groans around the courtyard
11	by day. At night they sleep beside their respected wives
12	on coverlets and on corded beds.
13	We came to their city and their beautiful house.
14	A whole month he made me welcome and asked about each thing,
15	Ilium and the ships of the Argives and the homecoming of the Achaeans.
16	I told him everything in due order.
17	But when I too asked for the road and told him
18	to send us, he did not refuse at all. He made an escort.
19	He gave me a bag he had flayed from a nine-year-old ox,
20	and in it he bound the paths of the blustering winds.
21	The son of Cronus had made him steward of the winds,
22	both to stop them and to raise whichever he wished.
23	In the hollow ship he tied it with a shining cord,
24	a silver one, so that not even a little would blow past.
25	For me he sent a breath of Zephyrus to blow,
26	to carry the ships and us. It was not going
27	to be finished. We were destroyed by our own mindlessness.
28	For nine days we sailed night and day alike.
29	On the tenth our fatherland already appeared,
30	and we were close, and we could see men tending fires.
31	Then sweet sleep came on me, worn out,
32	for I had been handling the ship's sheet the whole time, and I did not give it
33	to any other of the companions, so we would reach the fatherland sooner.
34	The companions spoke words to one another,
35	and they said I was bringing home gold and silver
36	gifts from great-hearted Aeolus, son of Hippotas.
37	Someone would say, looking at the man next to him:
> a companion
38	Look at this. How this man is dear and honored
39	by all people, whoever's city and land he comes to.
40	He is bringing many beautiful treasures from Troy
41	out of the spoil, and we, who finished the same road,
42	are going home holding empty hands.
43	And now Aeolus has given him this too, favoring him in friendship.
44	But come, let us see quickly what this is,
45	how much gold and silver is in the bag.
> (narrator)
46	So they spoke, and the bad plan of the companions won.
47	They loosed the bag, and all the winds rushed out.
48	The storm snatched them at once and carried them to the open sea,
49	weeping, away from their fatherland. I
50	waking up turned it over in my noble heart,
51	whether I should throw myself from the ship and die in the sea,
52	or endure it in silence and still be among the living.
53	I endured and I stayed. Covering myself in the ship
54	I lay there. The ships were carried by the bad storm of wind
55	back to the Aeolian island, and the companions groaned.
56	There we stepped onto the land and drew water,
57	and at once the companions took their meal beside the fast ships.
58	But when we had tasted food and drink,
59	then I, taking a herald and a companion,
60	went to the famous house of Aeolus. I found him
61	feasting beside his wife and his children.
62	Coming into the house we sat down on the threshold by the doorposts.
63	They wondered in their hearts and asked:
> Aeolus's household
64	How did you come, Odysseus? What bad spirit attacked you?
65	We sent you off with care, so you would reach
66	your fatherland and house and whatever is dear to you.
> (narrator)
67	So they spoke, and I spoke among them, grieving in my heart:
> Odysseus
68	My bad companions hurt me, and with them sleep,
69	a cruel sleep. But heal it, friends. The power is in you.
> (narrator)
70	So I spoke, taking hold of them with soft words.
71	They were silent. The father answered with a story:
> Aeolus
72	Get off the island quickly, most shameful of living men.
73	It is not right for me to take in or to send off
74	a man who is hated by the blessed gods.
75	Go. You come here hated by the gods.
> (narrator)
76	So speaking he sent me from the house, groaning heavily.
77	From there we sailed on, grieving in our hearts.
78	The spirit of the men was worn down by the painful rowing
79	through our own folly, since an escort no longer appeared.
80	For six days we sailed night and day alike,
81	and on the seventh we came to the sheer city of Lamus,
82	Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, where a shepherd calls to a shepherd
83	as he drives in, and the one driving out answers.
84	There a sleepless man could have earned double wages,
85	one herding cattle, the other pasturing silver-white sheep.
86	For the paths of night and of day are close.
87	When we came into the famous harbor, which a towering rock
88	surrounds all the way through on both sides,
89	and jutting headlands opposite each other
90	run out at the mouth, and the entrance is narrow,
91	there they all held their ships that curve at both ends inside.
92	They were tied inside the hollow harbor
93	close together. For a wave never rose in it,
94	neither great nor small, and there was a white calm around.
95	I alone held my black ship outside,
96	there at the edge, tying the cables from a rock.
97	I went up and stood on a lookout, a jagged one.
98	There neither works of cattle nor of men appeared,
99	but we saw only smoke shooting up from the ground.
100	Then I sent companions to go and learn
101	who the men were on the land who eat bread,
102	choosing two men, and I sent a third, a herald, with them.
103	They went, after they landed, on a smooth road, where wagons
104	used to bring wood down from the high mountains to the town.
105	They met a girl in front of the town, drawing water,
106	the strong daughter of Laestrygonian Antiphates.
107	She had gone down to the fair-flowing spring
108	Artacia. From there they used to carry water to the town.
109	Standing beside her they spoke and asked
110	who was king of these people and over whom he ruled.
111	She at once pointed out her father's high-roofed house.
112	When they went into the famous house they found the wife
113	as tall as a mountain peak, and they were horrified at her.
114	She at once called famous Antiphates from the assembly,
115	her husband, who planned a wretched death for them.
116	At once he seized one of the companions and made his meal ready.
117	The other two started up and came to the ships in flight.
118	He raised a cry through the town. Hearing it
119	the strong Laestrygonians came from every side,
120	thousands, not like men but like Giants.
121	They threw from the rocks man-killing stones.
122	A bad din rose at once through the ships
123	of men being destroyed and ships being broken at the same time.
124	Spearing them like fish they carried off a joyless feast.
125	While they were killing them inside the deep harbor,
126	I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh
127	and with it I cut the cables of the dark-prowed ship.
128	At once I urged my companions and told them
129	to throw themselves on the oars, so we might escape the evil.
130	They all tossed up the sea, fearing destruction.
131	Gladly my ship escaped the overhanging rocks
132	into the open sea. The others were destroyed there in a crowd.
133	From there we sailed on, grieving in our hearts,
134	glad to have escaped death, having lost our dear companions.
135	We came to the Aeaean island. There lived
136	Circe of the beautiful hair, a dread goddess with a human voice,
137	own sister of baneful-minded Aeetes.
138	Both were born of the Sun who brings light to mortals
139	and of the mother Perse, a child Ocean bore.
140	There we put the ship in to shore in silence
141	into a harbor fit for ships, and some god led us.
142	There then, going out, for two days and two nights
143	we lay, eating our hearts with weariness and pains together.
144	But when fair-haired Dawn completed the third day,
145	then I took my spear and my sharp sword
146	and went quickly up from the ship to a lookout,
147	if somehow I might see works of mortals and hear a voice.
148	I went up and stood on a lookout, a jagged one,
149	and smoke appeared to me from the wide-wayed earth,
150	in Circe's halls, through the thick brush and the wood.
151	Then I turned it over in my mind and in my heart
152	whether to go and learn, since I had seen the flashing smoke.
153	As I thought, this seemed more profitable:
154	first to go to the fast ship and the shore of the sea
155	to give the companions a meal and send them to learn.
156	But when I was close, going toward the ship that curves at both ends,
157	then some one of the gods pitied me, being alone,
158	who sent a great high-horned stag onto the path itself.
159	He was coming down to the river from the pasture of the wood
160	to drink. For the force of the sun held him.
161	As he came out I struck him along the spine in the middle of the back.
162	The bronze spear went clean through,
163	and he fell in the dust crying out, and his spirit flew away.
164	Stepping on him I drew the bronze spear from the wound
165	and laid it down there on the ground
166	and left it. I pulled twigs and withies,
167	and a rope about a fathom long, well twisted on both sides,
168	I braided and bound the feet of the dread monster,
169	and I went carrying him slung around my neck to the black ship,
170	leaning on the spear, since there was no way to carry him on my shoulder
171	with the other hand. For it was a very large beast.
172	I threw him down in front of the ship and woke my companions
173	with honeyed words, standing beside each man:
> Odysseus
174	Friends, we will not yet go down, though we are grieving,
175	into the house of Hades, before the fated day comes.
176	But come, while there is food and drink in the fast ship,
177	let us think of food, and not wear ourselves out with hunger.
> (narrator)
178	So I spoke, and they quickly obeyed my words.
179	Uncovering themselves beside the beach of the barren sea
180	they gazed at the stag. For it was a very large beast.
181	But when they had taken their fill of looking with their eyes,
182	they washed their hands and made a glorious feast.
183	So then all day long until the sun went down
184	we sat feasting on endless meat and sweet wine.
185	When the sun went down and dusk came on,
186	then we lay down to sleep at the break of the sea.
187	When early-born Dawn appeared, rose-fingered,
188	then I held an assembly and spoke among them all:
> Odysseus
189	Hear my words, companions, though you are suffering evils.
190	Friends, we do not know where the gloom is or where the dawn is,
191	or where the sun that brings light to mortals goes under the earth,
192	or where it comes up. But let us think quickly
193	if there is still some plan. I do not think there is.
194	For I went up onto a jagged lookout and saw
195	that the island is crowned by the boundless sea all around.
196	It itself lies low. In the middle I saw smoke
197	with my eyes through the thick brush and the wood.
> (narrator)
198	So I spoke, and their own heart was broken
199	remembering the works of Laestrygonian Antiphates
200	and the force of the great-hearted Cyclops, the man-eater.
201	They wept shrilly, shedding thick tears.
202	But there was no use in their mourning.
203	I counted all the well-greaved companions in two
204	and I gave a leader to both.
205	I led one group, and godlike Eurylochus the other.
206	We shook the lots quickly in a bronze-fitted helmet.
207	The lot of great-hearted Eurylochus jumped out.
208	He went, and with him twenty-two companions
209	weeping. They left us behind groaning.
210	They found in a glen the house of Circe, built
211	of polished stones, in a place open to view all around.
212	Around it were mountain wolves and lions,
213	which she herself had bewitched, since she gave them bad drugs.
214	They did not rush at the men, but they
215	stood up fawning, wagging their long tails.
216	As when dogs fawn around their master coming from a feast,
217	for he always brings things that soothe the heart,
218	so the strong-clawed wolves and lions
219	fawned around them. They were afraid when they saw the dread monsters.
220	They stood in the forecourt of the goddess of the beautiful hair,
221	and inside they heard Circe singing in a beautiful voice,
222	going back and forth at a great immortal loom, the kind of work
223	goddesses do, fine and graceful and shining.
224	Polites, a leader of men, began the words among them,
225	who was dearest to me of my companions and most trusted:
> Polites
226	Friends, someone inside going back and forth at a great loom
227	is singing beautifully, and the whole floor is echoing around,
228	either a goddess or a woman. But let us call out quickly.
> (narrator)
229	So he spoke, and they called out, crying to her.
230	She came out at once and opened the shining doors
231	and called them. They all followed together in their ignorance.
232	Eurylochus stayed behind, suspecting it was a trick.
233	She brought them in and sat them on couches and chairs,
234	and she mixed for them cheese and barley and pale honey
235	in Pramnian wine. She stirred wretched drugs into the food
236	so they would forget their fatherland completely.
237	When she had given it and they had drunk it, at once
238	she struck them with a wand and penned them in the pigsties.
239	They had the heads of swine and the voice and the hair
240	and the body, but the mind was still in place, as it was before.
241	So they were penned, weeping, and Circe threw them
242	acorns and mast and the fruit of the cornel
243	to eat, the things wallowing swine always eat.
244	Eurylochus came at once to the fast black ship
245	to tell the news of the companions and their ungentle fate.
246	He could not speak a word, though he was eager,
247	his heart struck with great pain. His eyes
248	filled with tears, and his spirit imagined lament.
249	But when we all wondered at him, asking,
250	then he told the destruction of the other companions:
> Eurylochus
251	We went, as you told us, through the woods, shining Odysseus.
252	We found in a glen a beautiful house, built
253	of polished stones, in a place open to view all around.
254	Someone there going back and forth at a great loom was singing clearly,
255	either a goddess or a woman. They called out, crying to her.
256	She came out at once and opened the shining doors
257	and called them. They all followed together in their ignorance.
258	I stayed behind, suspecting it was a trick.
259	They vanished together in a crowd, and not one of them
260	appeared. I sat a long time watching.
> (narrator)
261	So he spoke, and I slung my silver-studded sword
262	about my shoulders, a great bronze one, and my bow around me.
263	I told him to lead me back by the same road.
264	He took my knees with both hands
265	and begging me he spoke winged words:
> Eurylochus
266	Do not take me there against my will, Zeus-nourished. Leave me here.
267	I know that you yourself will not come back, and you will not
268	bring any other of your companions. But with these men here
269	let us flee quickly. We can still escape the bad day.
> (narrator)
270	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
> Odysseus
271	Eurylochus, you stay right here in this place
272	eating and drinking beside the hollow black ship.
273	I am going. A strong necessity is on me.
> (narrator)
274	So speaking I went up from the ship and the sea.
275	But when I was about to go through the holy glens
276	and reach the great house of Circe of the many drugs,
277	there Hermes of the golden staff met me
278	as I was going toward the house, like a young man
279	with his first beard, when youth is most graceful.
280	He put his hand in mine and spoke and named me:
> Hermes
281	Where now again, unlucky man, are you going alone through the ridges,
282	not knowing the country? Your companions here in Circe's house
283	are penned like swine, holding close hiding-places.
284	Are you coming here to free them? I say you yourself
285	will not return. You will stay where the others are.
286	But come, I will release you from evils and save you.
287	Here, take this good drug and go to Circe's house.
288	It will keep the bad day from your head.
289	I will tell you all Circe's deadly designs.
290	She will make you a mixed drink and throw drugs in the food.
291	Even so she will not be able to bewitch you. The good drug
292	I will give you will not allow it. I will tell you each thing.
293	When Circe strikes you with her very long wand,
294	then draw the sharp sword from beside your thigh
295	and rush at Circe as if you were eager to kill her.
296	She will be afraid and will ask you to go to bed.
297	Do not then refuse the goddess's bed,
298	so she will free your companions and take care of you yourself.
299	But tell her to swear a great oath of the blessed ones
300	that she will not plan any other bad harm for you yourself,
301	or make you weak and unmanly when you are stripped.
> (narrator)
302	So speaking the slayer of Argus gave me the drug,
303	pulling it from the ground, and he showed me its nature.
304	The root was black, and the flower was like milk.
305	The gods call it moly. It is hard to dig
306	for mortal men, but the gods can do everything.
307	Hermes then went away toward long Olympus
308	over the wooded island, and I went to Circe's house,
309	and my heart was in turmoil as I went.
310	I stood in the doorway of the goddess of the beautiful hair.
311	Standing there I called out, and the goddess heard my voice.
312	She came out at once and opened the shining doors
313	and called me. I followed, grieving in my heart.
314	She brought me in and sat me on a silver-studded chair,
315	beautiful, well-worked. There was a footstool under my feet.
316	She made me a mixed drink in a golden cup, so I would drink,
317	and she put a drug in it, thinking bad things in her heart.
318	When she had given it and I had drunk it, it did not bewitch me.
319	Striking me with the wand she spoke and named me:
> Circe
320	Go now to the pigsty. Lie down with the other companions.
> (narrator)
321	So she spoke, and I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh
322	and rushed at Circe as if I were eager to kill her.
323	She gave a great cry and ran under and took my knees,
324	and begging me she spoke winged words:
> Circe
325	Who are you among men, and from where? Where is your city and your parents?
326	Wonder holds me that you drank these drugs and were not bewitched.
327	No other man has ever withstood these drugs,
328	whoever drinks and they first pass the fence of his teeth.
329	You have a mind in your chest that cannot be bewitched.
330	You are Odysseus of many turnings, who the slayer of Argus
331	with the golden staff always said would come to me,
332	coming up from Troy with a fast black ship.
333	But come, put your sword in the sheath, and then let us two
334	go up onto our bed, so that mixing
335	in bed and in love we may trust each other.
> (narrator)
336	So she spoke, and I answered her and said:
> Odysseus
337	Circe, how can you tell me to be gentle with you,
338	you who made my companions swine in the halls,
339	and now holding me here you tell me, thinking a trick,
340	to go into the chamber and go up onto your bed,
341	so that when I am stripped you may make me weak and unmanly.
342	I would not be willing to go up onto your bed
343	unless you would dare, goddess, to swear a great oath
344	that you will not plan any other bad harm for me myself.
> (narrator)
345	So I spoke, and she swore at once, as I told her.
346	When she had sworn and completed the oath,
347	then I went up onto Circe's very beautiful bed.
348	Meanwhile serving-women were working in the halls,
349	four, who are workers through her house.
350	They are born from springs and from groves
351	and from holy rivers that flow down to the sea.
352	One of them threw beautiful purple rugs on the chairs
353	above, and underneath she threw linen.
354	Another stretched silver tables in front of the chairs
355	and set golden baskets on them.
356	The third mixed honey-hearted wine in a mixing-bowl,
357	sweet, in a silver one, and she served golden cups.
358	The fourth brought water and lit a fire,
359	a large one, under a great tripod. The water grew warm.
360	When the water boiled in the bright bronze,
361	she sat me in a bathtub and washed me from the great tripod,
362	mixing it to please my heart, over my head and my shoulders,
363	until she took the heart-wearing weariness from my limbs.
364	When she had washed me and anointed me richly with oil,
365	she threw a beautiful cloak around me and a tunic,
366	and she brought me in and sat me on a silver-studded chair,
367	beautiful, well-worked. There was a footstool under my feet.
368	A serving-woman brought water for the hands and poured it
369	from a beautiful golden pitcher over a silver basin
370	to wash. She stretched a polished table beside me.
371	The respected housekeeper brought bread and set it out,
372	putting many dishes on it, favoring me with what was there.
373	She told me to eat. It did not please my heart.
374	I sat thinking of other things, and my heart looked for evils.
375	When Circe saw me sitting and not putting my hands
376	to the food, and a strong grief holding me,
377	she stood close and spoke winged words:
> Circe
378	Why do you sit like this, Odysseus, like a man with no voice,
379	eating your heart, and you do not touch food or drink?
380	Do you suspect some other trick? You do not need
381	to be afraid. I have already sworn a strong oath.
> (narrator)
382	So she spoke, and I answered her and said:
> Odysseus
383	Circe, what man who was in the right
384	would dare to taste food and drink
385	before he freed his companions and saw them with his eyes?
386	If you are telling me in earnest to drink and eat,
387	free them, so I may see my faithful companions with my eyes.
> (narrator)
388	So I spoke, and Circe went out through the hall
389	holding a wand in her hand, and she opened the doors of the pigsty
390	and drove them out, looking like nine-year-old barrows.
391	They then stood opposite, and she going through them
392	anointed each with another drug.
393	The hair fell from their limbs, the hair that
394	the deadly drug had grown, which the lady Circe had given them.
395	They became men again, younger than they were before,
396	and much more beautiful and larger to look at.
397	They knew me and each one clung to my hands.
398	A longing lament sank into all of them, and the house
399	rang terribly around. The goddess herself pitied them.
400	Standing close to me the bright goddess spoke:
> Circe
401	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
402	go now to the fast ship and the shore of the sea.
403	First haul the ship up onto the land,
404	and put your goods and all the tackle in the caves.
405	You yourself come back and bring your faithful companions.
> (narrator)
406	So she spoke, and my proud heart was persuaded.
407	I went to the fast ship and the shore of the sea.
408	Then I found my faithful companions at the fast ship
409	lamenting pitifully, shedding thick tears.
410	As when calves in the field around the cows of the herd,
411	when they have come to the dung, after they have had their fill of grass,
412	all skip together toward them. The pens no longer
413	hold them, but they run around their mothers lowing thickly.
414	So those men, when they saw me with their eyes,
415	poured around me weeping. Their heart seemed to them
416	to be as it would be if they had reached their fatherland and the city itself
417	of rugged Ithaca, where they were raised and born.
418	Lamenting me they spoke winged words:
> companions
419	We were as glad at your coming home, Zeus-nourished,
420	as if we had reached Ithaca, our fatherland.
421	But come, tell the destruction of the other companions.
> (narrator)
422	So they spoke, and I spoke with soft words:
> Odysseus
423	First let us haul the ship up onto the land,
424	and put our goods and all the tackle in the caves.
425	You yourselves get ready and all follow me,
426	so you may see your companions in the holy house of Circe
427	drinking and eating. They have it in plenty.
> (narrator)
428	So I spoke, and they quickly obeyed my words.
429	Eurylochus alone tried to hold back all the companions.
430	Calling to them he spoke winged words:
> Eurylochus
431	Poor fools, where are we going? Why do you long for these evils,
432	to go down into Circe's hall, who will make us all
433	swine or wolves or lions,
434	and we would guard her great house by force,
435	as the Cyclops did, when our companions came to his yard,
436	and bold Odysseus went with them.
437	Those men died by that man's reckless doing.
> (narrator)
438	So he spoke, and I turned it over in my mind
439	whether to draw the long-edged sword from beside my thick thigh
440	and strike his head off and bring it to the ground,
441	though he was very close kin to me. But my companions
442	held me back with honeyed words, one from one side and one from another:
> companions
443	Zeus-born, we will leave this man, if you tell us to,
444	to stay here by the ship and to guard the ship.
445	You lead us to the holy house of Circe.
> (narrator)
446	So speaking they went up from the ship and the sea.
447	Eurylochus was not left beside the hollow ship,
448	but he followed. For he feared my terrible threat.
449	Meanwhile Circe had kindly washed the other companions in the house
450	and anointed them richly with oil,
451	and she threw thick cloaks around them and tunics.
452	We found them all feasting well in the halls.
453	When they saw one another and recognized each other face to face,
454	they wept lamenting, and the house groaned around.
455	Standing close to me the bright goddess spoke:
> Circe
457	Do not raise a thick lament now. I myself know
458	both how many pains you suffered on the fishy sea
459	and how many hostile men harmed you on the dry land.
460	But come, eat food and drink wine,
461	until you take heart again in your chests
462	as when you first left your fatherland
463	of rugged Ithaca. Now you are dried out and spiritless,
464	always remembering the hard wandering, and never
465	is your heart in good cheer, since you have suffered very many things.
> (narrator)
466	So she spoke, and our proud heart was persuaded.
467	There all the days through a year that brings fulfillment
468	we sat feasting on endless meat and sweet wine.
469	But when the year was there, and the seasons had turned
470	as the months waned, and the long days were completed,
471	then my faithful companions called me out and said:
> companions
472	Strange man, remember your fatherland now,
473	if it is ordained for you to be saved and to reach
474	your high-roofed house and your fatherland.
> (narrator)
475	So they spoke, and my proud heart was persuaded.
476	So then all day long until the sun went down
477	we sat, feasting on endless meat and sweet wine.
478	When the sun went down and dusk came on,
479	they lay down to sleep through the shadowy halls.
480	I went up onto Circe's very beautiful bed
481	and begged at her knees, and the goddess heard my voice.
482	Speaking I said winged words to her:
> Odysseus
483	Circe, fulfill the promise you undertook,
484	to send me home. My heart is already rushing,
485	and the hearts of the other companions, who waste my own heart

> Circe
504	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
505	do not let a longing for a guide by the ship be a care to you.

507	and sit. The breath of Boreas will carry her for you.

509	there is a low shore and groves of Persephone,
510	tall poplars and willows that lose their fruit.
511	Beach the ship there on deep-eddying Ocean,
512	and you yourself go into the moldy house of Hades.
513	There into Acheron Pyriphlegethon flows
514	and Cocytus, which is a branch of Styx water,
515	and a rock and the meeting of two roaring rivers.
516	There then, hero, drawing near, as I tell you,
517	dig a pit, about a cubit this way and that,
518	and around it pour a drink-offering to all the dead,
519	first with honey-milk, then with sweet wine,
520	and third with water. Sprinkle white barley on it.
521	Pray much to the strengthless heads of the dead,
522	that when you come to Ithaca you will sacrifice a barren cow, the best,
