# Odyssey Book 23

> (narrator)
1	The old woman went up to the upper rooms laughing aloud,
2	to tell the mistress that her own husband was inside.
3	Her knees were moving, and her feet were stepping over themselves.
4	She stood over her head and spoke a word to her:
> Eurycleia
5	Wake, Penelope, own child, so you may see
6	with your own eyes what you wish all the days.
7	Odysseus has come and has reached the house, coming late.
8	He has killed the proud suitors, who were distressing
9	his own house and eating the possessions and doing violence to the child.
> (narrator)
10	Then careful Penelope spoke to her:
> Penelope
11	Dear nurse, the gods have made you ravenous, who can
12	make even a very thoughtful person mindless,
13	and they have set a loose-minded one onto good sense.
14	They have harmed you. Before this your wits were of the right portion.
15	Why do you insult me, who have a much-grieving heart,
16	telling these things beside the truth, and wake me from a sleep
17	sweet, which bound me covering my own lids around?
18	I have not yet slept such a sleep, since Odysseus
19	went to look on Bad-Ilium, not to be named.
20	Come now, go down and go back to the hall.
21	If some other of the women who are mine
22	had come and reported these things and woken me from sleep,
23	I would soon have sent her to go hated
24	back into the hall. This old age will help you at least.
> (narrator)
25	Then the own nurse Eurycleia spoke to her:
> Eurycleia
26	I am not insulting you, own child, but it is true for you:
27	Odysseus has come and has reached the house, as I say,
28	the stranger, whom all were dishonoring in the halls.
29	Telemachus already knew long ago that he was inside,
30	but by good sense he was hiding the thoughts of the father,
31	so he might pay the force of the overbearing men.
> (narrator)
32	So she spoke, and she was glad and springing from the bed
33	she wrapped herself around the old woman, and she sent a tear from her lids,
34	and speaking she addressed winged words to her:
> Penelope
35	Come, dear nurse, tell me without missing,
36	if truly he has reached the house, as you say,
37	how he put his hands on the shameless suitors
38	being one, and they were always remaining together inside.
> (narrator)
39	Then the own nurse Eurycleia spoke to her:
> Eurycleia
40	I did not see, I did not learn, but I only heard the groan
41	of them being killed. We in the inner part of the well-built rooms
42	were sitting stunned, and the close-fitted planks held us,
43	until your son called me from the hall,
44	Telemachus. The father sent him forward to call.
45	I then found Odysseus standing among the killed dead.
46	They around him, having the hard-floored earth,
47	were lying on one another. Seeing it you would have been warmed in your heart.
48	Now they are all at the courtyard doors
50	in a heap, and he is sulphuring the very beautiful house,
51	having kindled a great fire. He has sent me forward to call you.
52	Come, so that you two may set your own heart on good cheer,
53	both of you, since you have suffered many evils.
54	Now this long wish has already been finished.
55	He himself has come living to the hearth, and he has found you
56	and the child in the halls. Those who were doing him badly,
57	the suitors, he has paid all of them in his own house.
> (narrator)
58	Then careful Penelope spoke to her:
> Penelope
59	Dear nurse, do not yet boast greatly laughing aloud.
60	You know how welcome he would appear in the halls
61	to all, and most to me and to the son we bore.
62	This word is not genuine, as you say,
63	but some one of the deathless killed the shining suitors,
64	angry at the heart-painful violence and the bad works.
65	They used to honor no one of humans on the earth,
66	not a bad one and not a good one, whoever reached them.
67	Therefore through recklessness they have suffered evil. Odysseus
68	has lost the homecoming of Achaea far away, and he himself is lost.
> (narrator)
69	Then the own nurse Eurycleia answered her:
> Eurycleia
70	My child, what word has escaped you, the fence of the teeth,
71	you who used to say that the husband inside beside the hearth
72	would never come home. Your heart is always unbelieving.
73	Come, I will also tell you some other very clear sign,
74	the scar, which a boar once drove into him with a white tooth.
75	Washing it I marked it, and I wanted to tell you yourself.
76	But that man taking me at the throat with his hands
77	would not let me say it, by the many-knowings of his mind.
78	Come. I will wager myself from myself,
79	if I deceive you, kill me with the most pitiable destruction.
> (narrator)
80	Then careful Penelope answered her:
> Penelope
81	Dear nurse, it is hard to guard the devices of the gods who are always,
82	even being very knowing.
83	All the same let us go after my child, so I may see
84	the suitor men dead, and the one who killed them.
> (narrator)
85	Speaking so she was going down from the upper rooms. Her heart
86	was much considering, whether she should question her own husband from far off,
87	or standing beside him she should kiss his head and take his two hands.
88	When she went in and stepped over the stone threshold,
89	she then sat opposite Odysseus, in the fire's gleam,
90	of the other wall. He toward a long pillar
91	was sitting looking down, waiting if his strong wife
92	would say something to him, when she saw with her eyes.
93	She sat long without a voice, and astonishment reached her heart.
94	With her look she would at one time look at him face to face,
95	and at another she would fail to know him, having bad clothes on his skin.
96	Telemachus rebuked her and spoke a word and named her:
> Telemachus
97	My mother, hard mother, having a harsh heart,
98	why do you turn away from father like this, and not sitting
99	beside him question him with words or inquire?
100	No other woman would hold back like this with an enduring heart
101	from a man who, after laboring many evils,
102	would come in the twentieth year to the fatherland earth.
103	Your heart is always harder than stone.
> (narrator)
104	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
105	My child, my heart in the chest is stunned.
106	I cannot address a word or ask
107	or look into the face opposite. If truly
108	it is Odysseus and he has reached the house, we two
109	will know each other even better. There are for us
110	signs, which we two know hidden from the others.
> (narrator)
111	So she spoke, and much-enduring shining Odysseus smiled,
112	and at once he addressed winged words to Telemachus:
> Odysseus
113	Telemachus, leave the mother in the halls
114	to test me. Soon she will also mark it better.
115	Now because I am dirty, and I have put bad clothes on my skin,
116	for that she dishonors me and does not yet say I am that man.
117	Let us mark how it will be by far the best.
118	If someone kills even one man in the people,
119	who does not have many helpers behind him,
120	he flees leaving kinsmen and the fatherland earth.
121	We have killed the stay of the city, who were by far the best
122	of the young men in Ithaca. I bid you mark these things.
> (narrator)
123	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite:
> Telemachus
124	See these things yourself, own father. They say your
125	counsel is the best among humans, and no other
126	man of mortal humans would contend with you.
127	We following eagerly will go along, and I say we will not
128	be lacking in defense, as much power as is present.
> (narrator)
129	Answering him Odysseus of many devices spoke:
> Odysseus
130	I will tell how it seems to me to be best.
131	First wash and put on tunics,
132	and bid the serving women in the halls to take clothes.
133	Let the divine singer holding a clear phorminx
134	lead us in a play-loving dance,
135	so that someone hearing outside would say it is a wedding,
136	either going along the road, or those who live around.
137	Let not wide fame of the killing become through the town
138	of the suitor men, before we go out
139	to our many-treed field. Then there
140	we will mark what profit the Olympian puts in our hands.
> (narrator)
141	So he spoke, and they heard him well and obeyed.
142	First they washed and put on tunics,
143	and the women armed themselves. The divine singer took
144	the hollow phorminx, and he raised desire in them
145	of sweet song and blameless dance.
146	The great house was groaning around with the feet
147	of men playing and of well-girdled women.
148	Someone hearing from outside the house would say:
> someone outside
149	Surely someone has married the much-courted queen.
150	Hard one, she did not endure to keep the great house
151	of her wedded husband all the way through, until he should come.
> (narrator)
152	So someone would say, and they did not know how it had been made.
153	Great-hearted Odysseus in his own house
154	the housekeeper Eurynome washed and anointed with oil,
155	and she threw a beautiful cloak around him and a tunic.
156	From the head Athena poured much beauty,
157	greater to look at and thicker. From the head
158	she sent curling hair, like the hyacinth flower.
159	As when a man pours gold around silver,
160	a knowing one, whom Hephaestus taught and Pallas Athena
161	every art, and he finishes graceful works:
162	so she poured grace around him on the head and the shoulders.
163	He stepped from the bathtub, a body like the deathless.
164	He sat down again on the chair from which he had stood,
165	opposite his own wife, and he spoke a word to her:
> Odysseus
166	Strange one, the ones who have Olympian houses have put
167	a heart harder than others around you among female women.
168	No other woman would hold back like this with an enduring heart
169	from a man who, after laboring many evils,
170	would come in the twentieth year to the fatherland earth.
171	Come, nurse, spread a bed, so that I myself
172	may also lie. This woman has an iron heart in her wits.
> (narrator)
173	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
174	Strange one, I am not making myself great or making light
175	or admiring too much, and I know very well what you were
176	going from Ithaca on a long-oared ship.
177	Come, spread a close bed for him, Eurycleia,
178	outside the well-based room, which he himself made.
179	Putting the close bed out there throw bedding on it,
180	fleeces and cloaks and shining covers.
> (narrator)
181	So she spoke testing the husband. Odysseus
182	was vexed and spoke to his wife who knew trusted things:
> Odysseus
183	Woman, this word you have spoken is very heart-painful.
184	Who has put my bed elsewhere? It would be hard
185	even for one who knows very well, unless a god himself coming
186	easily wishing should put it in another place.
187	No living mortal man, not even one in the prime,
188	could easily pry it over, since a great sign has been made
189	in the worked bed. I labored that, and no other.
190	A long-leaved olive bush grew inside the enclosure,
191	at the height of thriving. It was thick as a pillar.
192	Throwing around that I built a room, until I finished it,
193	with close stones, and I roofed it well above,
194	and I put on joined doors, closely fitted.
195	Then I cut the crown of the long-leaved olive,
196	and cutting the trunk from the root I smoothed it around with bronze
197	well and knowingly, and I straightened it to the line,
198	working a bedpost, and I bored all of it with a drill.
199	Beginning from that I planed a bed, until I finished it,
200	decorating with gold and silver and ivory.
201	I stretched from it a strap of ox, shining with purple.
202	I show you this sign. I do not know at all
203	whether the bed is still fast, woman, or whether already some
204	man has put it elsewhere, cutting under the olive's base.
> (narrator)
205	So he spoke, and her knees and her own heart were loosened right there,
206	recognizing the signs that Odysseus had marked for her as fast.
207	Then crying she ran straight, and she threw her arms
208	around Odysseus's neck, and she kissed his head and spoke:
> Penelope
209	Do not be angry at me, Odysseus, since in the other things most
210	of humans you were thoughtful. The gods were giving misery,
211	who begrudged us two remaining beside each other
212	to take pleasure in youth and to reach the threshold of old age.
213	Do not now be angry at this or resent,
214	because I did not love you like this at first, when I saw you.
215	Always my heart in my own chest
216	was shivering lest some one of mortals deceive me with words
217	coming. Many plan bad profits.
218	Argive Helen, born from Zeus, would not
219	have mixed with a foreign man in love and bed,
220	if she had known that the warlike sons of the Achaeans
221	were going to bring her home again to her own fatherland.
222	A god stirred her to do an unseemly work.
223	She did not put the ruinous ate in her own heart
224	before, from which grief first reached us too.
225	Now, since you have already told the very clear signs
226	of our bed, which no other mortal had seen,
227	but only you and I and one serving woman alone,
228	Actoris, whom my father gave me still coming here,
229	who used to keep the doors of the close room for us two,
230	you persuade my heart, even being very harsh.
> (narrator)
231	So she spoke, and she raised still more the desire of lament in him.
232	He was crying holding his heart-fitting wife, who knew trusted things.
233	As when welcome land appears to swimming men,
234	whose well-made ship Poseidon in the sea
235	smashes, driven by wind and a solid wave,
236	and few have escaped the grey salt to the mainland
237	swimming, and much brine has thickened around the skin,
238	and they have stepped on the earth welcome, fleeing badness:
239	so welcome was the husband to her looking,
240	and she was not yet letting go his neck at all with her white arms.
241	Rosy-fingered Dawn would have appeared to them lamenting,
242	if the goddess owl-eyed Athena had not marked something else.
243	She held the night long at the limit, and Dawn
244	she kept at Ocean, gold-throned, and she did not let the horses
245	be yoked, the swift-footed ones who bring light to humans,
246	Lampos and Phaethon, who are the colts that lead Dawn.
247	Then Odysseus of many devices spoke to his own wife:
> Odysseus
248	Woman, we have not yet come to the limits of all the contests,
249	but still behind there will be unmeasured labor,
250	much and hard, which I must finish all.
251	So the soul of Teiresias prophesied to me
252	on that day when I went down into the house of Hades,
253	seeking a homecoming for the companions and for myself.
254	Come, let us go to the bed, woman, so that now
255	we two lying down may also take pleasure under sweet sleep.
> (narrator)
256	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
257	The bed will be there for you whenever in your heart
258	you wish, since the gods have made you reach
259	the well-built house and your fatherland earth.
260	But since you have marked it and a god has thrown it into your heart,
261	come tell me the contest, since I think even later
262	I will learn it, and to know it at once is not worse at all.
> (narrator)
263	Answering her Odysseus of many devices spoke:
> Odysseus
264	Strange one, why are you bidding me again urging me much
265	to tell? I will speak and I will not hide it.
266	Your heart will not be glad. I myself
267	am not glad either, since he has bid me go to very many
268	towns of mortals, having in my hands a well-fitted oar,
269	until I reach those who do not know the sea,
270	men, and they do not eat food mixed with salt,
271	and they do not know ships with purple cheeks,
272	or well-fitted oars, which are wings for ships.
273	He told me this very clear sign, and I will not hide it from you:
274	whenever another traveler meeting me
275	says I have a winnowing-fan on my bright shoulder,
276	then he bid me, planting the oar in the earth,
277	and doing beautiful sacred things to lord Poseidon,
278	a ram and a bull and a boar, mounter of sows,
279	to go home, and to do sacred hecatombs
280	to the deathless gods who have the wide sky,
281	to all in order. Death will come to me myself from the sea
282	very gentle, of such a kind, which will kill me
283	worn out under sleek old age. Around me the people
284	will be blessed. He said all these things would be finished.
> (narrator)
285	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
286	If the gods finish old age as better,
287	there is hope then for you of an escape from evils.
> (narrator)
288	So they were saying such things to each other.
289	Meanwhile Eurynome and the nurse were making the bed
290	of soft clothing, under shining torches.
291	When they had spread the close bed hurrying,
292	the old woman going to lie down had gone back to the house,
293	and Eurynome the room-attendant was leading them
294	going to the bed, holding a torch in her hands.
295	Leading them into the room she went back. They then
296	welcome reached the ordinance of the old bed.
297	Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd
298	stopped the feet from the dance, and they stopped the women,
299	and they themselves were going to bed through the shadowy halls.
300	When the two had taken pleasure of lovely love,
301	they were taking pleasure in words, telling each other,
302	she how many things she had held up in the halls, shining among women,
303	looking at the destructive crowd of suitor men,
304	who because of her many cattle and fat sheep
305	were slaughtering, and much wine was being drawn from the jars.
306	Zeus-born Odysseus how many cares he had put
307	on humans and how many he himself had labored being miserable,
308	he was telling all. She was taking pleasure hearing, and sleep
309	was not falling on her lids before he told everything.
310	He began how first he overcame the Cicones, then
311	he came to the rich plowland of Lotus-eater men,
312	and how many things the Cyclops did, and how he paid the penalty
313	of the strong companions, whom he was eating and did not pity,
314	and how he reached Aeolus, who received him readily
315	and sent him, and it was not yet the portion to reach the own fatherland,
316	but a storm snatching him again
317	was carrying him on the fishy sea groaning heavily,
318	and how he reached Telepylus of the Laestrygonians,
319	who destroyed the ships and the well-greaved companions
320	all. Odysseus alone escaped under with the black ship.
321	He told the trick of Circe and her many-deviceness,
322	and how he came to the moldy house of Hades
323	to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias,
324	with a many-benched ship, and he saw all the companions
325	and the mother, who bore him and reared him being small,
326	and how he heard the voice of the close-packed Sirens,
327	and how he reached the Wandering rocks and terrible Charybdis
328	and Scylla, whom heart-unharmed men have never yet escaped,
329	and how the companions killed the cattle of Helios,
330	and how Zeus who thunders high struck the swift ship
331	with a sooty thunderbolt, and the good companions perished
332	all alike, and he himself escaped the bad fates,
333	and how he reached the island Ogygia and the nymph Calypso,
334	who was holding him, longing to be a husband,
335	in the hollow caves, and she was rearing him and saying
336	she would make him deathless and ageless all the days.
337	But she never persuaded the heart in his chest.
338	And how he reached the Phaeacians after laboring many things,
339	who honored him around the heart like a god
340	and sent him with a ship to his own fatherland earth,
341	giving bronze and gold in plenty and clothing.
342	This last word he said, when sweet sleep
343	limb-loosening rushed on him, loosening the cares of the heart.
344	The goddess owl-eyed Athena marked something else again.
345	When she hoped in her own heart that Odysseus
346	had taken pleasure of the bed of his own wife and also of sleep,
347	at once from Ocean she raised gold-throned early-born
348	to bring light to humans. Odysseus rose
349	from the soft bed, and he laid a word on his wife:
> Odysseus
350	Woman, we have already had our fill of many contests,
351	both of us. You here my much-caring homecoming
352	crying. Zeus and the other gods were binding me
353	eager, away from my fatherland earth, with pains.
354	Now since we two have reached the much-loved bed,
355	the possessions that I have, take care of them in the halls,
356	and the sheep that the overbearing suitors have cut down,
357	I myself will plunder many, and the Achaeans will give
358	others, until they fill all the folds.
359	I will go to the many-treed field
360	to see the good father, who is closely grieving for me.
361	To you, woman, I lay these things on, even being wise:
362	at once with the sun going up a rumor will go
363	of the suitor men, whom I killed in the halls.
364	Going up to the upper rooms with the serving women
365	sit, and do not look at anyone or ask.
> (narrator)
366	He spoke and put beautiful armor around his shoulders,
367	and he raised Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd,
368	and he bid all of them take warlike gear in their hands.
369	They did not disobey him, and they were arming with bronze,
370	and they opened the doors and went out. Odysseus was leading.
371	Already there was light on the earth, and Athena
372	hiding them with night was leading them quickly out of the city.
