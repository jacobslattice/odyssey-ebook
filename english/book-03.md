# Odyssey Book 3

> (narrator)
1	The Sun sprang up, leaving the lake of great beauty,
2	into the sky of much bronze, so he might shine for the immortals
3	and for mortal men upon the grain-giving earth.
4	And they came to Pylos, Neleus's well-built city.
5	They arrived. Those on the seashore were performing sacred rites,
6	all-black bulls, to the earth-shaker of the dark-blue mane.
7	There were nine companies, and five hundred in each
8	sat, and at each place they held out nine bulls.
9	When they had tasted the innards and were burning the thigh-pieces for the god,
10	the others put straight in to shore, and the sails of the well-balanced ship
11	they furled, gathering them up; they moored her, and they themselves stepped out.
12	Then Telemachus stepped from the ship, and Athena led.
13	The goddess spoke to him first, owl-eyed Athena:
> Athena
14	Telemachus, you must not still feel shame, not even a little.
15	This is why you sailed the sea: so you might learn news
16	of your father, where the earth has hidden him and what fate he has met.
17	But come now, go straight to Nestor, tamer of horses.
18	Let us see what counsel he has hidden in his breast.
19	You yourself must entreat him, so he will speak what does not miss.
20	He will not tell a lie; for he is very clear-headed.
> (narrator)
21	Then clear-headed Telemachus spoke in answer to her:
> Telemachus
22	Mentor, how am I to go? How am I to greet him?
23	I have not yet been tried at all in close-woven words.
24	And shame holds a young man back from questioning an older one.
> (narrator)
25	Then the goddess spoke to him, owl-eyed Athena:
> Athena
26	Telemachus, some things you yourself will think out in your own mind,
27	and a god will put others into your mind. For I do not think
28	you were born and raised against the will of the gods.
> (narrator)
29	So speaking, Pallas Athena led the way
30	swiftly; and he then walked in the tracks of the goddess.
31	They came to the gathering and the seats of the men of Pylos,
32	where Nestor sat with his sons, and around them companions
33	were making ready the feast, roasting meats and piercing others on spits.
34	And when they saw the strangers, they all came in a body,
35	took them by the hands in greeting, and bid them sit.
36	First Nestor's son Pisistratus, coming close,
37	took both by the hand and seated them beside the feast
38	on soft fleeces on the sands of the sea,
39	beside his brother Thrasymedes and his own father.
40	Then he gave them portions of the innards, and poured wine
41	in a golden cup; and pledging them he spoke
42	to Pallas Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus:
> Pisistratus
43	Pray now, stranger, to lord Poseidon;
44	for you have come upon his feast, arriving here.
45	But when you have poured a libation and prayed, as is right,
46	then give this man too a cup of honey-sweet wine
47	to pour a libation, since I think this one too will pray
48	to the immortals. All men have need of the gods.
49	But he is younger, and of the same age as I myself;
50	therefore I will give the golden cup first to you.
> (narrator)
51	So saying he placed in her hand the cup of sweet wine.
52	And Athena was glad at the clear-headed, just man,
53	because he gave the golden cup first to her.
54	At once she prayed many things to lord Poseidon:
> Athena
55	Hear me, Poseidon who holds the earth, and do not begrudge
56	to bring these works to completion for us as we pray.
57	First of all grant glory to Nestor and his sons,
58	and then give a graceful return to the others,
59	all the Pylians, for the far-famed hecatomb.
60	And grant still that Telemachus and I go back with our work done,
61	the work for which we came here with a swift black ship.
> (narrator)
62	So she prayed then, and she herself was bringing it all to pass.
63	She gave Telemachus a beautiful two-handled cup.
64	And in the same way the dear son of Odysseus prayed.
65	When they had roasted the outer meats and drawn them off,
66	they divided the portions and feasted on the high-glory feast.
67	But when they had put from them the desire of drink and food,
68	Gerenian horseman Nestor began the talk among them:
> Nestor
69	Now it is better to ask and inquire
70	of the strangers, who they are, since they have taken their pleasure in food.
71	Strangers, who are you? From where do you sail the wet paths?
72	Is it on some business, or do you wander at random
73	like pirates over the sea, who roam
74	staking their lives, bringing harm to men of other lands?
> (narrator)
75	Then clear-headed Telemachus spoke in answer to him,
76	taking heart; for Athena herself had put courage in his mind,
77	so that he might ask him about his father who was gone away,
78	and so that he might have noble glory among men:
> Telemachus
79	Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans,
80	you ask from where we are. I will tell it out for you.
81	We have come from Ithaca that lies under Neion.
82	This business I speak of is my own, not the people's.
83	I go after the wide glory of my father, if I may hear it somewhere,
84	of godlike Odysseus of the enduring heart, whom they say once
85	fighting beside you sacked the city of the Trojans.
86	For all the others, as many as made war on the Trojans,
87	we have learned where each perished in a painful death,
88	but of that man the son of Cronus has made even his death without news.
89	For no one can say clearly where he has died,
90	whether he was beaten down on the mainland by men who hated him,
91	or in the open sea among the waves of Amphitrite.
92	Therefore now I come to your knees, if you should be willing
93	to tell that man's painful death, if you have seen it somewhere
94	with your own eyes, or have heard the tale of another
95	who was driven off course. For his mother bore him to be beyond others in misery.
96	And do not, out of shame before me, sweeten it, nor out of pity,
97	but tell me well in order how you met with the sight.
98	I beg you, if ever my father, noble Odysseus,
99	undertook and finished some word or some deed for you
100	in the land of the Trojans, where you Achaeans suffered pains,
101	remember those things now for me, and tell me what does not miss.
> (narrator)
102	Then Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
> Nestor
103	Dear friend, since you have reminded me of the misery that in that
104	land we sons of the Achaeans, unstoppable in force, endured —
105	both all that we, with the ships, on the mist-dark sea
106	roaming after plunder, wherever Achilles would lead,
107	and all that we also fought around the great city of lord Priam.
108	And there then were killed as many as were the best.
109	There lies warlike Ajax, and there Achilles,
110	and there Patroclus, a counsellor equal to the gods,
111	and there my own dear son, both strong and blameless,
112	Antilochus, outstanding as a swift runner and a fighter.
113	And besides these we suffered many other evils. Who of mortal men
114	could tell all those things?
115	Not even if you stayed five years and six years
116	and asked how many evils the godlike Achaeans suffered there
117	would you reach your fatherland worn out before you finished.
118	For nine years we stitched evils for them, working at it
119	with every kind of trick, and only with difficulty did the son of Cronus bring it to an end.
120	There no one ever wanted to match himself in counsel face to face,
121	since godlike Odysseus far surpassed them
122	in every kind of trick — your father, if truly
123	you are that man's offspring. Awe holds me as I look at you.
124	For your words are like his. You would not say
125	a younger man could speak so like him.
126	There, as long as I and godlike Odysseus
127	never spoke apart in the assembly or in the council,
128	but having one spirit, with mind and careful counsel,
129	we planned for the Argives how things might turn out far the best.
130	But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam,
131	we went in the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans,
132	and then Zeus planned a painful homecoming in his mind
133	for the Argives, since they were not all thoughtful or just.
134	Therefore many of them met an evil doom
135	from the deadly wrath of the owl-eyed one, daughter of a mighty father.
136	She set strife between the two sons of Atreus.
137	The two of them called an assembly of all the Achaeans —
138	recklessly, and not in good order, at the setting of the sun —
139	and the sons of the Achaeans came heavy with wine,
140	and the two of them spoke the word for which they had gathered the people.
141	Then Menelaus bid all the Achaeans
142	to think of homecoming over the wide back of the sea,
143	but it did not please Agamemnon at all. For he wanted
144	to hold the people back and offer holy hecatombs,
145	so that he might heal the terrible anger of Athena —
146	a fool, and he did not even know this, that she was not going to be persuaded.
147	For the mind of the gods who live forever is not turned at once.
148	So the two of them stood exchanging hard words,
149	and the well-greaved Achaeans sprang up
150	with a god-sent cry, and the plan that pleased them was split in two.
151	We spent the night turning hard things over in our minds
152	against one another. For Zeus was making ready the pain of evil.
153	At dawn some of us hauled the ships down to the bright sea
154	and put our goods aboard, and the deep-girdled women.
155	Half the people were held back, staying
156	there beside Atreus's son Agamemnon, shepherd of the people;
157	and half of us went aboard and drove on. The ships very swiftly
158	sailed, and a god laid smooth the sea of great sea-beasts.
159	Coming to Tenedos we offered holy things to the gods,
160	eager for home. But Zeus was not yet planning a homecoming,
161	the hard one, who stirred an evil strife a second time again.
162	Some turned around their ships, curved at both ends, and went
163	with lord Odysseus, the war-minded, of changeful counsel,
164	doing a favor again to Atreus's son Agamemnon.
165	But I, with the ships in a body that followed me,
166	fled, since I knew that a god was planning evils.
167	And the warlike son of Tydeus fled, and he roused his companions.
168	Late then after us two came fair-haired Menelaus,
169	and he caught us in Lesbos as we were turning over the long voyage in our minds —
170	whether we should sail above rugged Chios,
171	by the island of Psyria, keeping Chios on the left,
172	or below Chios, past windy Mimas.
173	We asked the god to show a sign. And he
174	showed it to us, and bid us cut the open sea through the middle to Euboea,
175	so that we might slip out from under the evil as quickly as possible.
176	A shrill wind rose to blow. The ships very swiftly
177	ran over the fish-full paths, and to Geraestus
178	they put in at night. And to Poseidon many
179	thigh-pieces of bulls we set, having measured the great open sea.
180	It was the fourth day when in Argos the well-balanced ships
181	the companions of Tydeus's son, Diomedes tamer of horses,
182	were setting up. But I held on for Pylos, and never did the wind
183	die, after the god first sent it forth to blow.
184	So I came, dear child, without news, and I know nothing
185	of those, which of the Achaeans were saved and which perished.
186	But whatever I learn sitting in our halls,
187	as is right, you shall know, and I will not hide it from you.
188	Well, they say the spear-fighting Myrmidons came home,
189	whom the shining son of great-hearted Achilles led,
190	and well, Philoctetes, the splendid son of Poeas.
191	And Idomeneus brought all his companions to Crete,
192	who escaped from the war, and the sea took none of them from him.
193	And of Atreus's son you yourselves hear, though you are far away,
194	how he came, and how Aegisthus planned a painful death.
195	But that man paid a most miserable price.
196	How good it is that a son be left behind of a man who has died,
197	since that man too paid back the killer of his father,
198	Aegisthus of the tricky counsel, who killed his famous father.
199	And you, dear friend — for I see you are very handsome and tall —
200	be brave, so that someone of those born later may speak well of you too.
> (narrator)
201	Then clear-headed Telemachus spoke in answer to him:
> Telemachus
202	Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans,
203	that man paid it back very fully, and the Achaeans
204	will carry his wide glory even for those who will be, to hear of it.
205	If only the gods would put such power around me,
206	to pay the suitors back for their painful transgression,
207	who in their outrage devise reckless things against me.
208	But the gods have not spun such happiness for me,
209	for my father and for me. Now I must endure it all the same.
> (narrator)
210	Then Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
> Nestor
211	Dear friend, since you have reminded me of this and spoken it,
212	they say many suitors for the sake of your mother
213	in your halls, against your will, are devising evils.
214	Tell me, are you willingly put under, or do the people
215	hate you through the district, following a god's voice?
216	Who knows if he will someday come and pay them back for their violence,
217	either he alone, or all the Achaeans with him?
218	For if owl-eyed Athena should be willing to love you
219	as she then cared for glorious Odysseus
220	in the land of the Trojans, where we Achaeans suffered pains —
221	for I have never yet seen gods loving so openly
222	as Pallas Athena stood openly by that man —
223	if she should be willing to love you so and care for you in her heart,
224	then some of those men would even forget their marrying.
> (narrator)
225	Then clear-headed Telemachus spoke in answer to him:
> Telemachus
226	Old man, I do not think this word will ever be fulfilled.
227	You have said too great a thing. Wonder holds me. These things would not
228	happen for me even in my hoping, not even if the gods should wish it so.
> (narrator)
229	Then the goddess spoke to him, owl-eyed Athena:
> Athena
230	Telemachus, what kind of word has escaped the barrier of your teeth?
231	A god, if he is willing, could easily save a man even from far away.
232	I for my part would rather, even after suffering many pains,
233	come home and see the day of coming home
234	than come and die at my own hearth, as Agamemnon
235	died by the trick of Aegisthus and of his own wife.
236	But death that comes alike to all — not even the gods
237	can fend it from a man they love, whenever
238	the deadly portion of long-grieving death takes him down.
> (narrator)
239	Then clear-headed Telemachus spoke in answer to her:
> Telemachus
240	Mentor, let us no longer talk of these things, though we care.
241	For that man there is no true homecoming anymore. Already
242	the immortals have planned death and the black doom for him.
243	Now I want to ask another word and inquire
244	of Nestor, since he knows beyond others of rights and of good sense.
245	For they say he has been king over three generations of men.
246	He seems to me like an immortal to look at.
247	Nestor, son of Neleus, you tell the truth:
248	How did Atreus's son die, wide-ruling Agamemnon?
249	Where was Menelaus? What death did Aegisthus of the tricky counsel
250	plan for him, since he killed a man far better?
251	Was he not in Achaean Argos, but somewhere else
252	was he driven among men, and the other took heart and killed him?
> (narrator)
253	Then Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
> Nestor
254	Then I will tell you, child, all the true things.
255	You yourself guess these things, how it would have happened,
256	if he had found Aegisthus still alive in the halls —
257	Atreus's son coming from Troy, fair-haired Menelaus.
258	Then they would not have poured the heaped earth over him even in death,
259	but the dogs and the birds would have eaten him
260	lying in the plain far from the city, and no one
261	of the Achaean women would have wept for him. For he planned a very great deed.
262	We sat there completing many labors,
263	while he at ease in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos
264	kept charming Agamemnon's wife with many words.
265	She at first refused the unseemly deed,
266	bright Clytemnestra. For she had a good mind.
267	And there was also a singer with her, a man whom Atreus's son
268	going to Troy charged with many instructions, to guard his wife.
269	But when the portion of the gods bound her to be overcome,
270	then he led the singer to a deserted island
271	and left him to become prey and spoil for birds,
272	and he led her, willing, she willing, to his own house.
273	He burned many thigh-pieces on the gods' holy altars,
274	and he hung up many offerings, woven cloth and gold,
275	having finished the great deed he had never hoped for in his heart.
276	For we were sailing together, coming from Troy,
277	Atreus's son and I, feeling friendship for one another.
278	But when we reached holy Sunium, the cape of Athens,
279	there Phoebus Apollo came upon the steersman of Menelaus
280	and killed him with his gentle arrows,
281	as he held the steering-oar of the running ship in his hands,
282	Phrontis, son of Onetor, who surpassed the tribes of men
283	at steering a ship, whenever the storm-winds rushed.
284	So that man was held there, though he was eager for the road,
285	so he might bury his companion and give him the funeral gifts.
286	But when he too, going over the wine-dark sea
287	in the hollow ships, the steep mountain of Malea
288	reached, running — then far-voiced Zeus planned a hateful road
289	and poured out the breath of shrill winds,
290	and the waves swelled huge, equal to mountains.
291	There, splitting the fleet, he drove some ships to Crete,
292	where the Cydonians lived around the streams of the Iardanus.
293	There is a sheer steep rock into the sea
294	at the edge of Gortyn in the mist-dark sea.
295	There the South Wind pushes a great wave against the left headland,
296	toward Phaestus, and a small stone keeps off the great wave.
297	The ships came there, and the men barely escaped destruction,
298	but the waves smashed the ships against the reefs.
299	But the five dark-prowed ships
300	the wind and the water carried and drove to Egypt.
301	So he, gathering much livelihood and gold there,
302	wandered with his ships among men of other speech.
303	Meanwhile Aegisthus planned these painful things at home.
304	He had killed Atreus's son, and the people were subdued under him.
305	For seven years he ruled Mycenae rich in gold,
306	but in the eighth a bad thing came to him, godlike Orestes
307	back from Athens, and he killed the killer of his father,
308	Aegisthus of the tricky counsel, who killed his famous father.
309	When he had killed him, he held a funeral feast for the Argives
310	for his hateful mother and for cowardly Aegisthus.
311	And that same day Menelaus good at the war-cry came to him
312	bringing many goods, as much as his ships lifted as a load.
313	And you, dear friend, do not wander long far from your home,
314	leaving your goods and men in your house
315	so overbearing, or they may eat up everything,
316	dividing the goods, and you will have come on an empty road.
317	But I urge and bid you go to Menelaus.
318	For that man has newly come from elsewhere,
319	from those people from whom you would not hope in your heart
320	to come back, once the storm-winds have first driven a man off course
321	into so great an open sea, from which not even birds
322	go in the same year, since it is great and terrible.
323	But go now with your ship and your companions.
324	Or if you want to go by land, there is a chariot and horses for you,
325	and my sons, who will be your escorts
326	to bright Sparta, where fair-haired Menelaus is.
327	You yourself must entreat him, so he will tell what does not miss.
328	He will not tell a lie; for he is very clear-headed.
> (narrator)
329	So he spoke, and the sun went down and dusk came on.
330	And the goddess spoke among them, owl-eyed Athena:
> Athena
331	Old man, you have told these things truly, in due measure.
332	But come, cut the tongues, and mix the wine,
333	so that we may pour to Poseidon and the other immortals
334	and then take thought of bed. For it is the hour for that.
335	Already the light has gone under the gloom, and it is not seemly
336	to sit long at a feast of the gods, but to go back.
> (narrator)
337	So the daughter of Zeus spoke, and they listened to her as she spoke.
338	Heralds poured water over their hands,
339	and young men filled the mixing-bowls to the brim with drink,
340	and they served it to all, beginning the rite in the cups.
341	They threw the tongues in the fire, and standing they poured libations.
342	But when they had poured and drunk as much as the heart wanted,
343	then Athena and godlike Telemachus
344	both were eager to go to the hollow ship to return.
345	But Nestor held them back, taking hold of them with words:
> Nestor
346	May Zeus and the other immortal gods ward that off,
347	that you should go from my house to your swift ship
348	as if from some man altogether without clothes and poor,
349	who has neither cloaks nor many blankets in his house,
350	neither for himself to sleep softly nor for his guests.
351	But I have cloaks and beautiful blankets.
352	The dear son of this man Odysseus shall not, I tell you,
353	lie down on the deck-planks of a ship, as long as I
354	live, and after that my sons are left in the halls
355	to host guests, whoever comes to my house.
> (narrator)
356	Then the goddess spoke to him, owl-eyed Athena:
> Athena
357	You have spoken this well, dear old man. It is fitting
358	that Telemachus obey you, since it is much better so.
359	But this one will now follow along with you, so he may sleep
360	in your halls. I will go to the black ship
361	to hearten the companions and tell them each thing.
362	For I claim to be the only older man among them.
363	The others who follow in friendship are younger men,
364	all of the same age as great-hearted Telemachus.
365	There I would lie down beside the hollow black ship
366	for now. But at dawn I will go among the great-hearted Cauconians,
367	where a debt is owed to me, and it is not new
368	nor small. You, since this man has come to your house,
369	send him on with a chariot and with your son. Give him horses,
370	the ones lightest at running and best in strength.
> (narrator)
371	So speaking, owl-eyed Athena went away,
372	likening herself to a sea-eagle. Wonder took all who saw it.
373	The old man marveled, when he saw it with his eyes.
374	He took Telemachus by the hand, and spoke a word and called him by name:
> Nestor
375	Dear friend, I did not think you would be a coward and weak,
376	if the gods go with you as escorts when you are so young.
377	For this is no other of those who have homes on Olympus,
378	but the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia,
379	who also honored your noble father among the Argives.
380	Be gracious, queen, and give me noble glory,
381	to me and my children and my respected wife.
382	And I in turn will sacrifice to you a yearling broad-browed heifer,
383	unbroken, that no man has yet led under the yoke.
384	I will sacrifice her to you, pouring gold around her horns.
> (narrator)
385	So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him.
386	Gerenian horseman Nestor led them,
387	his sons and sons-in-law, to his own beautiful house.
388	But when they reached the far-famed house of that lord,
389	they sat down in order on chairs and thrones.
390	The old man mixed a bowl for them when they had come
391	of sweet-drinking wine, which in the eleventh year
392	the housekeeper opened, loosing the lid-cloth from it.
393	From this the old man mixed a bowl, and he prayed many things to Athena
394	as he poured, the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus.
395	But when they had poured and drunk as much as the heart wanted,
396	they went each to his home to lie down,
397	but Gerenian horseman Nestor put to bed there
398	Telemachus, the dear son of godlike Odysseus,
399	on a corded bed under the loud-echoing portico,
400	and beside him Pisistratus of the good ashen spear, leader of men,
401	who was still an unmarried young man of his children in the halls.
402	He himself slept in the inner part of the high house,
403	and the lady his wife made ready the bed and the bedding.
404	When early-born rose-fingered Dawn appeared,
405	Gerenian horseman Nestor rose from his bed,
406	and coming out he sat down on the polished stones,
407	which were in front of his high doors,
408	white, gleaming with oil. On these before
409	Neleus used to sit, a counsellor equal to the gods.
410	But he had already been overcome by death and gone to Hades,
411	and now Nestor sat there, the Gerenian, watcher of the Achaeans,
412	holding a scepter. And his sons gathered around him in a body,
413	coming from their rooms: Echephron and Stratius
414	and Perseus and Aretus and godlike Thrasymedes.
415	And then sixth the hero Pisistratus came to them,
416	and they brought godlike Telemachus and seated him beside them.
417	Then Gerenian horseman Nestor began the talk among them:
> Nestor
418	Quickly, dear children, fulfill my wish,
419	so that first of all I may win over Athena among the gods,
420	who came to me in clear sight at the god's rich feast.
421	But come, let one go to the plain for a cow, so that as quickly as possible
422	it may come, and let the cowherd drive it.
423	And let one go to the black ship of great-hearted Telemachus
424	and bring all the companions, and leave only two.
425	And let one bid Laerces the goldsmith to come here,
426	so he may pour gold around the cow's horns.
427	The rest of you stay here together, and tell the women inside
428	through the far-famed house to prepare a feast,
429	and to bring seats and wood around, and bright water.
> (narrator)
430	So he spoke, and they all bustled. The cow came
431	from the plain, and from the swift well-balanced ship came
432	the companions of great-hearted Telemachus, and the bronze-smith came
433	holding his bronze tools in his hands, the workings of his craft,
434	anvil and hammer and well-made tongs,
435	with which he worked gold. And Athena came
436	to be present at the holy rites. The old horseman Nestor
437	gave the gold. The man then poured it around the cow's horns,
438	having worked it, so the goddess might be glad seeing the offering.
439	Stratius and godlike Echephron led the cow by the horns.
440	Aretus came from the room bringing them washing-water in a flowered basin,
441	and in the other hand he held the barley-grains
442	in a basket. And war-staunch Thrasymedes
443	stood by the cow holding a sharp axe in his hand, to cut it down.
444	Perseus held the blood-bowl. The old horseman Nestor
445	began the rite with the washing-water and the scattered barley, and he prayed many things to Athena,
446	making the first-offering, throwing the hairs of the head in the fire.
447	But when they had prayed and thrown the barley-grains forward,
