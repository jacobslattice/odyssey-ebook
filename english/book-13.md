# Odyssey Book 13

> (narrator)
1	So he spoke, and they all became hushed in silence,
2	held by the spell through the shadowy halls.
3	Then Alcinous answered him and spoke:
> Alcinous
4	Odysseus, since you have come to my bronze-floored house,
5	high-roofed, I do not think you will be driven back
6	and return again, even though you have suffered very many things.
7	To each man of you I say this as a charge,
8	all who in my halls always drink the sparkling wine
9	of the elders and listen to the singer.
10	Clothes already lie for the stranger in a well-polished chest
11	and gold worked in many patterns and all the other
12	gifts the Phaeacian counsellors brought here.
13	But come, let us give him a great tripod and a cauldron
14	each man. We will collect through the people
15	and pay ourselves back. It is hard for one man to give a free gift.
> (narrator)
16	So Alcinous spoke, and the word pleased them.
17	They went home to lie down, each to his house.
18	When early-born Dawn appeared, the rose-fingered one,
19	they hurried to the ship and carried the bronze that makes men strong.
20	The holy strength of Alcinous put it well,
21	going himself through the ship under the benches, so it would not
22	harm any of the companions when they were driving hard with the oars.
23	They went to the house of Alcinous and took care of the feast.
24	The holy strength of Alcinous sacrificed an ox for them
25	to Zeus of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules all.
26	Burning the thigh-bones they feasted a famous feast,
27	taking pleasure. Among them the godlike singer sang,
28	Demodocus, honored by the people. Odysseus
29	turned his head many times toward the shining sun,
30	wanting it to go down. He was eager to go home.
31	As when a man longs for supper, for whom all day
32	two wine-faced oxen have dragged a fitted plow through fallow land.
33	The light of the sun goes down welcome to him
34	as he goes toward supper, and his knees are harmed as he goes.
35	So the light of the sun went down welcome to Odysseus.
36	At once he spoke among the oar-loving Phaeacians,
37	and he made the word especially clear to Alcinous:
> Odysseus
38	Lord Alcinous, distinguished among all the people,
39	send me, when you have poured a drink-offering, unharmed, and you yourselves be glad.
40	What my own heart wanted has already been finished,
41	an escort and dear gifts. May the gods of the sky
42	make them blessed. May I find a faultless wife at home
43	when I come, with my own people unhurt.
44	You stay here and make your wedded wives
45	and your children glad. May the gods grant you excellence
46	of every kind, and may there be no evil among the people.
> (narrator)
47	So he spoke, and they all approved and bid
48	send the stranger, since he had spoken in due measure.
49	Then the strength of Alcinous spoke to the herald:
> Alcinous
50	Pontonous, mix a bowl and serve the wine
51	to all through the hall, so that when we have prayed to father Zeus
52	we may send the stranger to his own fatherland.
> (narrator)
53	So he spoke, and Pontonous mixed honey-hearted wine
54	and served it to all, standing by. They poured
55	to the blessed gods who hold the wide sky,
56	from their seats right there. Godlike Odysseus stood up
57	and put a two-handled cup in Arete's hand,
58	and speaking he said winged words to her:
> Odysseus
59	Be glad, queen, all the way through, until old age
60	comes, and death, which come upon people.
61	I am going home. You take pleasure in this house
62	with your children and the people and king Alcinous.
> (narrator)
63	So speaking godlike Odysseus went over the threshold.
64	The strength of Alcinous sent a herald with him
65	to lead him to the fast ship and the shore of the sea.
66	Arete sent serving-women with him,
67	one holding a well-washed cloak and a tunic,
68	and she sent another to carry a close-built chest with them.
69	The other carried food and red wine.
70	When they had gone down to the ship and the sea,
71	the proud escorts at once took these things in the hollow ship
72	and put them down, all the drink and the food.
73	They spread a coverlet and a linen cloth for Odysseus
74	on the deck of the hollow ship, so he would sleep a sleep that does not wake,
75	in the stern. He himself went aboard and lay down
76	in silence. They sat at the benches, each
77	in order, and they loosed the cable from the pierced stone.
78	When they leaned back and threw the sea up with the blade,
79	sweet sleep fell on his eyelids,
80	a sleep that does not wake, the sweetest, most like death.
81	The ship, as when four-yoked stallion horses on a plain
82	all start together under the strokes of the whip
83	and lifting high they finish the road lightly,
84	so her stern lifted, and behind her a great dark
85	wave of the much-roaring sea boiled.
86	She ran very safely, firm. A hawk,
87	the falcon, lightest of winged things, could not have kept up.
88	So she running lightly cut the waves of the sea,
89	carrying a man who had counsels like the gods,
90	who before had suffered very many pains in his own heart
91	through the wars of men and piercing the painful waves,
92	then slept still, having forgotten how much he had suffered.
93	When the brightest star had risen, the one that most
94	comes announcing the light of early-born Dawn,
95	then the seagoing ship drew near the island.
96	There is a harbor of Phorcys, the old man of the sea,
97	in the land of Ithaca. Two jutting
98	broken headlands in it, bent toward the harbor,
99	keep off the great wave of hard-blowing winds
100	from outside. Inside the well-benched ships stay
101	without a bond, when they reach the measure of the mooring.
102	At the head of the harbor there is a long-leaved olive,
103	and near it a lovely misty cave,
104	holy to the nymphs who are called Naiads.
105	In it there are mixing-bowls and two-handled jars
106	of stone. Bees then store honey there.
107	In it there are very long stone looms, where the nymphs
108	weave sea-purple cloaks, a wonder to see.
109	In it there are waters always flowing. It has two doors.
110	The ones toward Boreas are the way down for people.
111	The ones toward Notus are more for gods. Men do not
112	go in that way. It is a road of the deathless.
113	They drove in there, knowing it before. Then the ship
114	ran up on the mainland as far as half her whole length,
115	hurrying. Such was the force of the rowers' hands.
116	They went out of the well-benched ship onto the mainland
117	and first lifted Odysseus from the hollow ship
118	with the linen and the shining coverlet,
119	and they set him down on the sand, overcome by sleep.
120	They lifted out the goods the proud Phaeacians
121	had given him as he went home, through great-hearted Athena.
122	They set them in a pile beside the trunk of the olive
123	off the road, so no passer of people
124	would come and harm them before Odysseus woke.
125	They themselves went back home. The earth-shaker
126	did not forget the threats he had first
127	threatened against godlike Odysseus, and he asked Zeus's will:
> Poseidon
128	Father Zeus, I will no longer be honored among the deathless gods
129	when mortals do not honor me at all,
130	the Phaeacians, who are of my own birth.
131	I said Odysseus would come home having suffered many evils.
132	I never took his homecoming from him
133	altogether, since you first promised and nodded.
134	They have brought him sleeping in the fast ship over the sea
135	and set him in Ithaca, and they have given him endless gifts,
136	bronze and gold in plenty and woven clothes,
137	many, as many as Odysseus would never have taken from Troy,
138	if he had come unharmed, drawing his share from the spoil.
> (narrator)
139	Cloud-gatherer Zeus answered him:
> Zeus
140	Oh, wide-powered earth-shaker, what a word you have spoken.
141	The gods do not dishonor you at all. It would be hard
142	to send the oldest and the best into dishonor.
143	If some man, yielding to force and strength,
144	does not honor you, the payment is always yours afterward too.
145	Do as you want, and as is dear to your heart.
> (narrator)
146	Then Poseidon the earth-shaker answered him:
> Poseidon
147	I would do it at once, dark-clouded one, as you say.
148	But I always regard your heart and I keep clear of it.
149	Now I want the Phaeacians' very beautiful ship,
150	as she comes back from the escort, on the misty sea,
151	to shatter, so they already stop and leave off the escort
152	of people, and to throw a great mountain around their city.
> (narrator)
153	Cloud-gatherer Zeus answered him:
> Zeus
154	Dear one, this is how it seems best in my heart:
155	when all the people are watching her driven
156	from the city, set her as a stone near the land
157	like the fast ship, so all people may wonder,
158	and throw a great mountain around their city.
> (narrator)
159	When Poseidon the earth-shaker heard that,
160	he went to Scheria, where the Phaeacians are born.
161	He waited there. The seagoing ship came very near,
162	driven lightly. The earth-shaker came near her
163	and made her a stone and rooted her underneath,
164	striking with the flat of his hand. He went away.
165	They spoke winged words to one another,
166	the long-oared Phaeacians, men famous for ships.
167	One would say, looking at another near him:
> a Phaeacian
168	Oh, who has bound the fast ship on the sea
169	as she was being driven home? She was already showing whole.
> (narrator)
170	So one would say. They did not know how it had been done.
171	Alcinous spoke among them and said:
> Alcinous
172	Oh, the old oracles of my father have very much reached me,
173	who said Poseidon was angry
174	at us, because we are unharming escorts of all.
175	He said he would someday shatter a very beautiful ship of the Phaeacian men
176	as she came back from the escort, on the misty sea,
177	and throw a great mountain around our city.
178	So the old man spoke. Now all of it is being finished.
179	But come, as I say, let us all obey.
180	Stop the escort of mortals, whenever someone comes
181	to our city. We will sacrifice to Poseidon
182	twelve chosen bulls, if he may pity us
183	and not throw a very long mountain around our city.
> (narrator)
184	So he spoke, and they were afraid, and they made the bulls ready.
185	So they were praying to lord Poseidon,
186	the leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacian people,
187	standing around the altar. Godlike Odysseus woke
188	sleeping in his fatherland, and he did not know it,
189	having been away a long time. A god had poured mist around him,
190	Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, so she might make
191	him himself unknown and tell him each thing,
192	so his wife and the townsmen and his own people would not know him
193	before he paid back all the suitors' transgression.
194	For that reason everything appeared in another form to the lord,
195	the unbroken paths and the harbors that hold all ships
196	and the sheer rocks and the flourishing trees.
197	He stood up with a start and looked at his fatherland.
198	Then he groaned and struck his thighs
199	with the flats of his hands, and lamenting he spoke a word:
> Odysseus
200	Oh I, to whose land of mortals have I come now?
201	Are they violent and wild and not just,
202	or welcoming to strangers, and is their mind god-fearing?
203	Where am I to take these many goods? Where am I myself
204	to wander? I wish I had stayed among the Phaeacians
205	right there. I would have reached some other over-powerful king
206	who would have loved me and sent me home.
207	Now I do not know where to put them, and I will not
208	leave them here, or they may become booty for others.
209	Oh, the leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians were not
210	all thoughtful and just.
211	They led me to another land. They said they would
212	lead me to clear Ithaca, and they did not finish it.
213	May Zeus of suppliants pay them, who looks on other
214	people too and punishes whoever misses the mark.
215	But come, I will count the goods and see them,
216	in case they have gone taking something on the hollow ship.
> (narrator)
217	So speaking he counted the very beautiful tripods and the cauldrons
218	and the gold and the fine woven clothes.
219	He did not miss any of them. He lamented his fatherland
220	crawling along the shore of the much-roaring sea,
221	lamenting much. Athena came near him,
222	likened in body to a young man, a herder of sheep,
223	very delicate, such as the sons of kings are,
224	wearing a well-made cloak folded double around her shoulders.
225	She had sandals under her shining feet, and a spear in her hands.
226	Odysseus was glad when he saw her and came opposite her,
227	and speaking he said winged words to her:
> Odysseus
228	Friend, since I meet you first in this place,
229	be glad, and do not meet me with any bad mind,
230	but save these things, and save me. I pray to you
231	as to a god, and I come to your own knees.
232	Tell me this truly, so I may know it well.
233	What land, what people, what men have been born here?
234	Is it some clear island, or some shore
235	lying on the sea of the rich-soiled mainland?
> (narrator)
236	The owl-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him:
> Athena
237	You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away,
238	if you ask about this land. It is not so
239	nameless as that. Very many know it,
240	both those who live toward the dawn and the sun
241	and those behind toward the misty dark.
242	It is rough and not for driving horses,
243	and not very poor, but it is not made wide.
244	Grain grows on it beyond measure, and wine
245	is made. Rain always holds it, and flourishing dew.
246	It is good for goats and good for cattle. There is wood
247	of every kind, and watering-places that do not fail are on it.
248	So, stranger, the name of Ithaca has reached even to Troy,
249	which they say is far from the land of Achaea.
> (narrator)
250	So she spoke, and much-enduring godlike Odysseus was glad,
251	rejoicing in his own fatherland, as Pallas Athena told him,
252	daughter of Zeus who holds the aegis.
253	Speaking he said winged words to her:
254	He did not tell the truth. He took the story back again,
255	always turning a mind of many gains in his chest:
> Odysseus
256	I used to hear of Ithaca even in wide Crete,
257	far over the sea. Now I myself have come
258	with these goods. I left as many still to my children.
259	I am in flight, since I killed the dear son of Idomeneus,
260	Orsilochus swift of foot, who in wide Crete
261	beat the grain-eating men with his fast feet,
262	because he wanted to strip me of all the spoil
263	from Troy, for which I suffered pains in my heart,
264	through the wars of men and piercing the painful waves,
265	because I would not serve his father, doing him a favor,
266	in the land of the Trojans, but I led other companions.
267	I hit him as he came down from the country with a bronze-tipped spear,
268	lying in wait near the road with a companion.
269	A very dark night held the sky, and no one
270	of people marked us. I took his heart from him unseen.
271	When I had killed him with the sharp bronze,
272	I went at once to the ship and begged the proud Phoenicians,
273	and I gave them spoil that would please their hearts.
274	I told them to put me ashore at Pylos and set me down
275	or at holy Elis, where the Epeians have power.
276	The force of the wind pushed them off from there
277	though they were very unwilling, and they did not want to deceive me.
278	Driven from there we came here at night.
279	With effort we rowed into the harbor, and none of us
280	had a thought of supper, though we very much wanted to take it,
281	but we just went off and lay down, all of us, from the ship.
282	There sweet sleep came on me, worn out,
283	and they took my goods from the hollow ship
284	and set them where I myself was lying on the sands.
285	They went aboard and left for well-settled Sidon.
286	I was left behind, grieving in my heart.
> (narrator)
287	So he spoke, and the owl-eyed goddess Athena smiled
288	and stroked him with her hand. She was likened in body to a woman
289	beautiful and tall and knowing shining works.
290	Speaking she said winged words to him:
> Athena
291	He would be a gainer and a thief who could get past you
292	in all tricks, even if a god should meet you.
293	Hard man, of many-colored devices, insatiate of tricks,
294	you were not going, even in your own land, to leave off deceits
295	and stolen stories, which are dear to you from the ground up.
296	But come, let us no longer say these things, since we both know
297	gains. You are far the best of all mortals
298	in counsel and in stories, and I among all the gods
299	am famed for cunning and for gains. You did not even know
300	Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, who always
301	stand by you in all your toils and guard you,
302	and I made you dear to all the Phaeacians.
303	Now I have come here again, so I may weave a cunning with you
304	and hide the goods the proud Phaeacians
305	gave you as you went home, by my counsel and mind,
306	and tell you how many cares it is your lot to bear
307	in the built house. You must endure even by necessity,
308	and tell it to no one, neither of men nor of women,
309	all of them, that you have come wandering, but in silence
310	suffer many pains, taking in the violence of men.
> (narrator)
311	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
312	It is hard, goddess, for a mortal who meets you to know you,
313	even one who knows very well. You make yourself like everything.
314	I know this well, that you were gentle to me before,
315	while we sons of the Achaeans were fighting at Troy.
316	When we had sacked the steep city of Priam
317	and went in the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans,
318	I did not see you then, daughter of Zeus, and I did not mark you
319	going aboard my ship, so you might keep some pain off me.
320	I always wandered with a heart cut apart in my mind
321	until the gods loosed me from the badness.
322	Not before, in the rich land of the Phaeacian men,
323	did you put heart in me with words and lead me to the city yourself.
324	Now I beg you by your father — I do not think
325	I have come to clear Ithaca, but I am turning
326	in some other land. I think you are mocking
327	when you say these things, so you may deceive my mind —
328	tell me if I truly come to my own dear fatherland.
> (narrator)
329	Then the owl-eyed goddess Athena answered him:
> Athena
330	Always you have such a thought in your chest.
331	That is why I cannot leave you, luckless as you are,
332	because you are ready with words and quick-witted and careful.
333	Another man, coming home from wandering, would gladly
334	hurry to see his children and his wife in the halls.
335	It is not yet dear to you to learn or to hear,
336	before you have tried your wife still more, who sits
337	just so in the halls, and the miserable nights and days
338	always waste her as she sheds tears.
339	I never disbelieved it. I knew in my heart
340	that you would come home having lost all your companions.
341	I was not willing to fight Poseidon
342	my father's brother, who put anger in his heart
343	in his rage that you blinded his dear son.
344	But come, I will show you the seat of Ithaca, so you may believe.
345	This is the harbor of Phorcys, the old man of the sea.
346	This at the head of the harbor is the long-leaved olive.
347	Near it is the lovely misty cave,
348	holy to the nymphs who are called Naiads.
349	This is the roofed cave where you used to make
350	many perfect hecatombs to the nymphs.
351	This is Mount Neriton, clothed in wood.
> (narrator)
352	So speaking the goddess scattered the mist, and the land appeared.
353	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus was glad,
354	rejoicing in his land, and he kissed the grain-giving plowland.
355	At once he prayed to the nymphs, holding up his hands:
> Odysseus
356	Naiad nymphs, daughters of Zeus, I never
357	thought I would see you. Now with gentle vows
358	be glad. We will also give gifts, as before,
359	if the daughter of Zeus, the driver of the spoil, is willing
360	to let me live myself and to raise my own son.
> (narrator)
361	The owl-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him:
> Athena
362	Take heart. Do not let these things be a care in your mind.
363	Let us put the goods in the inner part of the unearthly cave
364	right now, so they may stay safe for you.
365	We ourselves must plan how it will be far the best.
> (narrator)
366	So speaking the goddess went into the misty cave
367	searching the hiding-places through the cave. Odysseus
368	brought everything closer, the gold and the untiring bronze
369	and the well-made clothes the Phaeacians had given him.
370	She put them well, and she set a stone at the doors,
371	Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus who holds the aegis.
372	The two sat down beside the holy trunk of the olive
373	and planned destruction for the overbearing suitors.
374	The owl-eyed goddess Athena began the words:
> Athena
375	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
376	plan how you will put your hands on the shameless suitors,
377	who now for three years have been lords through the hall,
378	courting your godlike wife and giving bride-gifts.
379	She, always lamenting your homecoming in her heart,
380	gives hope to all and promises each man,
381	sending messages, but her mind intends other things.
> (narrator)
382	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
383	Oh, I was very much about to perish by the bad fate
384	of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, in the halls,
385	if you, goddess, had not told me each thing in due measure.
386	But come, weave a cunning, how I may pay them back.
387	Stand beside me yourself, putting in much-daring force,
388	as when we loosed the shining veil of Troy.
389	If you would stand by me so eager, owl-eyed one,
390	I would fight even three hundred men
391	with you, lady goddess, when you help me willingly.
> (narrator)
392	Then the owl-eyed goddess Athena answered him:
> Athena
393	I will very much be beside you, and you will not escape my notice
394	when we are busy at these things. I think someone
395	will spatter the endless floor with blood and brains
396	of the suitor men who eat up your livelihood.
397	But come, I will make you unknown to all mortals.
398	I will wither the handsome skin on your bent limbs,
399	and I will destroy the yellow hair from your head, and I will put around you a rag
400	that a person seeing it on a man would hate,
401	and I will dim your eyes that were very beautiful before,
402	so you will appear unseemly to all the suitors
403	and to your wife and the child you left in the halls.
404	You yourself go first to the swineherd,
405	who is the watcher of your pigs, and he has gentle thoughts toward you alike,
406	and he loves your child and careful Penelope.
407	You will find him sitting by the pigs. They feed
408	by the rock of Corax and at the spring Arethusa,
409	eating acorns that please the heart and drinking black water,
410	which grow a flourishing fat on the pigs.
411	Stay there and ask everything sitting by him,
412	until I go to Sparta of the beautiful women
413	to call Telemachus, your own son, Odysseus,
414	who went to wide Lacedaemon to Menelaus
415	to ask after your fame, if you still were somewhere.
> (narrator)
416	Odysseus of many devices answered her:
> Odysseus
417	Why then did you not tell him, you who know everything in your mind?
418	Was it so he too might wander and suffer pains
419	on the barren sea, and others eat his livelihood?
> (narrator)
420	Then the owl-eyed goddess Athena answered him:
> Athena
421	Do not let that one be too much on your heart.
422	I myself was escorting him, so he might win good fame
423	going there. He has no toil. At his ease
424	he sits in the house of the son of Atreus, and endless things lie beside him.
425	Young men are lying in wait for him with a black ship,
426	wanting to kill him before he reaches his fatherland.
427	I do not think that. First the earth will hold someone
428	of the suitor men who eat up your livelihood.
> (narrator)
429	So speaking Athena touched him with a staff.
430	She withered the handsome skin on your bent limbs
431	and destroyed the yellow hair from your head, and around him she put the skin
432	of an old man, old, on all his limbs.
433	She dimmed his eyes that were very beautiful before.
434	She threw around him another bad rag and a tunic,
435	torn, dirty, stained with bad smoke.
436	She put around him a great skin of a swift deer,
437	stripped. She gave him a staff and an unseemly bag,
438	torn close, and there was a twisted strap on it.
439	When the two had planned like this they parted. She then
440	went to holy Lacedaemon after the child of Odysseus.
