# Odyssey Book 15

> (narrator)
1	Pallas Athena went to wide Lacedaemon
2	to remind the shining son of great-hearted Odysseus
3	of his homecoming and to urge him to go.
4	She found Telemachus and the shining son of Nestor
5	sleeping in the porch of famous Menelaus.
6	The son of Nestor was overcome by a soft sleep.
7	Sweet sleep did not hold Telemachus. In his heart
8	through the deathless night cares for his father kept him awake.
9	Standing near him the owl-eyed goddess Athena spoke:
> Athena
10	Telemachus, it is no longer good that you wander far from your house,
11	leaving your possessions and men in your halls
12	so overbearing. They may eat all
13	the possessions, dividing them, and you may go a useless road.
14	Urge Menelaus, good at the war-cry, as quickly as you can
15	to send you, so you may still find your faultless mother at home.
16	Already her father and her brothers bid her
17	marry Eurymachus. He outdoes all
18	the suitors in gifts and has increased the bride-gifts.
19	Let her not carry some possession from the house against your will.
20	You know what kind of heart is in a woman's chest.
21	She wants to make grow the house of the man who marries her,
22	and she no longer remembers her earlier children and her own dear
23	wedded husband when he has died, and she does not ask.
24	You go yourself and hand each thing over
25	to whichever of the serving-women seems best to you,
26	until the gods show you a proud wife.
27	I will tell you another word, and you put it in your heart.
28	The best of the suitors are lying in wait for you on purpose
29	in the strait of Ithaca and rugged Samos,
30	wanting to kill you before you reach your fatherland.
31	I do not think that. First the earth will hold someone
32	of the suitor men who eat up your livelihood.
33	Keep the well-made ship away from the islands,
34	and sail by night as well. One of the deathless
35	who guards you and protects you will send a wind behind you.
36	When you reach the first shore of Ithaca,
37	send the ship to the city and all the companions,
38	and you yourself go first to the swineherd,
39	who is the watcher of your pigs, and he has gentle thoughts toward you alike.
40	Sleep the night there. Urge him to go into the city
41	to tell the message to careful Penelope,
42	that you are safe and have come from Pylos.
> (narrator)
43	So speaking she went away toward long Olympus.
44	He woke the son of Nestor from his sweet sleep,
45	moving him with his heel, and he spoke a word to him:
> Telemachus
46	Wake, Peisistratus son of Nestor. Bring the single-hoofed horses
47	and yoke them under the chariot, so we may finish the road.
> (narrator)
48	Peisistratus son of Nestor spoke opposite him:
> Peisistratus
49	Telemachus, it is not possible, though we are eager for the road,
50	to drive through the dark night. Dawn will be soon.
51	Wait until the hero son of Atreus, Menelaus famous for the spear,
52	brings gifts and sets them on the chariot
53	and sends us off, speaking us on with gentle words.
54	A stranger remembers all his days
55	the host man who has given friendship.
> (narrator)
56	So he spoke, and at once gold-throned Dawn came.
57	Menelaus, good at the war-cry, came near them,
58	rising from his bed, from beside Helen of the beautiful hair.
59	When the own son of Odysseus marked him,
60	he quickly put a shining tunic around his skin
61	and threw a great cloak around his strong shoulders,
62	the hero, and he went out the door, and standing beside him
63	Telemachus, own son of godlike Odysseus, spoke:
> Telemachus
64	Menelaus son of Atreus, Zeus-nourished, leader of the people,
65	send me now to my own dear fatherland.
66	My heart already longs to reach home.
> (narrator)
67	Then Menelaus, good at the war-cry, answered him:
> Menelaus
68	Telemachus, I will not hold you here a long time,
69	you who want your homecoming. I am angry at another
70	host man too, who loves beyond measure
71	and hates beyond measure. Due things are all better.
72	It is an equal evil, the man who urges a stranger
73	who does not want to go, and the man who holds back one who is hurrying.
74	One should love a stranger who is here, and send one who wants to go.
75	But wait until I bring gifts and set them on the chariot,
76	fine ones, and you may see them with your eyes, and I will tell the women
77	to make a supper in the halls from what is inside in plenty.
78	Both honor and brightness and a benefit:
79	to go, having dined, over the much boundless earth.
80	If you want to turn through Hellas and mid-Argos,
81	so I myself may follow you, I will yoke horses under for you
82	and I will lead you through the towns of people. No one will
83	send us off empty. He will give something at least to carry,
84	either some well-bronzed tripod or a cauldron,
85	or two mules or a gold cup.
> (narrator)
86	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
87	Menelaus son of Atreus, Zeus-nourished, leader of the people,
88	I want to go to our own now. I did not leave behind
89	anyone as a watcher going after my possessions.
90	I must not die myself seeking my godlike father,
91	or some fine stored thing be lost from my halls.
> (narrator)
92	When Menelaus, good at the war-cry, heard that,
93	he at once told his wife and the serving-women
94	to make a supper in the halls from what was inside in plenty.
95	Eteoneus, son of Boethous, came near him,
96	rising from his bed, since he did not live far from him.
97	Menelaus, good at the war-cry, told him to kindle a fire
98	and to roast meat. He did not disobey when he heard.
99	Menelaus himself went down to the fragrant store-room,
100	not alone. Helen went with him, and Megapenthes.
101	When they reached the place where the stored things lay,
102	the son of Atreus then took a two-handled cup,
103	and he told his son Megapenthes to carry a mixing-bowl
104	of silver. Helen stood by the chests,
105	where her robes of every pattern were, which she herself had worked.
106	Helen, bright among women, lifted one of them and carried it,
107	the one that was most beautiful in its patterns and the greatest.
108	It shone like a star. It lay lowest of the others.
109	They went on through the house until they reached
110	Telemachus. Fair-haired Menelaus spoke to him:
> Menelaus
111	Telemachus, may Zeus, the loud-thundering husband of Hera,
112	finish your homecoming as you intend it in your mind.
113	Of the gifts, as many stored things as lie in my house,
114	I will give the one that is most beautiful and most honored.
115	I will give you a worked mixing-bowl. It is silver
116	all through, and the rims are finished with gold,
117	a work of Hephaestus. The hero Phaedimus gave it,
118	king of the Sidonians, when his house covered me
119	as I came there on my way home. I want to give you this.
> (narrator)
120	So speaking the hero son of Atreus put the two-handled cup in his hand.
121	Strong Megapenthes set the shining mixing-bowl
122	of silver there in front of him, carrying it.
123	Helen of the beautiful cheeks stood by
124	holding a robe in her hands, and she spoke a word and called him by name:
> Helen
125	I too give you this gift, dear child,
126	a reminder of the hands of Helen, for the time of a much-loved marriage,
127	for your wife to wear. Until then let it lie
128	in the hall beside your own mother. May you reach
129	your well-built house and your fatherland glad.
> (narrator)
130	So speaking she put it in his hands, and he took it glad.
131	The hero Peisistratus received them and set them in the basket
132	and wondered at them all in his own heart.
133	Fair-haired Menelaus led them to the house.
134	Then they sat down on couches and thrones.
135	A serving-woman brought water for the hands and poured it from a pitcher,
136	a beautiful gold one, over a silver basin,
137	to wash. She drew a polished table beside them.
138	The respected housekeeper brought and set out bread,
139	putting many dishes on it, giving freely of what was there.
140	The son of Boethous carved the meat and distributed shares.
141	The son of famous Menelaus poured the wine.
142	They reached out their hands to the food lying ready.
143	When they had put away the desire for drink and food,
144	then Telemachus and the shining son of Nestor
145	yoked the horses and went up on the patterned chariot
146	and drove out from the gateway and the echoing porch.
147	Fair-haired Menelaus, son of Atreus, went after them
148	holding honey-hearted wine in his right hand
149	in a gold cup, so they might pour a drink-offering and go.
150	He stood in front of the horses, and greeting them he spoke:
> Menelaus
151	Be glad, you two young men, and tell Nestor, shepherd of the people:
152	he was gentle to me like a father,
153	while we sons of the Achaeans were fighting at Troy.
> (narrator)
154	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
155	We will tell him all these things very much, Zeus-nourished, as you say,
156	when we come. I wish that I,
157	coming home to Ithaca, finding Odysseus in the house,
158	could say how I come having met all friendship from you
159	and I bring many fine stored things.
> (narrator)
160	As he said that a bird flew on the right,
161	an eagle carrying a white goose, huge, in its talons,
162	a tame one from the yard. Men and women
163	followed shouting. Coming near them it
164	darted on the right in front of the horses. Seeing it they
165	were glad, and the heart in all their minds was warmed.
166	Peisistratus son of Nestor began the words:
> Peisistratus
167	Mark it, Menelaus, Zeus-nourished, leader of the people,
168	whether a god showed this sign for us two or for you yourself.
> (narrator)
169	So he spoke, and Menelaus dear to Ares turned it over
170	how he might think and answer in due measure.
171	Helen of the long robe spoke first and said a word:
> Helen
172	Hear me. I will prophesy, as the deathless
173	put it in my heart and as I think it will be finished.
174	As this eagle snatched the goose that was being raised in the house
175	coming from the mountain, where its birth and its brood are,
176	so Odysseus, having suffered many evils and wandered much,
177	will come home and pay. Or he is already
178	at home, and he is planting evil for all the suitors.
> (narrator)
179	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite her:
> Telemachus
180	May Zeus, the loud-thundering husband of Hera, make it so now.
181	Then even there I would pray to you as to a god.
> (narrator)
182	He threw the whip on the horses. They very quickly
183	darted toward the plain through the city, eager.
184	All day they shook the yoke, holding it on both sides.
185	The sun went down and all the ways grew dark.
186	They reached Pherae, to the house of Diocles,
187	son of Ortilochus, whom Alpheus fathered as a child.
188	They slept the night there, and he set guest-gifts beside them.
189	When early-born Dawn appeared, the rose-fingered one,
190	they yoked the horses and went up on the patterned chariot
191	and drove out from the gateway and the echoing porch.
192	He whipped them to drive, and the two flew, not unwilling.
193	Soon then they reached the steep citadel of Pylos.
194	Then Telemachus spoke to the son of Nestor:
> Telemachus
195	Son of Nestor, how could you promise me and finish
196	my word? We claim to be guests all the way through
197	from our fathers' friendship, and we are of the same age.
198	This road will put us still more into like-mindedness.
199	Do not lead me past the ship, Zeus-nourished. Leave me here,
200	or the old man may hold me in his house against my will
201	wanting to love me. I have need to reach home sooner.
> (narrator)
202	So he spoke, and the son of Nestor considered in his own heart
203	how he might promise and finish it in due measure.
204	This seemed more profitable to him as he thought:
205	he turned the horses toward the fast ship and the shore of the sea,
206	and he took out the beautiful gifts in the stern of the ship,
207	clothes and gold, which Menelaus had given him.
208	Urging him he said winged words:
> Peisistratus
209	Go aboard now in a hurry and tell all the companions,
210	before I reach home and report to the old man.
211	I know this well in my mind and in my heart.
212	Such is that man's overbearing heart, he will not let you go,
213	but he will come here himself calling you, and I do not think
214	he will go back empty. He will be very angry anyway.
> (narrator)
215	So speaking he drove the fair-maned horses
216	back to the town of the Pylians, and he reached the house quickly.
217	Telemachus urged the companions and told them:
> Telemachus
218	Arrange the tackle, companions, in the black ship,
219	and let us go aboard ourselves, so we may finish the road.
> (narrator)
220	So he spoke, and they heard him very much and obeyed.
221	They went aboard at once and sat at the benches.
222	He was busy at these things and praying, and he sacrificed to Athena
223	beside the stern of the ship. A man came near him
224	from far away, fleeing from Argos, having killed a man,
225	a seer. By birth he was a descendant of Melampus,
226	who once lived in Pylos, mother of flocks,
227	rich, living in a house far beyond the other Pylians.
228	Then he came to the people of others, fleeing his fatherland
229	and great-hearted Neleus, most proud of living men,
230	who held many of his goods by force through a year
231	that brings fulfillment. Meanwhile in the halls of Phylacus
232	he was bound in a hard bond, suffering hard pains
233	for the daughter of Neleus and a heavy blindness,
234	which the goddess Erinys, hard to approach, put in his mind.
235	But he escaped death and drove the loud-bellowing cattle
236	to Pylos from Phylace and paid back the unseemly work
237	to godlike Neleus, and he brought a wife for his brother
238	to the house. He came to the people of others,
239	to horse-pasturing Argos. There it was his lot
240	to live, ruling many Argives.
241	There he married a wife and set a high-roofed house,
242	and he fathered Antiphates and Mantius, two strong sons.
243	Antiphates fathered great-hearted Oicles,
244	and Oicles fathered Amphiaraus, who stirs the people,
245	whom Zeus who holds the aegis and Apollo loved around the heart
246	with every kind of friendship. He did not reach the threshold of old age,
247	but he died at Thebes because of a woman's gifts.
248	His sons were Alcmaon and Amphilochus.
249	Mantius in turn fathered Polypheides and Cleitus.
250	Gold-throned Dawn snatched Cleitus
251	because of his beauty, so he would be among the deathless.
252	Apollo made high-hearted Polypheides a seer
253	far the best of mortals, when Amphiaraus died.
254	He settled in Hyperesia, angry at his father.
255	Living there he prophesied to all mortals.
256	His son came, and his name was Theoclymenus.
257	He then stood near Telemachus. He found him
258	pouring and praying beside the fast black ship,
259	and speaking he said winged words to him:
> Theoclymenus
260	Friend, since I find you sacrificing in this place,
261	I beg you by the offerings and the god, and then
262	by your own head and the companions who follow you,
263	tell me what I ask, things that do not miss, and do not hide it.
264	Who are you among men, and from where? Where is your city and your parents?
> (narrator)
265	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
266	I will tell you, stranger, very exactly.
267	I am of the race of Ithaca, and my father is Odysseus,
268	if he ever was. Now already he has died a grim death.
269	For that I have now taken companions and a black ship
270	and come to ask after a father gone a long time.
> (narrator)
271	Then godlike Theoclymenus spoke to him:
> Theoclymenus
272	So I too am away from my fatherland, having killed a man
273	of my own people. Many brothers and companions
274	through horse-pasturing Argos have great power among the Achaeans.
275	Avoiding death from them and black doom
276	I am in flight, since it is my lot to wander among people.
277	Put me on the ship, since I have fled to you as a suppliant,
278	or they may kill me. I think they are chasing me.
> (narrator)
279	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
280	I will not push you from the even ship against your will.
281	Follow. There you will be loved, with such as we have.
> (narrator)
282	So speaking he took the bronze spear from him
283	and laid it on the deck of the double-ended ship.
284	He himself went aboard the seagoing ship.
285	He sat in the stern, and beside himself
286	he seated Theoclymenus. They loosed the stern-cables.
287	Telemachus urged the companions and told them
288	to take hold of the tackle. They obeyed in a hurry.
289	They set the fir mast inside the hollow mast-box
290	lifting it, and they bound it with the forestays,
291	and they hauled the white sails with well-twisted oxhide ropes.
292	Owl-eyed Athena sent them a following wind,
293	rushing hard through the air, so that as quickly as possible
294	the ship running might finish the salt water of the sea.
295	They went past Crouni and Chalcis of the beautiful streams.
296	The sun went down and all the ways grew dark.
297	The ship set her course for Pheae, driven by the wind of Zeus,
298	and past holy Elis, where the Epeians have power.
299	From there he sent her on toward the fast islands,
300	turning over whether he would escape death or be taken.
301	Meanwhile in the hut Odysseus and the godlike swineherd
302	were at supper. The other men were at supper beside them.
303	When they had put away the desire for drink and food,
304	Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd,
305	whether he would still love him kindly and tell him to stay
306	right there in the steading, or urge him to the city:
> Odysseus
307	Hear me now, Eumaeus and all you other companions.
308	At dawn I long to go back toward the town
309	to beg, so I will not wear you and the companions out.
310	Advise me well and give me a good guide with me
311	who will lead me there. Through the city I myself by necessity
312	will wander, if someone holds out a cup and a bit of bread.
313	I would go to the house of godlike Odysseus
314	and tell a message to careful Penelope,
315	and I would mix with the overbearing suitors,
316	if they would give me supper, they who have countless good things.
317	I would soon do well among them whatever they wanted.
318	I will tell you, and you put it together and hear me.
319	By the will of Hermes the guide, who gives grace and fame
320	to the works of all people,
321	no other mortal would vie with me in service,
322	to pile a fire well and to split dry wood
323	and to carve and to roast and to pour wine,
324	the kind of things lesser men do for the good.
> (narrator)
325	You answered him, greatly troubled, Eumaeus the swineherd:
> Eumaeus
326	Oh stranger, why has this thought been in your mind?
327	You are altogether longing to die right there,
328	if you want to go down into the crowd of the suitors,
329	whose violence and force reach the iron sky.
330	Their serving-men are not such as you.
331	They are young, well clothed in cloaks and tunics,
332	always shining in their heads and handsome faces,
333	who serve them. The well-polished tables
334	are heavy with food and meat and wine.
335	Stay. No one is pained by you being here,
336	neither I nor any other of the companions I have.
337	When the own son of Odysseus comes,
338	that one will put a cloak and a tunic on you as clothes
339	and send you wherever your heart and spirit bid.
> (narrator)
340	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus answered him:
> Odysseus
341	I wish you would become so dear to father Zeus, Eumaeus,
342	as you are to me, because you stopped me from wandering and grim misery.
343	There is nothing worse for mortals than being driven.
344	Men have bad cares because of the cursed belly,
345	whomever wandering and pain and grief reach.
346	Now since you hold me back and tell me to wait for that man,
347	come, tell me about the mother of godlike Odysseus
348	and his father, whom he left going on the threshold of old age,
349	whether they still live under the rays of the sun,
350	or they are already dead and in the house of Hades.
> (narrator)
351	Then the swineherd, leader of men, spoke to him:
> Eumaeus
352	I will tell you, stranger, very exactly.
353	Laertes still lives, and he prays always to Zeus
354	that the heart leave his limbs in his halls.
355	He laments terribly for his child who is gone
356	and for his wedded wife of knowing mind, who most
357	pained him dying and put him in a raw old age.
358	She died of grief for her famous child,
359	a wretched death. May no one die so who is dear to me
360	living here and who does dear things.
361	As long as she was there, grieving anyway,
362	it was dear to me to inquire and ask,
363	because she herself raised me with Ctimene of the long robe,
364	her strong daughter, the youngest she bore of her children.
365	I was raised together with her, and she honored me a little less.
366	When we both reached much-loved youth,
367	they then gave her to Same and took countless things,
368	and that woman put a cloak and a tunic around me
369	as very fine clothes, and giving me sandals for my feet
370	she sent me to the country. She loved me still more in her heart.
371	Now I already lack those things. For me myself
372	the blessed gods make the work grow which I stay at.
373	From it I have eaten and drunk and given to those who should be respected.
374	From the lady there is nothing gentle to hear,
375	neither a word nor any work, since an evil has fallen on the house,
376	overbearing men. Slaves greatly need
377	to speak face to face with the lady and to learn each thing
378	and to eat and drink, and then also to carry something
379	to the country, the kind of things that always warm a slave's heart.
> (narrator)
380	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
381	Oh, so when you were small, Eumaeus the swineherd,
382	you were driven far from your fatherland and your parents.
383	But come, tell me this and say it exactly.
384	Was a wide-streeted city of men sacked,
385	in which your father and your lady mother lived,
386	or did hostile men take you when you were left alone by the sheep or by the cattle
387	in ships and sell you
388	to this man's house, and he gave a worthy price?
> (narrator)
389	Then the swineherd, leader of men, spoke to him:
> Eumaeus
390	Stranger, since you ask me these things and inquire,
391	sit now in silence and take pleasure, and drink wine
392	sitting. These nights are beyond measure. There is time to sleep,
393	and there is time for those taking pleasure to listen. You have no need
394	to lie down before the time. Too much sleep is a pain too.
395	Of the others, whoever his heart and spirit bid,
396	let him go out and sleep. At the appearing of dawn
397	when he has dined let him follow the master's pigs.
398	We two in the hut, drinking and feasting,
399	will take pleasure in each other's wretched cares,
400	remembering. A man takes pleasure even among pains,
401	whoever has suffered very many things and wandered much.
402	I will tell you this that you ask me and inquire.
403	There is an island called Syria, if you have heard of it somewhere,
404	above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are.
405	It is not so very crowded, but it is good,
406	good for cattle, good for sheep, full of wine, of much grain.
407	Hunger never comes into the people, and no other
408	hateful disease comes on poor mortals.
409	When the tribes of people grow old through the city,
410	Apollo of the silver bow comes with Artemis
411	and coming at them with his gentle shafts he kills them.
412	There are two cities, and everything is divided in two for them.
413	My father was king over both,
414	Ctesius son of Ormenus, like the deathless.
415	Phoenician men famous for ships came there,
416	gnawers, bringing countless playthings in a black ship.
417	There was a Phoenician woman in my father's house,
418	beautiful and tall and knowing shining works.
419	The very cunning Phoenicians deceived her.
420	First one mixed with her as she was washing, beside the hollow ship,
421	in bed and love, which deceives the mind
422	of female women, even one who is a good worker.
423	He then asked who she was and where she came from.
424	She at once marked my father's high-roofed house:
425	I claim to be from Sidon of much bronze,
426	and I am the daughter of Arybas, overflowing with wealth.
427	Taphian pirate men snatched me
428	as I was coming from the country, and they sold me, bringing me here
429	to this man's house. He gave a worthy price.
430	Then the man who was mixing with her in secret spoke to her:
431	Would you now follow home with us again,
432	so you may see the high-roofed house of your father and mother
433	and them themselves? They are still alive and they are called rich.
434	The woman spoke to him and answered with a word:
435	That could be, if you sailors would be willing
436	to be bound by an oath that you will take me home unharmed.
437	So she spoke, and they all swore as she told them.
438	When they had sworn and finished the oath,
439	the woman spoke among them again and answered with a word:
440	In silence now, let none of your companions
441	speak to me with words, meeting me either in the street
442	or somewhere at a spring, or someone may go to the house and tell
443	the old man, and he, guessing, may bind me
444	in a hard bond, and he will plot destruction for you.
445	Hold the word in your minds, and hurry the price of your cargo.
446	When the ship is full of livelihood,
447	then let a message come to me quickly at the house.
448	I will bring gold too, whatever comes under my hand.
449	I would also willingly give another fare.
450	I am raising the child of a good man in the halls,
451	such a cunning one, running out the door with me.
452	I would bring him to the ship, and he would fetch you an endless price
453	wherever you sell him among men of another speech.
454	So speaking she went away to the beautiful house,
455	and they stayed right there with us a whole year
456	and they traded much livelihood in the hollow ship.
457	When the hollow ship was loaded for them to go,
458	then they sent a messenger to tell the woman.
459	A man of much knowledge came to my father's house
460	holding a gold necklace, strung with amber.
461	The serving-women and the lady mother in the hall
462	felt it with their hands and looked at it with their eyes,
463	promising a price. He nodded to her in silence.
464	When he had nodded he went to the hollow ship,
465	and she, taking me by the hand, led me out of the house to the door.
466	She found in the porch both cups and tables
467	of the feasting men who were attending my father.
468	They had gone forward to the seat and the rumor of the people,
469	and she quickly hid three cups under her fold
470	and carried them out. I followed in my foolishness.
471	The sun went down, and all the ways grew dark.
472	We came quickly to the famous harbor,
473	where the fast ship of Phoenician men was.
474	They then went aboard and sailed the wet ways,
475	putting us two aboard. Zeus sent a wind on them.
476	For six days we sailed alike, nights and day.
477	When Zeus, son of Cronus, set the seventh day on us,
478	Artemis who showers arrows then hit the woman,
479	and she dropped into the hold like a sea-diving bird.
480	They threw her out to become prey for seals and fish.
481	I was left behind, grieving in my heart.
482	Wind and water carrying them brought them to Ithaca,
483	where Laertes bought me with his own possessions.
484	So I saw this land with my eyes.
> (narrator)
485	Then Zeus-born Odysseus answered him with a word:
> Odysseus
486	Eumaeus, you have very much stirred the heart in my mind
487	telling each of these things, how many pains you suffered in your heart.
488	But Zeus has set a good thing beside you even with the bad,
489	since after much toil you reached the house of a
490	gentle man, who provides you food and drink
491	kindly, and you live a good life. I
492	have come here wandering through many towns of mortals.
> (narrator)
493	So they spoke such things to one another,
494	and they slept not for a long time, but a little.
495	Dawn of the good throne came at once. On the dry land
496	the companions of Telemachus loosed the sails and took down the mast
497	quickly, and they rowed her forward into the harbor.
498	They threw out the mooring-stones and bound the stern-cables.
499	They themselves went out onto the shore of the sea
500	and made supper and mixed sparkling wine.
501	When they had put away the desire for drink and food,
502	thoughtful Telemachus began the words:
> Telemachus
503	You now drive the black ship to the town,
504	and I will go to the fields and the herders.
505	In the evening I will come down to the town when I have seen my works.
506	At dawn I would set a travel-gift before you,
507	a good feast of meat and of wine sweet to drink.
> (narrator)
508	Then godlike Theoclymenus spoke to him:
> Theoclymenus
509	Where am I to go, dear child? To whose house am I to come
510	of the men who are lords through rocky Ithaca?
511	Or am I to go straight to your mother and your house?
> (narrator)
512	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
513	Otherwise I would tell you to go even to our house.
514	There is no lack of guest-gifts. But for you yourself
515	it would be worse, since I will be away, and my mother will not
516	see you. She does not often appear among the suitors in the house,
517	but away from them she weaves a loom in the upper room.
518	I will show you another man you could go to,
519	Eurymachus, the shining son of Polybus of knowing mind,
520	whom the men of Ithaca now look on equal to a god.
521	He is far the best man, and he most wants
522	to marry my mother and to have the honor of Odysseus.
523	But Olympian Zeus, living in the sky, knows
524	whether he will finish a bad day for them before the marriage.
> (narrator)
525	As he said that a bird flew on the right,
526	a falcon, the fast messenger of Apollo. In its feet
527	it held a dove and was plucking it, and it poured the feathers to the ground
528	between the ship and Telemachus himself.
529	Theoclymenus called him apart from the companions
530	and took his hand and spoke a word and called him by name:
> Theoclymenus
531	Telemachus, that bird did not fly on the right without a god.
532	I knew it looking it in the face, that it was a bird-sign.
533	There is no other race more kingly than yours
534	in the people of Ithaca. You will always be strong.
> (narrator)
535	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
536	I wish this word, stranger, were finished.
537	Then you would soon know friendship and many gifts
538	from me, so that someone meeting you would call you blessed.
> (narrator)
539	He also spoke to Peiraeus, a trusted companion:
> Telemachus
540	Peiraeus, son of Clytius, you most of all
541	obey me of my companions who followed me to Pylos.
542	Now too, leading this stranger in your halls,
543	love him kindly and honor him until I come.
> (narrator)
544	Then Peiraeus famous for the spear spoke opposite him:
> Peiraeus
545	Telemachus, even if you should stay here a long time,
546	I will care for this man, and he will have no lack of guest-gifts.
> (narrator)
547	So speaking he went aboard the ship and told the companions
548	to go aboard themselves and to loose the stern-cables.
549	They went aboard at once and sat at the benches.
550	Telemachus bound beautiful sandals under his feet
551	and took a strong spear, sharpened with sharp bronze,
552	from the deck of the ship. They loosed the stern-cables.
553	They pushed off and sailed to the city, as
554	Telemachus, own son of godlike Odysseus, told them.
555	His feet carried him going quickly forward, until he reached the courtyard
556	where he had very many pigs, with which the swineherd,
557	being good, slept, knowing gentle things toward his lords.
