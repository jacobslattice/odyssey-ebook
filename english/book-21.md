# Odyssey Book 21

> (narrator)
1	The goddess, owl-eyed Athena, put it in the mind
2	of the daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope,
3	to set the bow and the grey iron for the suitors
4	in the halls of Odysseus, contests and a beginning of killing.
5	She went up the high ladder of her own house
6	and took a well-bent key in a thick hand,
7	beautiful, of bronze. A handle of ivory was on it.
8	She went to go to the room with serving-women
9	the farthest. There the stored things of the lord lay,
10	bronze and gold and much-worked iron.
11	There the back-bent bow lay and a quiver
12	holding arrows, and many groaning arrows were in it,
13	gifts a guest-friend in Lacedaemon happened to give him,
14	Iphitus son of Eurytus, like the deathless.
15	The two met one another in Messene
16	in the house of war-minded Ortilochus. Odysseus
17	had come after a debt that the whole people owed him.
18	Messenian men had lifted from Ithaca
19	three hundred flocks and the herders in many-benched ships.
20	For these Odysseus had come on a long road as a messenger,
21	being a child. His father and the other old men had sent him forward.
22	Iphitus was seeking horses that had been lost to him,
23	twelve females, and under them hard-working mules.
24	They later became death and fate for him too,
25	when he reached the strong-hearted son of Zeus,
26	the man Heracles, knowing in great works,
27	who killed him being a guest-friend in his own house,
28	hard man, and he did not respect the watch of the gods or the table
29	which he himself had set beside him. Then he also killed him,
30	and he himself had the strong-hoofed horses in the halls.
31	Asking about those he met Odysseus, and he gave the bow,
32	which great Eurytus used to carry before, and he
33	left it to his child dying in the high houses.
34	Odysseus gave him a sharp sword and a fighting spear,
35	the beginning of a caring guest-friendship. They did not know
36	one another at the table. Before that the son of Zeus killed
37	Iphitus son of Eurytus, like the deathless,
38	who gave him the bow. Godlike Odysseus never
39	going to war on the black ships
40	used to take it, but there as a memorial of a dear guest-friend
41	it used to lie in the halls, and he used to carry it on his own land.
42	When the bright one of women reached that room
43	she went up on the oak threshold, which a carpenter once
44	had planed skillfully and straightened to the line,
45	and he fitted doorposts in it and put on shining doors.
46	At once she quickly loosed the strap from the hook
47	and put the key in, and she knocked the bars of the doors
48	up, aiming opposite. They bellowed like a bull
49	grazing in a meadow. So much the beautiful door-leaves bellowed
50	hit by the key, and they opened quickly for her.
51	She went on a high plank. Chests stood there,
52	and in them fragrant clothes lay.
53	Reaching from there she took the bow from a peg
54	with the case itself, the shining one that lay around it.
55	Sitting down there, putting it on her own knees,
56	she cried very clearly, and she took out the lord's bow.
57	When she had had her fill of the much-wept lament
58	she went to go to the hall among the shining suitors
59	holding in her hand the back-bent bow and the quiver
60	holding arrows. Many groaning arrows were in it.
61	With her the serving-women carried a chest, where iron
62	lay much, and bronze, the contests of that lord.
63	When the bright one of women reached the suitors
64	she stood beside the post of the well-made roof,
65	holding the shining veil across her cheeks.
66	A trusted serving-woman stood on each side.
67	At once she spoke among the suitors and said a word:
> Penelope
68	Hear me, proud suitors, who this house
69	have been using to eat and drink always without stopping
70	of a man gone a long time. You have not been able
71	to make any other holding-back of a word,
72	but wanting to marry me and make me a wife.
73	Come, suitors, since this contest appears.
74	I will set the great bow of godlike Odysseus.
75	Whoever most easily strings the bow in his palms
76	and shoots an arrow through all twelve axes,
77	I would follow with that man, leaving this house
78	of my wedding, very beautiful, full of living.
79	I think I will remember it even in a dream.
> (narrator)
80	So she spoke, and she told Eumaeus, the godlike swineherd,
81	to set the bow and the grey iron for the suitors.
82	Crying Eumaeus took it and set it down.
83	The cowherd cried in another place, when he saw the lord's bow.
84	Antinous rebuked them and spoke a word and called them by name:
> Antinous
85	Childish countrymen, thinking things of the day,
86	ah wretched, why do you pour a tear and
87	stir the heart in the woman's chest? Her heart
88	anyway lies in pains, since she has lost her own husband.
89	Dine sitting silent, or to the door
90	go out and cry, leaving the bows there,
91	an unhurtful contest for the suitors. I do not think
92	this well-polished bow will be strung easily.
93	There is no such man among all these
94	as Odysseus was. I myself have seen him,
95	and I am remembering; I was still a child.
> (narrator)
96	So he spoke, and the heart in his chest hoped
97	to string the sinew and shoot an arrow through the iron.
98	He was going to taste the arrow first
99	from the hands of faultless Odysseus, whom he then was dishonoring
100	sitting in the halls, and he was urging all the companions on.
101	The holy force of Telemachus also spoke among them:
> Telemachus
102	Oh, Zeus son of Cronus has very much made me mindless.
103	My own mother, even being sensible, says
104	she will follow another leaving this house.
105	I laugh and take pleasure with a mindless heart.
106	Come, suitors, since this contest appears,
107	such a woman as is not now through the Achaean land,
108	not of holy Pylos or of Argos or of Mycenae,
109	not of Ithaca itself or of the black mainland.
110	You yourselves know this. Why do I need the mother's praise?
111	Come, do not drag it with delays and no longer
112	turn away from the stringing of the bow for long, so we may see.
113	I myself would also try this bow.
114	If I string it and shoot an arrow through the iron,
115	the lady mother would not leave these houses for me grieving
116	going with another, when I were left behind
117	already able to take up the father's beautiful contests.
> (narrator)
118	He spoke and put the crimson cloak off his shoulders
119	springing up straight, and he put the sharp sword off his shoulders.
120	First he set the axes, digging a trench
121	one long one for all, and he straightened them to the line,
122	and he rammed earth around. Wonder took all who saw
123	how he set them in good order. He had never yet seen it before.
124	Going he stood on the threshold and was trying the bow.
125	Three times he made it quiver, eager to draw it,
126	and three times he let go of the force, hoping that in his heart,
127	to string the sinew and shoot an arrow through the iron.
128	He would have strung it drawing it the fourth time by force,
129	but Odysseus nodded no and held him even though he was eager.
130	The holy force of Telemachus spoke among them again:
> Telemachus
131	Oh, then I will also be bad and without strength,
132	or I am younger and I do not yet trust my hands
133	to defend a man, when someone earlier makes it hard.
134	Come, you who are better than me in force,
135	try the bow, and let us finish the contest.
> (narrator)
136	So speaking he put the bow away from himself to the ground,
137	leaning it against the fitted well-polished planks,
138	and he leaned the swift missile there against the beautiful tip,
139	and he sat again on the throne from which he had stood.
140	Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke among them:
> Antinous
141	Rise in order from the right, all companions,
142	beginning from the place from which the wine is poured.
> (narrator)
143	So Antinous spoke, and the word pleased them.
144	Leodes stood up first, son of Oenops,
145	who was their sacrifice-watcher, and beside the beautiful mixing-bowl
146	he always sat farthest in. Reckless things to him alone
147	were hateful, and he blamed all the suitors.
148	He then first took the bow and the swift missile.
149	Going he stood on the threshold and was trying the bow,
150	and he did not string it. Before that he wore out his hands drawing,
151	unworn, soft. He spoke among the suitors:
> Leodes
152	Friends, I do not string it. Let another also take it.
153	This bow will make many of the best men lose
154	heart and life, since it is much better
155	to die than living to miss, for whose sake always
156	we mix here, waiting all the days.
157	Now someone even hopes in his mind and desires
158	to marry Penelope, the wife of Odysseus.
159	When he tries the bow and sees,
160	then some other of the well-robed Achaean women
161	let him woo seeking with bride-gifts. She then
162	would marry whoever gives the most and comes as the fated one.
> (narrator)
163	So he spoke and put the bow away from himself,
164	leaning it against the fitted well-polished planks,
165	and he leaned the swift missile there against the beautiful tip,
166	and he sat again on the throne from which he had stood.
167	Antinous rebuked him and spoke a word and called him by name:
> Antinous
168	Leodes, what word has escaped you, the fence of the teeth,
169	terrible and hard — I am angry hearing it —
170	if this bow will make the best men lose
171	heart and life, because you cannot string it.
172	The lady mother did not bear you such
173	as to be a drawer of the bow and of arrows.
174	The other shining suitors will soon string it.
> (narrator)
175	So he spoke, and he told Melanthius the goatherd:
176	Come, light a fire in the halls, Melantheus,
177	and set a great chair beside it and a fleece on it,
178	and bring a great wheel of fat from what is inside,
179	so that we young men warming it, anointing it with oil,
180	may try the bow and finish the contest.
> (narrator)
181	So he spoke, and Melanthius at once lit the unwearying fire,
182	and bringing a chair he set it and a fleece on it,
183	and he brought a great wheel of fat from what is inside.
184	The young men warming it with that were trying. They could not
185	string it, and they were much lacking in force.
186	Antinous still held off, and godlike Eurymachus,
187	leaders of the suitors. They were outstanding best in excellence.
188	The two went from the house going together, both,
189	the cowherd and the swineherd of godlike Odysseus.
190	Godlike Odysseus himself came from the house after them.
191	When they were outside the doors and the courtyard,
192	speaking he addressed them with honeyed words:
> Odysseus
193	Cowherd, and you, swineherd, would I tell some word,
194	or do I hide it myself? The heart tells me to speak.
195	What sort would you be to defend Odysseus, if he came from somewhere
196	so very suddenly and some god brought him?
197	Would you defend the suitors or Odysseus?
198	Say how the heart and the spirit tell you.
> (narrator)
199	Then the man, overseer of cattle, spoke to him:
> Philoetius
200	Father Zeus, I wish you would finish this wish,
201	that that man come, and a god bring him.
202	You would know what my power is and how the hands follow.
> (narrator)
203	So also Eumaeus prayed to all the gods
204	that many-minded Odysseus come home to his own house.
205	When he knew their mind without missing,
206	he answered them again with words:
> Odysseus
207	Inside here I am this man myself. Having labored many evils
208	I have come in the twentieth year to the fatherland.
209	I know that I reach you two wishing it,
210	alone of the slaves. Of the others I have not heard anyone
211	praying that I come home again turning back.
212	To you two, as it will be, I will tell the truth.
213	If a god under me breaks the shining suitors,
214	I will lead wives for both and I will grant possessions
215	and houses built near me. Then
216	you will be companions and brothers of Telemachus for me.
217	Come, I will also show some other very clear sign,
218	so that you may know me well and trust it in your heart,
219	the scar, which a boar once drove into me with a white tooth
220	when I went to Parnassus with the sons of Autolycus.
> (narrator)
221	So speaking he held the rags away from the great scar.
222	When the two had seen it and marked each thing well,
223	they cried around war-minded Odysseus throwing their hands,
224	and they kissed him loving him, the head and the shoulders.
225	So also Odysseus kissed their heads and hands.
226	The light of the sun would have gone down on them lamenting,
227	if Odysseus himself had not held them back and spoken:
> Odysseus
228	Stop the crying and the lament, so that someone going out
229	of the hall does not see, and also say it inside.
230	Go in one after another, not all together,
231	I first, then you. Let this be made a sign:
232	all the others, as many as are shining suitors,
233	will not let them give me the bow and the quiver.
234	You, godlike Eumaeus, bringing the bow through the house
235	put it in my hands, and tell the women
236	to lock the doors of the hall closely fitted,
237	and if someone hears a groaning or a noise inside
238	of men in our enclosures, do not at all
239	come forward to the door, but stay there silent beside the work.
240	You, godlike Philoetius, I charge to lock the doors of the courtyard
241	with a key, and quickly throw a fastening on.
> (narrator)
242	So speaking he went into the well-lived house.
243	He sat then going to the chair from which he had stood.
244	The two slaves of godlike Odysseus also went in.
245	Eurymachus was already turning the bow in his hands,
246	warming it here and here in the shine of the fire. Even so he could not
247	string it, and he groaned greatly, the famous heart.
248	Troubled he said a word and spoke and called them by name:
> Eurymachus
249	Oh, this is a pain for me about myself and about all.
250	I do not lament the marriage so much, though I am grieved.
251	There are also many other Achaean women, some in
252	sea-girt Ithaca itself, some in other cities.
253	But if we are so much lacking in the force
254	of godlike Odysseus, that we cannot string
255	the bow, it is a shame even for those who will be to learn.
> (narrator)
256	Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to him:
> Antinous
257	Eurymachus, it will not be this way. You yourself also think it.
258	Now through the people there is a holy feast of that god.
259	Who would stretch bows? Sit in peace
260	and put it down. We may leave the axes even all of them
261	standing. I do not think anyone will take them up,
262	coming into the hall of Odysseus son of Laertes.
263	Come, let the wine-pourer begin with the cups,
264	so that pouring a libation we may put down the curved bows.
265	At dawn tell Melanthius the goatherd
266	to lead goats that stand out greatly among all the herds,
267	so that setting thigh-bones for famous-bowed Apollo
268	we may try the bow and finish the contest.
> (narrator)
269	So Antinous spoke, and the word pleased them.
270	Heralds poured water on their hands,
271	and young men crowned the mixing-bowls with drink,
272	and they distributed to all beginning with the cups.
273	When they had poured and drunk as much as the heart wished,
274	Odysseus of many devices spoke among them planning a trick:
> Odysseus
275	Hear me, suitors of the very famous queen,
276	so I may say what the heart in my chest tells me.
277	I beg Eurymachus most and godlike Antinous,
278	since he also said this word according to portion,
279	now to stop the bow, and turn it over to the gods.
280	At dawn a god will give force to whoever he wishes.
281	But come, give me the well-polished bow, so that among you
282	I may try the hands and the strength, whether I still have
283	force, such as was in the bent limbs before,
284	or wandering and lack of care have already destroyed it for me.
> (narrator)
285	So he spoke, and they all were very angry at the outrage,
286	afraid he would string the well-polished bow.
287	Antinous rebuked him and spoke a word and called him by name:
> Antinous
288	Ah wretched of strangers, there are not even a few wits in you.
289	Are you not content that in peace among us overbearing
290	you dine, and you are not robbed of the feast at all, and you hear
291	our words and our speech? No other
292	stranger and beggar hears our words.
293	Honey-sweet wine is wounding you, which also hurts
294	others, whoever takes it gaping and does not drink what is portioned.
295	Wine also wrecked the Centaur, very famous Eurytion,
296	in the hall of great-hearted Pirithous,
297	when he came to the Lapiths. When he wrecked his wits with wine
298	raging he did bad things through the house of Pirithous.
299	Pain took the heroes, and through the doorway to the door
300	they dragged him springing up, and with pitiless bronze
301	they reaped ears and nose. He wrecked in his own wits
302	went carrying his ruin with a blown-about heart.
303	From that a quarrel was made for Centaurs and men,
304	and he first found evil for himself being heavy with wine.
305	So I also show a great pain for you, if you
306	string the bow. You will not meet any kindness
307	in our people, and at once in a black ship
308	to Echetus the king, destroyer of all mortals,
309	we will send you. From there you will not be saved at all. Drink in peace
310	and do not quarrel among younger men.
> (narrator)
311	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
312	Antinous, it is not beautiful or just to short
313	the strangers of Telemachus, whoever reaches this house.
314	Do you hope, if the stranger strings the great bow of Odysseus
315	trusting in his hands and his own force,
316	that he will lead me home and make me his wife?
317	He himself does not hope this in his chest somewhere.
318	Let none of you grieving the heart for that
319	dine here, since it is not even fitting.
> (narrator)
320	Then Eurymachus, child of Polybus, spoke opposite her:
> Eurymachus
321	Daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope,
322	we do not think this man will lead you. It is not fitting.
323	We are ashamed at the speech of men and women,
324	so that some other worse of the Achaeans may never say
325	that much worse men are wooing the wife of a faultless man,
326	and they cannot string the well-polished bow at all.
327	But some other beggar man coming wandering
328	easily strung the bow and sent it through the iron.
329	So they will say, and these would be shames for us.
> (narrator)
330	Then careful Penelope spoke to him:
> Penelope
331	Eurymachus, there is no way to be well-famed through the people
332	for those who eating dishonor the house
333	of a best man. Why do you set these as shames?
334	This stranger is very large and well-built,
335	and he claims to be the son of a good father by birth.
336	Come, give him the well-polished bow, so we may see.
337	I will tell you this way, and this will also be finished:
338	if he strings it, and Apollo gives him the boast,
339	I will put a cloak and a tunic on him, beautiful clothes,
340	and I will give a sharp javelin, a warden-off of dogs and men,
341	and a two-edged sword. I will give sandals under his feet,
342	and I will send him wherever the heart and the spirit tell him.
> (narrator)
343	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite her:
> Telemachus
344	Mother of mine, no one of the Achaeans is stronger than me
345	over the bow, to whom I wish, to give it and to refuse it,
346	not as many as lord it through rocky Ithaca,
347	not as many as on the islands toward horse-feeding Elis.
348	None of those will force me unwilling, if I wish
349	even once to give these bows to the stranger to carry.
350	Go into the house and care for your own works,
351	the loom and the spindle, and tell the serving-women
352	to go after the work. The bow will be a care to the men
353	all of them, and most to me. The force in the house is mine.
> (narrator)
354	Wondering she went back to the house.
355	She put the child's thoughtful word in her heart.
356	Going up to the upper room with serving-women
357	she then cried for Odysseus, her own husband, until
358	owl-eyed Athena threw sweet sleep on her eyelids.
359	The godlike swineherd taking the curved bow was carrying it.
360	All the suitors were shouting together in the halls.
361	So one of the overbearing young men would say:
> a suitor
362	Where are you carrying the curved bows, unenviable swineherd,
363	wanderer? Soon again the swift dogs will eat you down among the pigs
364	alone from humans, which you raised, if Apollo
365	is kind to us and the other deathless gods.
> (narrator)
366	So they spoke, and he set it carrying it in the same place,
367	afraid, because many were shouting together in the halls.
368	Telemachus from the other side threatened and called out:
> Telemachus
369	Atta, carry the bows forward. Soon you will not obey all well.
370	I, even being younger, may drive you to the country,
371	throwing with stones. I am better in force.
372	I wish I were so much better in hands and force
373	than all the suitors who are through the house.
374	Then I would soon send someone to go hated
375	out of our house, since they contrive evils.
> (narrator)
376	So he spoke, and they all laughed sweetly at him,
377	the suitors, and they let go of the hard anger
378	at Telemachus. The swineherd carrying the bow through the house
379	standing put it in the hands of war-minded Odysseus.
380	Calling her out he spoke to the nurse Eurycleia:
> Eumaeus
381	Telemachus tells you, careful Eurycleia,
382	to lock the doors of the hall closely fitted.
383	If someone hears a groaning or a noise inside
384	of men in our enclosures, do not at all
385	come forward to the door, but stay there silent beside the work.
> (narrator)
386	So he spoke, and the word was wingless for her.
387	She locked the doors of the well-lived halls.
388	In silence Philoetius sprang out of the house to the door
389	and then locked the doors of the well-fenced courtyard.
390	Under the porch lay a cable of a double-curved ship,
391	of papyrus, with which he bound the doors, and he himself went in.
392	He sat then going to the chair from which he had stood,
393	looking at Odysseus. He was already turning the bow
394	turning it every way, trying it here and here,
395	so that worms had not eaten the horns, the lord gone.
396	So one would say looking at another near:
> a suitor
397	This man is some viewer and a thief of bows.
398	Either such things also lie for him at home
399	or he is starting to make one, as in his hands
400	he turns it here and here, a wanderer practiced in evils.
> (narrator)
401	Another of the overbearing young men would say:
> a suitor
402	I wish he would meet so much of benefit
403	as this man will ever be able to string this.
> (narrator)
404	So the suitors spoke. Odysseus of many devices,
405	at once when he had lifted the great bow and seen it every way,
406	as when a man knowing the lyre and song
407	easily stretches a string around a new peg,
408	fastening from both sides the well-twisted gut of a sheep,
409	so without effort Odysseus strung the great bow.
410	Taking it in the right hand he tried the sinew.
411	It sang beautifully under, like a swallow in voice.
412	A great pain came to the suitors, and the skin of all
413	turned. Zeus thundered greatly showing signs.
414	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus was glad
415	that the child of crooked-planned Cronus had sent him a sign.
416	He took a swift arrow, which lay beside him at the table
417	bare. The others lay inside the hollow quiver,
418	which the Achaeans were soon going to try.
419	Taking it on the bridge he drew the sinew and the notches,
420	sitting right there from the chair, and he sent the arrow
421	aiming opposite, and he did not miss any of the axes
422	from the first socket, and through and through out to the door
423	went the bronze-heavy arrow. He spoke to Telemachus:
> Odysseus
424	Telemachus, the stranger sitting in the halls does not shame you.
425	I did not miss the mark at all and I did not
426	labor long stringing the bow. The force is still fixed in me,
427	not as the suitors dishonoring me blame me.
428	Now it is the season for the Achaeans to make supper too
429	in the light, and then also otherwise to play
430	with song and the lyre. Those are the dedications of the feast.
> (narrator)
431	He spoke and nodded on the brows. Telemachus,
432	the own son of godlike Odysseus, put the sharp sword around him
433	and threw his own hand around the spear, and near him
434	he stood beside the throne armed with flaming bronze.
