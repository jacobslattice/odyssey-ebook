# Odyssey Book 16

> (narrator)
1	Meanwhile in the hut Odysseus and the godlike swineherd
2	were making a morning meal at dawn, having kindled a fire,
3	and they sent out the herders with the gathered pigs.
4	The barking dogs fawned around Telemachus
5	and they did not bark as he came. Godlike Odysseus marked
6	the dogs fawning, and the sound of feet came around.
7	At once he said winged words to Eumaeus:
> Odysseus
8	Eumaeus, some companion of yours will come here,
9	or some other known man, since the dogs are not barking
10	but they are fawning around. I hear the thud of feet.
> (narrator)
11	The word was not yet all said when his own son
12	stood in the doorway. The swineherd sprang up astonished,
13	and the vessels he was working with fell from his hands,
14	mixing sparkling wine. He went opposite his lord
15	and kissed his head and both his beautiful eyes
16	and both his hands. A thick tear fell from him.
17	As a father lovingly embraces his own child
18	coming from a far land in the tenth year,
19	his only late-born, for whom he has suffered many pains,
20	so then the godlike swineherd kissed godlike Telemachus
21	all over, clinging around him, as one escaped from death.
22	Lamenting he said winged words:
> Eumaeus
23	You have come, Telemachus, sweet light. I no longer
24	thought I would see you, since you went in a ship to Pylos.
25	But come in now, dear child, so I may
26	take pleasure in my heart looking at you, new from elsewhere, being inside.
27	You do not often come to the country or the herders.
28	You stay in town. That is how it has pleased your heart,
29	to look on the destructive crowd of suitor men.
> (narrator)
30	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
31	It will be so, atta. I have come here for your sake,
32	so I may see you with my eyes and hear a word,
33	whether my mother still stays in the halls, or someone
34	already of other men has married her, and the bed of Odysseus
35	somewhere lies having bad spider-webs from lack of sleepers.
> (narrator)
36	Then the swineherd, leader of men, spoke to him:
> Eumaeus
37	She stays very much, with an enduring heart,
38	in your halls. The miserable nights and days
39	always waste her as she sheds tears.
> (narrator)
40	So speaking he took the bronze spear from him.
41	Telemachus went inside and crossed the stone threshold.
42	As he came on, his father Odysseus gave up the seat.
43	Telemachus held him back from the other side and spoke:
> Telemachus
44	Sit, stranger. We will find a seat elsewhere
45	in our steading. There is a man here who will set it.
> (narrator)
46	So he spoke, and Odysseus went back and sat down. The swineherd
47	threw green brush under him and a fleece on top.
48	Then the own son of Odysseus sat there.
49	The swineherd set plates of meat before them
50	roasted, which they had left eating the day before,
51	and he quickly piled bread beside them in baskets,
52	and he mixed honey-sweet wine in an ivy-wood bowl.
53	He himself sat opposite godlike Odysseus.
54	They reached out their hands to the food lying ready.
55	When they had put away the desire for drink and food,
56	then Telemachus spoke to the godlike swineherd:
> Telemachus
57	Atta, where did this stranger come to you from? How did sailors
58	bring him to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be?
59	I do not think he reached here on foot.
> (narrator)
60	You answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
> Eumaeus
61	I will tell you, child, all things true.
62	He claims his birth from wide Crete,
63	and he says he has been turned through many towns of mortals
64	wandering. A god has spun that for him.
65	Now he has run from a ship of Thesprotian men
66	and come to my steading. I put him in your hands.
67	Do as you want. He claims to be your suppliant.
> (narrator)
68	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
69	Eumaeus, that is a very heart-hurting word you have spoken.
70	How am I to receive the stranger in the house?
71	I myself am young, and I do not yet trust my hands
72	to keep a man off, when someone is harsh first.
73	My mother's heart in her mind turns two ways,
74	whether she should stay right here with me and care for the house,
75	respecting the bed of her husband and the rumor of the people,
76	or already follow whoever is best of the Achaeans
77	who courts in the halls, the man who brings the most.
78	But the stranger, since he has come to your house,
79	I will put a cloak and a tunic on him, fine clothes,
80	and I will give him a two-edged sword and sandals for his feet,
81	and I will send him wherever his heart and spirit bid.
82	If you want, you take care of him, holding him in the steading.
83	I will send clothes here and all the food
84	to eat, so he will not wear you and the companions out.
85	I would not let him go there among the suitors.
86	They have too much reckless violence.
87	They may mock him, and it will be a grim grief for me.
88	It is hard for a man, even a strong one, to do anything
89	being among more, since they are much stronger.
> (narrator)
90	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus spoke to him:
> Odysseus
91	Friend, since it is right for me to answer too,
92	my own heart is very much torn as I listen,
93	such reckless things you say the suitors are devising
94	in the halls, against your will, you being such a man.
95	Tell me, are you willingly put under, or do the people
96	through the land hate you, following a god's voice,
97	or do you find fault with brothers, on whom a man
98	trusts when they fight, even if a great quarrel rises?
99	I wish I were so young, with this heart,
100	either the child of faultless Odysseus or even himself.
102	Then at once a stranger man might cut my head from me,
103	if I did not become an evil to all of them,
104	going into the hall of Odysseus, son of Laertes.
105	If they overcame me by numbers, I being alone,
106	I would rather be killed in my own halls
107	and be dead than always look on these unseemly works,
108	strangers being beaten and serving-women
109	dragged unseemly through the beautiful house,
110	and wine being drawn, and them eating food
111	just so, unfinished, at a work that cannot be finished.
> (narrator)
112	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
113	I will tell you, stranger, very exactly.
114	The whole people is not hateful and harsh to me,
115	and I do not find fault with brothers, on whom a man
116	trusts when they fight, even if a great quarrel rises.
117	The son of Cronus has made our race only in this way:
118	Arceisius fathered Laertes as an only son,
119	and the father in turn fathered Odysseus as an only son. Odysseus
120	fathered me only in the halls and left me, and had no joy of me.
121	For that there are now very many enemies in the house.
122	As many as are the best who have power on the islands,
123	Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus,
124	and as many as are lords through rocky Ithaca,
125	so many court my mother, and they wear the house out.
126	She neither refuses the hateful marriage nor can she
127	make an end. They waste my house eating.
128	Soon they will shatter me myself too.
129	These things lie on the knees of the gods.
130	Atta, you go quicker, tell careful Penelope
131	that I am safe and have come from Pylos.
132	I will stay right here, and you come back here
133	when you have reported to her alone. Let none of the other Achaeans
134	learn it. Many are devising evils for me.
> (narrator)
135	You answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
> Eumaeus
136	I know, I understand. You tell these things to one who already thinks them.
137	But come, tell me this and say it exactly,
138	shall I go as a messenger the same road even to Laertes
139	the luckless, who until now, grieving greatly for Odysseus,
140	used to look over the work and in the house with the slaves
141	drink and eat, when the heart in his chest bid him.
142	Now, since you went in a ship to Pylos,
143	they say he has not yet eaten and drunk just so,
144	nor looked on the work, but with groaning and lament
145	he sits grieving, and the skin wastes around his bones.
> (narrator)
146	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
147	It is more painful, but we will leave him anyway, though we grieve.
148	If all things could somehow be taken at will by mortals,
149	we would first take our father's day of coming home.
150	You, when you have reported, come back, and do not
151	wander through the country after that man. Tell the mother
152	to urge the serving-woman housekeeper as quickly as possible
153	in secret. She could report to the old man.
> (narrator)
154	He spoke and started the swineherd. Eumaeus took sandals in his hands,
155	and binding them under his feet he went to the city. Athena
156	did not miss Eumaeus the swineherd going from the steading,
157	but she came near. She was likened in body to a woman
158	beautiful and tall and knowing shining works.
159	She stood opposite the door of the hut, appearing to Odysseus.
160	Telemachus did not see her opposite and did not mark her,
161	for the gods do not yet appear clear to all,
162	but Odysseus and the dogs saw her, and they did not bark.
163	With a whimper they shrank the other way through the steading.
164	She nodded with her brows. Godlike Odysseus marked it
165	and went out of the hut past the great wall of the courtyard
166	and stood in front of her. Athena spoke to him:
> Athena
167	Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
168	now tell your word to your child and do not hide it,
169	so that when you two have fitted death and doom for the suitors
170	you may go toward the famous town. I myself
171	will not be long away from you two, eager to fight.
> (narrator)
172	Athena touched him with a gold staff.
173	First she put a well-washed cloak and a tunic
174	around his chest, and she increased his body and his youth.
175	He became dark-skinned again, and his jaws were stretched,
176	and dark beard-hair came around his chin.
177	When she had done this she went back. Odysseus
178	went into the hut. His own son was astonished at him,
179	and afraid he threw his eyes the other way, lest he be a god,
180	and speaking he said winged words to him:
> Telemachus
181	You have appeared different to me, stranger, just now than before.
182	You have other clothes, and your skin is no longer the same.
183	You are very much some god, of those who hold the wide sky.
184	Be gracious, so we may give you pleasing offerings
185	and gold gifts, worked ones. Spare us.
> (narrator)
186	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus answered him:
> Odysseus
187	I am no god. Why do you liken me to the deathless?
188	I am your father, for whose sake you groan
189	and suffer many pains, taking in the violence of men.
> (narrator)
190	So speaking he kissed his son, and down from his cheeks
191	he let a tear to the ground. Before he had always held it without a break.
192	Telemachus, for he was not yet persuaded that he was his father,
193	answered him again with words:
> Telemachus
194	You are not Odysseus, my father. A god
195	is charming me, so I will groan and lament still more.
196	No mortal man could devise these things
197	by his own mind, unless a god himself came
198	and easily, wanting it, made him young or old.
199	Just now you were an old man and you wore unseemly things.
200	Now you are like the gods who hold the wide sky.
> (narrator)
201	Odysseus of many devices answered him:
> Odysseus
202	Telemachus, it is not right for you, your own father being inside,
203	to wonder too much or to be amazed.
204	No other Odysseus will come here again,
205	but I am this one, such as I am, having suffered evils and wandered much,
206	I have come in the twentieth year to my fatherland.
207	This is the work of Athena, driver of the spoil,
208	who has made me such as she wants. She can,
209	at one time like a beggar, at another time
210	a young man wearing fine clothes on his skin.
211	It is easy for the gods who hold the wide sky
212	both to make a mortal man famous and to make him bad.
> (narrator)
213	So speaking he sat down, and Telemachus
214	threw himself around his good father and lamented, shedding tears,
215	and a longing for lament rose in both of them.
216	They cried out shrilly, more thickly than birds,
217	sea-eagles or hook-clawed vultures, whose children
218	country-men have taken before they become winged.
219	So they shed a pitiable tear under their brows.
220	The light of the sun would have gone down on them lamenting,
221	if Telemachus had not spoken to his father at once:
> Telemachus
222	On what kind of ship, own father, did sailors
223	bring you here to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be?
224	I do not think you reached here on foot.
> (narrator)
225	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus spoke to him:
> Odysseus
226	I will tell you the truth, child.
227	The Phaeacians famous for ships brought me, who also send
228	other people, whoever comes to them.
229	They brought me sleeping in the fast ship over the sea
230	and set me in Ithaca, and they gave me shining gifts,
231	bronze and gold in plenty and woven clothes.
232	Those things lie in caves by the will of the gods.
233	Now I have come here again by the urgings of Athena,
234	so we may take counsel about the slaughter of enemies.
235	But come, count the suitors and tell them out,
236	so I may know how many they are and what kind of men.
237	I will turn it over in my faultless heart
238	and plan whether we two will be able to stand against them
239	alone without others, or whether we will look for others too.
> (narrator)
240	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
241	Father, I have always heard your great fame,
242	that you are a spearman in your hands and of thoughtful counsel.
243	But you have said too great a thing. Wonder holds me. Two men
244	could not fight many, and strong ones.
245	The suitors are not a ten exactly, or even two only.
246	They are much more. You will soon know the number here.
247	From Dulichium there are two and fifty
248	chosen young men, and six serving-men follow them.
249	From Same there are four and twenty men.
250	From Zacynthus there are twenty young men of the Achaeans.
251	From Ithaca itself there are twelve, all the best,
252	and with them is Medon the herald and the godlike singer
253	and two attendants, skilled in carving.
254	If we meet all of these being inside,
255	I fear you will pay back their violence coming here with much bitterness and grimness.
256	You, if you can think of some defender,
257	plan who might defend us two with a willing heart.
> (narrator)
258	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus spoke to him:
> Odysseus
259	I will tell you, and you put it together and hear me.
260	Think whether Athena with father Zeus
261	will be enough for us two, or I will think of some other defender.
> (narrator)
262	Then thoughtful Telemachus spoke opposite him:
> Telemachus
263	Those two you speak of are good defenders,
264	sitting high in the clouds. They have power
265	over other men and over the deathless gods.
> (narrator)
266	Then much-enduring godlike Odysseus spoke to him:
> Odysseus
267	Those two will not be apart from us a long time
268	from the strong fight, when the force of Ares is decided
269	for the suitors and for us in my halls.
270	You go now at the appearing of dawn
271	home, and mix with the overbearing suitors.
272	The swineherd will later lead me toward the town,
273	like a wretched beggar and an old man.
274	If they dishonor me through the house, let your own heart
275	endure in your chest while I suffer badly,
276	even if they drag me through the house by the feet to the door
277	or hit me with missiles. You look on and hold out.
278	Tell them to stop their follies,
279	speaking them on with honeyed words. They will not
280	obey you at all. Their due day already stands beside them.
281	I will tell you something else, and put it in your mind.
282	When Athena of many counsels puts it in your mind,
283	I will nod to you with my head, and you then, marking it,
284	whatever Ares-gear lies in the halls,
285	take it all up and put it down in the inner part of the high store-room.
286	Speak aside to the suitors with soft words
287	when they miss it and ask you:
288	I have put it down out of the smoke, since it is no longer like
289	what Odysseus left when he went to Troy,
290	but it is spoiled, as far as the breath of the fire has reached.
291	The son of Cronus has put this still greater thing in my mind too:
292	that somehow, having drunk wine, setting up a quarrel among you,
293	you may wound one another and shame the feast
294	and the courting. Iron itself draws a man.
295	Leave two swords and two spears for us two alone
296	and two oxhide shields to take in our hands,
297	so we may rush and take them. Then Pallas Athena
298	will charm the others, and counsellor Zeus.
299	I will tell you something else, and put it in your mind.
300	If you are truly mine and of our blood,
301	let no one then hear that Odysseus is inside,
302	and let neither Laertes know it, nor the swineherd,
303	nor any of the household, nor Penelope herself,
304	but we two alone will learn the way of the women.
305	We may still test some of the slave men,
306	both where someone honors us and fears us in his heart,
307	and whoever does not regard it, and dishonors you being such a man.
> (narrator)
308	The shining son answered him:
> Telemachus
309	Father, I think you will know my heart even afterward.
310	No slackness holds me.
311	I do not think this will be a gain
312	for both of us. I bid you consider it.
313	You will go a long time just so, testing each one,
314	going after the work. They in the halls at their ease
315	devour the possessions beyond measure, and there is no sparing.
316	I bid you learn the women,
317	those who dishonor you and those who are without fault.
318	I would not want us to test the men
319	through the steadings. Let that work come later,
320	if you truly know some sign of Zeus who holds the aegis.
> (narrator)
321	So they spoke such things to one another,
322	and the well-made ship was being brought down to Ithaca,
323	the one that carried Telemachus from Pylos and all the companions.
324	When they reached the inside of the deep-bighted harbor
325	they hauled the black ship onto the mainland,
326	and the high-hearted attendants carried the tackle for them,
327	and at once they carried the very beautiful gifts to the house of Clytius.
328	They sent a herald forward to the house of Odysseus
329	to tell the message to careful Penelope,
330	that Telemachus was in the country, and he had told the ship
331	to sail off to the town, so the strong queen
332	would not be afraid in her heart and shed a tender tear.
333	The herald and the godlike swineherd met
334	for the same message, to tell the woman.
335	When they reached the house of the godlike king,
336	the herald spoke among the serving-women in the middle:
> herald
337	Already, queen, your own child has come.
> (narrator)
338	The swineherd spoke to Penelope standing close
339	all that his own son had told him to say.
340	When he had spoken the whole charge,
341	he went to go after the pigs, and he left the enclosures and the hall.
342	The suitors were pained and downcast in their hearts.
343	They went out of the hall past the great wall of the courtyard
344	and they sat there in front of the doors.
345	Eurymachus, son of Polybus, began to speak among them:
> Eurymachus
346	Friends, this road of Telemachus has been finished, a great work, overbearingly.
347	We said it would not be finished for him.
348	But come, let us haul the black ship, whichever is best,
349	and gather rower-sailors, who as quickly as possible
350	may tell those men to come home quickly.
> (narrator)
351	The word was not yet all said when Amphinomus saw the ship,
352	turning from his place, inside the deep-bighted harbor,
353	men taking in the sails and holding oars in their hands.
354	Laughing sweetly he spoke among his companions:
> Amphinomus
355	Let us not send any message now. These men are inside.
356	Either some one of the gods told them this, or they themselves saw
357	the ship going by, and they could not catch it.
> (narrator)
358	So he spoke, and they stood up and went to the shore of the sea.
359	At once they hauled the black ship onto the mainland,
360	and the high-hearted attendants carried the tackle for them.
361	They themselves went to the assembly in a crowd, and they did not let
362	any other, of the young or of the old, sit among them.
363	Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke among them:
> Antinous
364	Oh, how the gods have loosed this man from badness.
365	By day the watchers sat on the windy ridges
366	always one after another. With the sun going down
367	we never slept the night on the mainland, but on the sea
368	sailing in the fast ship we waited for bright Dawn,
369	lying in wait for Telemachus, so we might take him and waste him
370	himself. A god led him home meanwhile.
371	Here let us plan a grim death
372	for Telemachus, and let him not slip away from us. I do not think
373	these works will be finished while this one is alive.
374	He himself is knowing in counsel and mind,
375	and the people no longer altogether bring us their favor.
376	Come, before he gathers the Achaeans
377	to the assembly — I do not think he will let it go,
378	but he will be angry, and he will stand up and speak among all
379	that we fitted a sheer killing for him and did not catch him.
380	They will not approve when they hear the bad works.
381	They may do some evil and drive us out
382	of our land, and we may come to the people of others.
383	Let us take him first in the country away from the city
384	or on the road. We ourselves will have the livelihood and the possessions,
385	dividing them in due shares among us, and the house in turn
386	we would give his mother to have, and whoever marries her.
387	If this word does not please you, and you want
388	him himself to live and to have all his father's things,
389	then let us not gather here and eat his heart-pleasing possessions
390	in plenty, but let each man from the hall
391	court, seeking her with bride-gifts. She would then
392	marry whoever brings the most and comes as the fated one.
> (narrator)
393	So he spoke, and they all became hushed in silence.
394	Amphinomus spoke among them and said,
395	the shining son of Nisus, lord son of Aretias,
396	who from Dulichium of much grain, grassy,
397	led the suitors, and he most
398	pleased Penelope with his words. He had a good mind.
399	With good thought he spoke among them and said:
> Amphinomus
400	Friends, I would not want to kill
401	Telemachus. It is a terrible thing to kill a kingly race.
402	First let us ask the plans of the gods.
403	If the ordinances of great Zeus approve,
404	I myself will kill him and I will tell all the others.
405	If the gods turn us away, I bid us stop.
> (narrator)
406	So Amphinomus spoke, and the word pleased them.
407	At once then they stood up and went to the house of Odysseus,
408	and coming they sat on the polished thrones.
409	Careful Penelope thought of something else,
410	to appear to the suitors who have overbearing violence.
411	She had learned of the destruction of her child in the halls.
412	The herald Medon had told her, who had learned the plans.
413	She went to go to the hall with serving-women.
414	When the bright among women reached the suitors,
415	she stood by the pillar of the close-built roof,
416	holding the shining veils before her cheeks,
417	and she rebuked Antinous and spoke a word and called him by name:
> Penelope
418	Antinous, you who have violence, plotter of evils, they say you
419	are the best in the people of Ithaca among your age-mates
420	in counsel and in stories. You were not such.
421	Madman, why do you stitch death and doom for Telemachus,
422	and you do not regard suppliants, for whom Zeus
423	is a witness? It is not holy to stitch evils for one another.
424	Do you not know when your father came here in flight,
425	fearing the people? They were too angry,
426	because following Taphian pirates
427	he had pained the Thesprotians. They were at peace with us.
428	They wanted to waste him and tear out his own heart
429	and eat up his much livelihood that pleased the heart.
430	Odysseus held them back and stopped them, though they were eager.
431	Now you eat his house without honor, and you court his wife
432	and you kill his child, and you pain me greatly.
433	I bid you stop, and tell the others.
> (narrator)
434	Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, spoke opposite her:
> Eurymachus
435	Daughter of Icarius, careful Penelope,
436	take heart. Do not let these things be a care in your mind.
437	There is no such man, and there will not be, and there will not be born,
438	who will put his hands on Telemachus your son
439	while I am alive and looking on the earth.
440	I will say it this way, and it will be finished:
441	soon his dark blood will flow around our
442	spear, since the sacker of cities Odysseus
443	often sat me on his knees and put roast meat
444	in my hands, and held out red wine.
445	For that Telemachus is far the dearest to me
446	of all men, and I do not bid him tremble at death
447	from the suitors. From a god there is no keeping clear.
> (narrator)
448	So he spoke, putting heart in her, and he himself was fitting destruction for him.
449	She went up to the shining upper rooms
450	and then wept for Odysseus, her own husband, until
451	owl-eyed Athena threw a sweet sleep on her eyelids.
452	In the evening the godlike swineherd came to Odysseus and his son.
453	They were making supper standing by,
454	having sacrificed a yearling pig. Athena,
455	standing near, striking Odysseus, son of Laertes,
456	with a staff, made him an old man again,
457	and she put wretched clothes around his skin, so the swineherd
458	would not know him looking him in the face and go tell
459	careful Penelope, and not hold it in his mind.
460	Telemachus spoke a word to him first:
> Telemachus
461	You have come, godlike Eumaeus. What is the fame through the town?
462	Are the proud suitors already inside
463	from the ambush, or are they still watching for me as I come home?
> (narrator)
464	You answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
> Eumaeus
465	I did not care to inquire these things and ask
466	as I went down through the town. My heart bid me as quickly as possible
467	when I had said the message to come back here.
468	A fast messenger from the companions met me,
